For Peter, every step of shooting analogue photos is sacred, and he treasures every negative he shoots. My name is Peter Seljan, and I’m from Budapest, Hungary. Photography has become one of my biggest passions. I started taking photos several years ago, after having LASIK surgery. I was so happy to finally have good eyesight that I created an Instagram account, bought my first camera – a DSLR – in 2019, and started with travel photography in my free time. Eventually, I switched to analogue street photography. I'm not a professional photographer, so most of the time I'm figuring things out as I go. Every roll of film and every photograph feels like an opportunity to learn something new, and that's what I enjoy most about shooting film. © Peter Seljan ,  Shot on Lomography Color Negative 35 mm ISO 400 From what we can see, you picked up your first analogue camera quite recently. I shot my first roll of film in November 2024, after buying my first analogue camera, a Canon A-1, simply to give film photography a try. I quickly became fascinated by the process, and since then, it has become a much bigger part of my life than I initially expected. In fact, I just shot my 100th roll of film during my recent trip to Helsinki. I remember using simple film cameras as a kid, but once digital cameras became the norm, I assumed film photography had become a thing of the past. Then one day, Instagram's algorithm showed me some analogue photographs while I was scrolling, and I was genuinely surprised to discover that film was still very much alive. I immediately thought, “This is something I have to try.” I did some research and found a basic and affordable analogue camera that I could buy second-hand. The moment I got my first roll developed and saw the results, I was completely hooked. Since then, I’ve been carrying a film camera with me almost everywhere I go. Looking back, I think the timing was no coincidence. Around the time I started shooting film, I was going through one of the most difficult periods of my life. I was still processing the loss of my parents while also navigating the breakdown of a deeply meaningful yet emotionally very challenging relationship. This chapter of my life was both very painful and transformative. Wandering the streets of Budapest with a film camera gradually became a way to reconnect with myself, my surroundings, and the people around me. What started as a simple experiment turned into something much more meaningful. Film photography became both a therapeutic outlet and a constant source of joy, and today I honestly can't imagine my life without it. © Peter Seljan ,  Shot on Lomography Color Negative 35 mm ISO 400 So, what were your initial impressions when shooting with your first Lomography rolls? **Born Chaotic**

A film needs to be born twice before it shines. The first birth is the exposure. When a shutter is fired, a certain amount of light, bounced from physical objects, passes through glass and tiny openings, engraving an image onto a blank film. That’s the first birth we all know about. But the second, which I would argue against common sense, is probably far more meaningful. It doesn’t happen until the film is developed, scanned, and finally presented in front of you, days, weeks, or even months later. This is what many people call the disadvantage of shooting film: you never really know what you’ve got until the film is already born as what it is. Before pressing that little button called a shutter, you can put all your imagination and effort into what you expect the photograph to become. But once it’s done, you either accept it or discard it. There is no second chance. But aside from simply accepting or discarding a film, sometimes there is another possibility, at least for me: to discover a new kind of meaning in something that has already been born. More often than not, this new interpretation is completely different from what I had in mind when I pressed the shutter. A wrong exposure, an awkward composition, or even a pointless subject can become unexpectedly moody. Some frames I shot almost carelessly carry far greater emotional weight than an entire roll of well controlled photographs. That is what I call the second birth. I have found the beauty of the second birth throughout my work over the past two years. Some of my rolls were precisely planned and executed. I wouldn’t say none of those results had ever excited me. But I gradually realized that whether a film ultimately moves me has very little to do with what it was about or how it was shot. Not just technical aspects, sometimes even the original idea doesn’t matter that much. During the second birth, every frame is judged equally by the same emotional sensor. Once the emotional sensor took over, I began wondering how I had arrived at this point. I still remember the excitement of getting my first SLR. I experimented with every piece of equipment I could afford and every technique I could imagine or learn from the Internet. Within just two or three years, I filtered out most of them, settled on a few focal lengths, and formed my own technical and aesthetic discipline. Then? It’s boring. I wanted to repeat the old experiments that once excited me, but I would stop myself: I’ve already ruled those approaches out. Why go back? … Until I found the second birth. And I realized all those disciplines in photography and storytelling only belonged to the first birth. During the second, I no longer cared about descriptiveness, one of the fundamental elements of photography. I cared about how it feels when I look at it. Digital rarely gives this privilege. A frame that doesn’t perfectly reflect the original idea is often killed on the memory card seconds after its first birth. I would look for a second shot, not a second birth. And that’s why I took my Minolta Weathermatic Dual 35, an underwater toy camera, into the operating room to document Anastasia’s first second to her first seven days in this world. My journalist instinct still told me to anticipate every critical moment, plan how the story would unfold, and predict the exposure and composition. I knew there wouldn’t be a second chance for such a documentary. But, heck, why fear an imperfect first birth, when I knew another one was still waiting? **A Study in Deep Shade – A One Shot Story**

This image was made one Saturday morning at about 8.30AM. At the time, the southern counties of England experienced three weeks of extreme heat, with temperatures rising to 30C or more during the day. I wanted make an early trip to the supermarket to buy some basic supplies and then to shelter indoors. Each day my wife and I threw our windows open between 4AM-7AM to get cooling air into the house. When the temperature inside rose by 0.1C, we knew that the house was as cool as it would be that day. We then shut the windows and drew the curtains on the southern side of the home. The cool air inside made the house bearable during the day. During this walk I decided to take my camera and practice some of the Adams/Archer zone system exposure techniques I had been studying. The heat had meant spending extended periods indoors and I wanted to be outside, even for an hour. Because of the rising heat and burning sun, I sought a route that I knew would give the most shade. I found myself at the large open area alongside the river that provides recreational space for local people. This heavily shaded area beside the railway line provided an opportunity to puzzle out the enigma that is 35mm SLR exposure – no live view, no histogram, no instant review – just a strip of flashing and glowing LEDs to guide me. I began to photograph the marked edge between sun and shade, metering back and forth between the shadow and brightly lit scorched grass. It was too hot to be in the sunlight so I worked from the deep shade of the large trees. I made a series of images in this location, trying to balance the extremes of light and dark. There were very few people about. A handful of dog walkers wandered past, but for a lot of the time I was the only person around. It brought back memories of the pandemic, the deserted streets and parks. A phrase struck me – the politics of deep shade. In a world where our lives may be governed by cycles of extreme heat, those who have access to deep shade and cool air may be able to manage. Individuals trapped in overheating urban areas, without access to deeply shaded areas like this, or where such areas are very limited in extent, may find their world unbearable. Wooded space – even a row of mature trees beside a railway line – is costed as a luxury in our commercial age. And, as a luxury, wooded space and cool shade is not available to everyone. In a world of extreme heat, these luxuries may be critical to having any semblance of quality of life, or indeed life itself. Image made on a Centon DF-300 with a wide angle lens using Fomapan 100. **UNIVERSE CONTAINED**

Space is often mistaken for emptiness. As if silence were the absence of life, rather than evidence of everything it has absorbed. But space remembers. It stretches around memory, desire, grief, instinct. It holds traces of movement long after bodies disappear from it. A woman moves through space differently. She does not simply occupy it; she alters its emotional temperature. Presence becomes something expansive, difficult to contain within fixed borders. She carries softness beside severity, restraint beside hunger, solitude beside sensuality. Never singular, never fixed, she exists as an accumulation: of past selves, imagined selves, inherited selves, hidden selves. Entire interior worlds coexisting at once. In emptiness, these layers become visible. Without distraction, space begins to echo the architecture of the self. Corners feel like memory. Light becomes almost tactile. Silence sharpens perception. What first appears vacant slowly reveals itself to be dense with emotional residue. There is a particular power in flourishing without excess. In discovering that abundance is not always material, but internal. That beauty can emerge not from decoration or fullness, but from tension, atmosphere, and the ability to remain expansive even within limitation. Perhaps this is why emptiness can feel strangely transformative. It leaves room for contradiction to surface. For fragility and confidence to exist together. For stillness to reveal movement underneath it. A woman becomes not one identity, but many unfolding simultaneously—shifting between presence and absence, exposure and protection, intimacy and distance.

**Hiroshi Sugimoto Is Playing the Long Game**

For someone who has made a career of contemplating sight, Hiroshi Sugimoto is skeptical of appearances. “All my life I have made a habit of never believing my eyes,” he once remarked. Born in Tokyo in 1948, a son in a family of merchants, Sugimoto studied economics before deciding, in 1970, to go west—not to America, but to the Soviet Union, on a backpacking trip inspired by the utopian promises of the socialist experiment. After his travels in Eastern Europe, he moved to Los Angeles, the other end of the ideological spectrum, to study photography at Art Center. His interest in the medium had long been simmering, at least since seeing the revelatory, strange magic of a photogram in an elementary school science class. After college, Sugimoto moved to New York and sold Japanese antiques in SoHo, a business that connected him to the leading Conceptual artists of the 1970s at a time when he grappled with photography’s second-tier status within the art world. The experience deepened his fascination with old objects as markers of time, surfaces of memory and projection, and sources of aesthetic intrigue. When we invited Sugimoto to guest edit an issue of the magazine, he responded that he’d like to focus on the impossible task of stopping time. Beneath his quiet poise, there’s a touch of the mad scientist about Sugimoto, aspiring chrononaut. His New York studio doubles as an impeccably organized cabinet of curiosities. Near a window, a large glass prism splits light into its constituent parts. A Tesla coil (when switched on) emits an antiquated crackling buzz. Dragonfly fossils dating from the Triassic period rest on a table. He is perhaps the only photographer who keeps a 400,000-volt Van de Graaff generator in his darkroom. When he’s not conducting high-voltage experiments, he enjoys the traditional tearoom he had meticulously constructed upstairs. The large philosophical ideas that animate Sugimoto’s work are entwined with his daily life. As an artist, he has embraced typological rigor, returning to subjects over many decades—dioramas, seascapes, studies of light, waxworks, the Surrealists—to ponder the uncanny nature of representation, the indeterminacy of space-time, and the pathos and folly of humanity’s quest for immortality despite our species being a mere blip when registered in cosmic scale. His Enoura Observatory, overlooking Japan’s Sagami Bay, may be the culmination of his life’s work, showcasing his aesthetic and philosophical obsessions while allowing visitors serene environs to contemplate their own relationships to a vast sea, sky, and unknowable universe. This all might make Sugimoto sound intensely serious, but he also projects an air of playful humor and bonhomie. My Mingei Gallery started from 1979, so it was almost at the same time that I started my career as an antique dealer and as an artist. Famighetti: Did this also drive your longstanding interest in collecting historical objects, which includes examples of photography’s earliest experiments? We’re sitting in your studio among many intriguing things, including your collection of fossils of dinosaur eggs and trilobites, which might be considered a form of proto-photography. Sugimoto: The nice thing about my collection is I didn’t have a proper art history education. So I just trusted my eye. If I go out to a flea market and find something interesting, without knowing its age, the presence of the object itself is most important. There must be something behind it, because it has a very powerful presence. I usually purchase something, and then buy hundreds of books to study it. Famighetti: In many respects, duration is at the heart of your practice, both in terms of the long exposures in the theater pictures, for example, where you photograph an entire film in one frame, but also how, over many decades, you have returned to the same subjects again and again. Sugimoto: I have worked in series. Basically, there were three series that made my life fully booked. I started the Diorama series at the American Museum of Natural History in 1975. This is the fifty-first year of that series. Technically, I’m still doing exactly the same thing. Same developer. So no progress at all . . . Famighetti: But the Diorama work has recently taken a new direction: color photographs of dioramas mounted onto traditional screens. But, aside from this change, the work has been remarkably consistent, without an obvious time signature, sort of out of time. Sugimoto: I’m usually trying to match the original 1975 quality. That’s the most difficult part. The black-and-white photo paper has changed. It used to be very good, but now they use less silver in it to cut costs. I don’t want people to think, Wow, your quality is lower than fifty years ago. So that’s very challenging! Famighetti: There is a sense of accumulation, or at least repetition, in your work as you photograph the same subjects over long expanses of time. I know that you’re interested in the photographs of Bernd and Hilla Becher and other artists working in typology. Sugimoto: The Bechers were a few years before me. Early on, they were the only photographers being shown as contemporary artists. And then I just happened to join the same gallery, Sonnabend. Famighetti: Photography is all about reproducibility, but what, specifically, interests you about typologies as a form? Sugimoto: Well, repeating subjects, such as Seascapes (1980–ongoing) especially—just one seascape doesn’t have any conceptual meaning. Water and air are the two fundamental elements of the world. That’s the subject, not the seascapes. Advertisement Advertisement Famighetti: Just the elements. You photograph the sea at Enoura Observatory, in Japan, every year on January 1? Sugimoto: New Year’s Day is the only day I can photograph in Japan without any sea traffic. Even the fishermen take that day off. I traveled around the world to compare the quality of the water and air. Now, I have stopped traveling around. I’m aging. And also the quality of the sea is changing. We are losing nature. In the 1980s and 1990s, I traveled to the most remote places, far from civilization and human habitation. And then finally, I found the seas I was searching for. Wow. Kind of the primordial sea that humans used to see. But near civilization, there’s always heavy traffic on the sea, cargo ships and fishermen. I went to Scotland, to the North Sea, but I was too late. All you see are oil towers. If one big digital bomb is dropped, then we lose electricity, and we have to go back to the old darkroom techniques. Famighetti: Your introduction to photography came through your elementary school science teacher, who taught you how to make photograms. You have an abiding interest in Man Ray, who famously made photograms, many of which formed the center of an exhibition at the Met last year. You have also revisited the mathematical models he photographed in the 1930s. What drew you toward the Surrealists? Sugimoto: I encountered these mathematical models in 2002, and I instantly recognized that these were the same ones photographed by Man Ray. So I thought, Well, maybe I can do it better? Famighetti:Better than Man Ray? You’re also building your own sleek versions of mathematical models now as well, creating otherworldly sculptural forms. Sugimoto: [Laughs] And I can even make it more accurately. Man Ray’s photographs are of plaster models made in Germany in the late nineteenth century. The French Institut Henri Poincaré purchased the set, and then Man Ray and probably Marcel Duchamp saw it together, and then they were so interested. I carry the same kind of interest. Marcel Duchamp is my mentor. Famighetti: Duchamp was famously interested in chance as an artistic mode or strategy. When I think of your work, there’s always such rigor, a sense of control, but then you’re also are interested in experimentation—there is the element of scientific exploration, or tinkering, at play. How does that balance between the chance of photography and control play out in your photographs, especially with the Lighting Fields (2007–25) images that are made by deploying live current in the darkroom? Sugimoto: Photography is a chance operation, and creating Lightning Fields is a very dangerous operation. We risk our lives. What’s the voltage? Four hundred thousand? Famighetti: Shades of Mary Shelley. I was reading that you went up from one machine to another. Is that true? You started with one that was gentler but you needed a bigger jolt? Sugimoto: Right. But it’s all motivated by my visit to William Henry Fox Talbot’s home at Lacock Abbey, where I discovered many electric devices studied by Michael Faraday and Talbot. But then, Talbot stopped his research because the invention of photography took up all his time . . . life, actually. He wanted to continue the study of electricity but this photography issue distracted him. Many people began copying him, and he had to spend a great deal of time asserting his patent rights. Famighetti: I’m interested in your fascination with Newtonian theory and the first edition of Sir Isaac Newton’s Opticks. The Opticks series (2018–ongoing) is your first work in color? Sugimoto: Yes, I wanted to re-create his experiments on the nature of color. That happened to be the last year Polaroid film was produced, and a curator organized a farewell exhibition, Bye-bye Polaroid, at the Yokohama Museum of Art. They approached me, “Well, why don’t you try it? We’ll give you the last batch of film and an SX-70 camera.” So the museum show was planned, and I was invited. I already had this prism set up in my Tokyo studio. The room was designed as a chamber to observe the quality of light and shadow, with Japanese stucco walls. I projected the colors from the prism onto the walls so that I could photograph the spectrum of colors within white light. Hiroshi Sugimoto and studio views, New York, April 2026 All photographs by Daniel Shea for Aperture Famighetti: You split your time between New York and Tokyo. How has living between two cultures for so long shaped your practice? Sugimoto: The jet lag confuses my sense of time. But I can use that. I have my best ideas when I am jet-lagged. Famighetti: Creating a context for viewing your photographs is a central part of your work as an artist—how does architecture and space figure in your overall practice? Sugimoto: I started by designing the exhibition spaces for every show. It is kind of an obsession. People started asking me to design the spaces because they saw I have a very good sense of space. That’s how it started. And then the projects were getting bigger and bigger, and I had to establish my architectural studio, New Material Research Laboratory. But I don’t have an architect’s license, so I work with a licensed architect. Famighetti: What is essential to consider when planning an environment to view photographs? Sugimoto: It’s not just the walls, I have to consider the entire space. For example, the Cartier Foundation, in Paris, is a huge glass box. So I made free-standing monolithic walls. So it’s a sculptural space with my photography. Advertisement Advertisement Famighetti: Do you have a dream space, or building, that you would want to be exhibited in? Or I should ask: Was there a museum that you felt very excited about having your work shown in because of the space itself ? Sugimoto: For an exhibition at the Royal Ontario Museum, I built a free-standing, crescent-shaped wall in the exhibition hall because there were no vertical walls. It was the opening of the new building designed by Daniel Libeskind, but my wall was delivered on the day of the exhibition opening. It wasn’t completely finished! But the crescent-shaped wall was beautiful, with a super-sharp edge that requires special care. Famighetti: Enoura Observatory, outside of Tokyo, seems to be the apotheosis of your career, an expression of so many of your interests. There is a space for theater, an optical glass stage, a tea house, gardens. It’s your total work of art. Was it a longstanding dream to create this? Sugimoto: Enoura Observatory brings together the different artistic fields I’ve been practicing into one place. It was very difficult to begin with, to purchase the land, and then the construction cost a lot of money. But I didn’t want to ask the rich people to support me or borrow money from a bank. I decided to use all the money I make from my art to support the creation of a new type of art, which is my foundation. Famighetti: Across the grounds of Enoura, there is a sense of considering time on a grand scale—from objects representing longstanding traditions to sites meant to contemplate the cosmos or the sea and nature. It’s very much a living work, changing throughout the year. Do you have a favorite season there? Sugimoto: My favorite season? In Japan we only have two seasons now, winter and summer. Maybe soon there will only be summer. But my favorite time to be at Enoura is for the winter and summer solstices. Famighetti: You have described yourself as an “habitual self-interlocutor.” What conversation is playing out in your head these days? Sugimoto: These days? Well, the exhibition in Tokyo, at the National Museum of Modern Art, is titled Extinction. The extinction of black-and-white silver-gelatin prints, but at the same time, the possibility that our civilization might be facing extinction. Everything can be done by AI, and probably the artist is the last vestige of the first people. Art is so fragile, and everything is digital. If one big digital bomb is dropped, then we lose electricity, and we have to go back to the old darkroom techniques. Famighetti: Would you be happy to go back to the “old darkroom”? In many ways you already have through your interest in photography’s origins. Why do the origins of the medium continue to fascinate you? Sugimoto: In the very beginning, the process was not fully formed. There is a beauty in the imperfection of the technique. Now, we are in the post-photography era. Perhaps I will be memorialized as the last photographer. This interview originally appeared in Aperture No. 264, “Stop Time.”

**An Archive of Us**

News - September 1, 2026 by Marina Vitaglione Somewhere in the middle of assembling The Sound of Dirt and Beauty , Jesse Glazzard found a photograph of himself at seven years old, arm outstretched, taking his own picture. He hadn’t remembered it existed. “So I guess it’s always kind of been there, which is kind of crazy,” he says, when asked when self-portraiture began. The discovery was one of many that surfaced while searching through ten years of negatives for the earliest photograph he had made of his friend Ella – the first person he began documenting, and in many ways the starting point of the book. When he finally found the images, he was undone by them. “I was just crying,” he says, “because I was reliving so many things.” The book took a year to assemble, but the lives and friendships inside it had been unfolding for a decade. There is something of that unfolding in the photographs themselves – a sense of time accumulating gently, without announcing itself. Glazzard was introduced to photography by his father, in a darkroom set up in his grandad’s pantry and on walks along a canal where he learned to notice light, composition and the shape of things. “You can take a good photo on a shit phone,” his father would tell him. It wasn’t until years later, when a tutor at Salford questioned his admiration for the British fashion photographer Tim Walker and introduced him instead to Nan Goldin and Richard Kern, that his understanding of what a photograph could be began to loosen. “I don’t know why I’d assumed photography had to be this staged thing,” he says. “When she showed me that work, it changed my perspective.” The Sound of Dirt and Beauty© Jesse Glazzard The Sound of Dirt and Beauty© Jesse Glazzard That looseness gradually became a way of working. The early studio portraits now seem almost tender in their naivety, Glazzard says – “even though it was just people coming over to have a cup of tea” – before the photographs moved out of formal setups and into bedrooms, kitchens and the unposed middle of things. The camera became less an instrument for arranging a picture than a way of staying close to a person. “I think now it feels like I’m trying to get as close as possible to the person, without feeling like the camera’s there.” That closeness is built slowly. Glazzard often meets people at parties or through friends, spending time with them before making a photograph. Some sitters take years before he feels ready to photograph them – an ease that seems less constructed than sensed. “You know there’s going to be a connection,” he says, “because they’re already not hiding – they’re willing to be seen.” The photographs carry the traces of that patience: people caught between poses, laughing, waiting, looking away; domestic spaces becoming records of the relationships that formed within them. “I’m trying to build an archive of us,” Glazzard says, a phrase that describes the book’s deepest impulse – not simply to preserve what people looked like, but to hold onto the experience of being together. The Sound of Dirt and Beauty© Jesse Glazzard The Sound of Dirt and Beauty© Jesse Glazzard The Sound of Dirt and Beauty© Jesse Glazzard His self-portraits carry a quieter urgency. Growing up searching history books for people who looked like him and finding little to recognise, he came to photography as a way of writing himself into the picture. “I wanted to hold the power over my own image,” he says. Near the end of the book, the photograph of seven-year-old Glazzard returns with a new resonance. The child holding the camera could not have known what that gesture would become – a way of looking, of staying close to people, of making a record of the lives unfolding around him. What began almost accidentally has become an archive, not of a single life but of the people who have passed through it. **Making Chris Killip’s North**

Seacoal beach, Lynemouth, Northumberland, 1983 © Thames &amp; Hudson / Chris Killip Photography Trust / Magnum Photos “To be given the keys to the archive is a privilege,” says Mark Holborn who, nearly four decades after editing Chris Killip’s seminal In Flagrante, was trusted by Killip’s son Matthew to make a new book of his work, North When Mark Holborn sat down with a finished copy of North, the book he edited from Chris Killip’s archive, he was surprised. “I got an enormous charge off the book yesterday when I looked at it,” he says. “It’s almost visceral for me.” He had seen proofs and PDFs throughout its production, but this was the first time he had turned the pages of the physical book before. “It’s like playing an album for the first time. You sit and go from beginning to end.” There is more at stake here than the completion of another photobook. Holborn first worked with Killip almost four decades ago on In Flagrante, which was published by Secker &amp; Warburg in 1988 and is now regarded as one of the defining photobooks of Britain’s post-industrial era. Killip died in 2020, so it was his son, Matthew, who gave Holborn full access to the archive – including photographs the editor had never seen, alongside those he has known for most of his adult life. “For this one, I have a sort of duty,” Holborn tells me in a video call. “Killip is not here. To be given the keys to the archive is a privilege, which I have to qualify. I want to do him justice. This is not about the projection of my own fantasy of the North. It’s about my loyalty to him.” --> Wallsend, Tyneside, 1976 © Thames &amp; Hudson / Chris Killip Photography Trust / Magnum Photos Raby Street store, Byker, Tyneside, 1975 © Thames &amp; Hudson / Chris Killip Photography Trust / Magnum Photos “The first thing you see in the photographs is that they’re remarkably elegant. Their elegance is almost in contrast to their subject matter.” Mark Holborn North, published by Thames &amp; Hudson, is neither a conventional retrospective nor a collection of Killip’s greatest hits. Comprising 224 photographs, many previously unpublished, it follows a single extended sequence. It also comes less than four years after the publisher’s Chris Killip 1946–2020, which was conceived in conjunction with an international touring exhibition and organised around the breadth of Killip’s individual projects. Holborn says that book, which provides a definitive account of the photographer’s life and work, “will serve historians well in the future”. But his own editorial principle is almost its inverse. “I was trying to find a structure that could contain a single flowing narrative,” he says, “because that is what interests me and that is the way I work.” Project boundaries dissolve in consequence. Seacoal, Skinningrove, Huddersfield and photographs made across the North East meet within the same flow, while the main body of work is framed unexpectedly by early photographs from Killip’s native Isle of Man, and later images of pilgrimages at Croagh Patrick and Máméan in Ireland – what Holborn calls “stepping stones or border crossings” into and out of the sequence. Bad editing jars, often imperceptibly, whereas good editing goes almost unnoticed. North takes us back in time to view not just what Killip saw, but what he discerned. The effect is a kind of magical sense-making. Holborn’s astute grasp of Killip’s work, together with the magic he brings to placing pictures together – pairs working in harmony, setting each other off, building a sequence to achieve a poetic coherence – allows us to become fully absorbed within the mind’s eye of the photographer. Many darlings are killed along the way. Individual images give way to the whole. And while North never betters the concise, angry dignity of In Flagrante, it uses its 300 pages to convey Killip’s slow determination to map the margins – between city and country, the sea and land, industrial past and uncertain future – and his connection to the people caught in between. The sequence also brings terrain to the fore. Killip is celebrated for the intensity of his encounters with people – children and teenagers, shipyard workers, fishing communities, families gathering sea coal from the beach at Lynemouth – but North makes clearer the continuity of the landscape around them. Streets and housing estates, beaches, shipyards and industrial structures cease to be backgrounds; people and place become part of the same historical description. Holborn deliberately avoided many of Killip’s best-known pictures. “I was chasing something else, something altogether different,” he says. “I didn’t want this book to look like something you had seen before.” Only three photographs from In Flagrante appear, towards the end. “The inclusion of those three pictures was a huge concession on my part to what had come before.” One, Killip’s celebrated image of a boy on a wall in Jarrow, becomes “a coda of sorts”. There is, as Holborn writes in North, “both continuity and departure” Whippet and Richard, Skinningrove, North Yorkshire, 1982 © Thames &amp; Hudson / Chris Killip Photography Trust / Magnum Photos Byker, Tyneside, 1976 © Thames &amp; Hudson / Chris Killip Photography Trust / Magnum Photos Such decisions place unusual emphasis on the editor, a role Holborn has occupied for more than four decades but which, when In Flagrante was made, was yet to be clearly defined. He began at Creative Camera before moving to New York in the early 1980s to work at Aperture, an experience which immersed him in a photographic culture operating on a different scale to Britain. His subsequent career has encompassed long collaborations with Don McCullin and Daido Moriyama; major projects involving the archives of Larry Burrows, Lennart Nilsson and NASA; and books with artists such as Lucian Freud. In America, he worked with figures including William Eggleston, Lee Friedlander, Richard Avedon and Irving Penn. Most significantly for the Killip story, at Aperture he helped bring Nan Goldin’s The Ballad of Sexual Dependency into publication. “Nan was raw, authentic, and so it seemed to me totally obvious to publish The Ballad of Sexual Dependency,” he says. He adds that working with Killip seemed equally obvious. The feeling was mutual. When David Godwin at Secker &amp; Warburg offered Killip the opportunity to make a book – following his 1985 exhibition with Graham Smith, Another Country at the Serpentine – Killip specifically requested Holborn as editor. He later recalled that Holborn’s experience at Aperture, and his exposure to the best contemporary photography being published internationally, offered something he felt removed from, living in Newcastle. Aperture “was a very good place to serve an apprenticeship”, Hoborn says. “I could bring that back, in a sense, which is one of the reasons I started to work with Killip. I think he was attracted to some sense of internationalism that I might have brought with me.” Holborn had already tested some of the ideas that would shape In Flagrante. In 1986 he published Killip’s Seacoal photographs in Aperture magazine, running them across the gutter and giving them maximum height. “I loved the impact of the scale,” he recalls. For In Flagrante, Killip arrived at Holborn’s London home with work prints and a preliminary dummy. They abandoned the dummy and began again, laying the pictures across the floor; Holborn subsequently constructed another dummy on his kitchen table using prints, card and tape. His description of their respective roles is disarmingly simple: “He was an artist. He made pictures. I absorbed them and arranged them.” “Arranged” understates the intervention. The vertical format was Holborn’s decision; he resisted a large horizontal book because he feared it would resemble a “refined portfolio” (as Steidl would do nearly 30 years later with In Flagrante Two), in abrupt contrast to the photographs themselves. Instead, images frequently ran across the gutter. “A portfolio of good pictures doesn’t have the right voltage,” Holborn says. “This is an angry book. It’s savage.” A contemporary Time Out review complained about the gutter running through Killip’s photographs. Holborn remains unapologetic: “Well, of course we did. That was very purposeful. It benefits from the scale, and the gutter serves it. It’s an attack; an assault on the images themselves. Without it, the book would be docile.” For Holborn, editing a photobook is closer to editing film than arranging a portfolio. A sequence should become “cumulatively emotional”, controlling the reader’s experience of time, he says. “You move fast-forward, and you pause, and you slow down, and you accelerate The looking at the book has its own time.” Four decades on, what Holborn sees in Killip’s individual pictures is a similar tension between form and content. “The first thing you see in the photographs is that they’re remarkably elegant,” he says. “Their elegance is almost in contrast to their subject matter.” Bus shelter, North Shields, Tyneside, 1975 © Thames &amp; Hudson / Chris Killip Photography Trust / Magnum Photos Nini, Clare and Rocker, coming home from school, Seacoal camp, Lynemouth, Northumberland, 1984 © Thames &amp; Hudson / Chris Killip Photography Trust / Magnum Photos Killip’s slowly measured, formally controlled photographs possess what Holborn describes in North as a “pictorial grace”, sometimes so pronounced that it almost contradicts the stark reality being depicted. Yet what strikes Holborn equally is their tenderness. “It’s a very brutal world, and within this brutal world there are remarkable scenes of tenderness,” he points out. Children are held and embraced; dogs and puppies are handled affectionately; horses on the sea-coal beach are more than beasts of burden. “There is great picture-making, but within those pictures there is enormous humanity.” That humanity complicates the familiar shorthand of Killip as a photographer of Thatcher’s Britain. The central photographs in North begin in 1975 and were made under Labour and Conservative governments alike; to reduce them to opposition to Conservative power, Holborn writes, is “far too simplistic”. What Killip was witnessing was a larger, more fundamental historical transformation. “He was photographing a world that was passing as he photographed it,” Holborn says. “It was a world in transition, and the old world was decaying and collapsing all around.” The difference is that Killip and the original audience for In Flagrante were still living inside that transition. “At the time Killip was making it, he sensed it, but it was still there,” Holborn says. “Now it’s gone.” Time has therefore altered the images as much as it has the places they describe. Holborn realised while working on North that the distance separating a young reader today from Killip’s North East is roughly equivalent to that separating the 1980s from the Second World War. Photographs that once described an unfolding present have become historical evidence, and some have moved beyond photographic history into collective memory; “When someone wants to find imagery of the north of England in the late 20th century, they will turn to Killip.” North is an attempt to defamiliarise that familiar body of work – to return to the archive, remove many of the pictures through which Killip has come to be known, and construct another route through the landscape he photographed. Holborn describes the relationship between photographer and editor as a process of “osmosis”, in which each gradually absorbs the sensibility of the other. With Killip no longer present to approve or reject his choices, that relationship now depends on something more elusive. “I’ve made a living with my eyes,” Holborn says. “How to look is how I’ve made my living for the last 40 or 50 years. I have to trust that.” Alan and a dead deer, Seacoal camp, Lynemouth, Northumberland, 1984 © Thames &amp; Hudson / Chris Killip Photography Trust / Magnum Photos North East England, 1976 © Thames &amp; Hudson / Chris Killip Photography Trust / Magnum Photos Chris Killip: North is edited by Mark Holborn and published by Thames &amp; Hudson, priced £75 www.thamesandhudson.com The post Making Chris Killip's North appeared first on 1854 Photography.
