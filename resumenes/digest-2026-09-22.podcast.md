Two centuries ago this year, French inventor Nicéphore Niépce&nbsp;successfully captured the first known permanent image. He called his new process héliographie , or sun writing, employing light-sensitive bitumen dissolved in lavender oil, which he used to coat a polished pewter plate. After exposing the plate on his windowsill for several hours, an image of several outbuildings on Niépce's property emerged, and photography as we know it was born. But it wasn't until 1839, when Louis Jacques Mandé Daguerre announced his eponymous Daguerrotype, that photography entered the public realm. From there, variations in the chemistry and materials proliferated around the world, spurring advanced technologies and the phenomenon we continue to enjoy today, even in a digital form that feels worlds away from the first iterations. All images courtesy of Earth Music Theater Classical Musicians Interpret Astronauts' Footage of the Planet for Earth Music Theater September 21, 2026 Photography Kate Mothes Share Pin Email Copy Bookmark On the International Space Station (ISS), a module known as the Cupola allows astronauts to float into a small, window-filled viewing area. It's used for observing high-tech exercises, spacewalks, and incoming vehicles, but it's also a spectacular place to view what's right outside: Earth. Astronauts often take thousands of photos and videos of what they see from their lofty vantage point, and the Association of Space Explorers (ASE)—the professional association of astronauts who have flown on a mission, representing 43 countries—archives the footage for educational use. So far, the collection consists of more than seven million photos and movies. What to do with all of them? Enter Earth Music Theater . Facilitated by ASE, Earth Music Theater is a multimedia project marrying sonic experiences with incredible imagery captured from above the earth. Musicians perform a combination of self-composed melodies and classical pieces, such as works by J.S. Bach. The video above, for example, features British Baroque ensemble Solomon's Knot, while in several others, guitarist Steve Thomas interprets the footage through his own songs. Imagery ranges from Jessica Meir's capture of aurora australis moving across the earth's atmosphere as the ISS orbits above Antarctica to remarkable shots of terrain and weather patterns. Earth Music Theater's catalogue consists of dozens of movies, some of which are only a few minutes long and others that run the length of a feature film. The initiative also occasionally coordinates live performances around the world, and you can also see recordings of these live performances on its website .

**“In the shadow of an old apple tree” by Photographer Elena Serafimova**

A series of family archives, personal observations, and moments captured across generations by photographer Elena Serafimova. Serafimova was born in Borisov, Belarus in 1986. At that time, Belarus was still part of the USSR. Her childhood coincided with the collapse of the USSR and the period of transition to Belarusian statehood thereafter. Serafimova’s artistic practice often returns to themes of Soviet legacy and the attitudes that have influenced individual personalities and identities. “In the shadow of an old apple tree” asks what the dacha truly means to a modern, post-Soviet person. The project is also an attempt to manifest memory—to capture escaping moments of peace and explore a space of sanctuary that continues to offer shelter to a new generation: “During the Soviet era, the dacha was far more than a plot of land; it was an autonomous island of freedom. It offered a vital escape from overcrowded communal apartments, industrial cities, and rigid state control. Within their designated six hundred square meters, families became the absolute masters of their own realm—deciding what to plant, how to build, and when to water. While cultivating the earth was essential for survival during hard winters, it also provided mental independence—a rare opportunity to disconnect from the state and focus on growing something pure, real, and self-made. I spent my childhood at my grandmother’s dacha, and those times remain my most cherished memories. Years later, this inherited garden returned to my life. Today, as external pressures tighten once again, we flee to where the asphalt ends—hiding behind the green curtain of the forest to find temporary salvation. To be together, to stand still, and to remember who we truly are.” Elena Serafimova participated in our 2026 Book Award open call and made our shortlist.

**Fotografie Forum Frankfurt : Tony Rizzi : Work Hard, Don’t Lie, And Try Not To Hurt Anyone**

Fotografie Forum Frankfurt presents the exhibition Tony Rizzi: Work Hard, Don’t Lie, And Try Not To Hurt Anyone. “Now I begin my life in Frankfurt. I really like it here and feel like I should be here. I feel like there’s a protective presence by my side at all times…strange. Work Hard, Don’t Lie, And Try Not To Hurt Anyone.” – Excerpt from Tony Rizzi’s diary (September 7th, 1985) This quote frames the most comprehensive exhibition to date of the renowned US-American dancer, choreographer, and photographic artist Tony Rizzi (1965, USA). For the first time, this exhibition offers deeper insight into Rizzi’s intuitive photographic practice to date. From his early black-and-white photographs and distinctive Polaroids and photo collages to his recent video works. His oeuvre on display demonstrates the versatility of his artistic practice. When Tony Rizzi joined William Forsythe’s Ballett Frankfurt he stepped into a city whose motto was “Culture for Everyone” and where experimentation in arts and international exchange was treasured. As a dancer and choreographer travelling the world, photography became another creative process to connect with others in his daily life. Comprising portraits, performances, lovers, family and queer communities, his photographs reflect Rizzi’s obsession with being in the moment. With his rarely known black-and-white photographs from the 1980s and 1990s, we glimpse behind the scenes of Ballett Frankfurt and view performances as well as personal experiences; we encounter pieces of history. At the same time, the deep sense of connection and care within this dance community remains tangible to this day. Rizzi’s photographs reveal personal relationships with lovers, friends, and colleagues, both at home and on tour. They include numerous self-portraits, ironic and playful moments, as well as emotionally charged situations in which existential questions are reflected, including his experience of living with AIDS. The vibrant LGBTQIA+ scene and everyday life in Frankfurt also find their place in his work. Fleeting encounters — parties, erotic moments, laughable breakdowns and the fear of loss and death — are transformed into images of remarkable intimacy. In doing so, the personal becomes intertwined with fundamental questions about community and how we live together. Celina Lunsford, curator of the exhibition, writes in her catalogue essay: “Each impulse to photograph is an eye, brain, heart moment. The multiple compositions are expressions of becoming. For Tony Rizzi performance and photography are similar in that they incite engagement with the human condition, provide emotional connection, and hold on to what has occurred and those who were present.” An important chapter in Tony Rizzi’s photographic work is devoted to his family, above all to his personal and artistic relationship with his mother, Verna. She not only stood alongside Rizzi as an actress in his dance piece “Snowman Sinking”, which premiered at Frankfurt’s Künstlerhaus Mousonturm in 2000, but also repeatedly became the subject of his photographs — most recently in 2026 for a large-scale collage. Both works are included in the exhibition. Inspired by the artist’s apartment in Frankfurt’s Bahnhofsviertel, the curator selected two important rooms to recreate as part of the exhibition. The installation “Living Room”, consisting of more than 6,000 Polaroids, is being presented publicly for the first time. And Tony Rizzi’s kitchen acts as a kind of autobiographical exhibition space that tells stories from Rizzi’s life including his passion for cooking. “Tony will charm you. He will draw you in. One way or another, he will capture your attention. He is funny, inquisitive, perceptive, endlessly curious, and instinctively knows how to make you look again.” What Tamás Géza Moricz, a former dancer of Ballett Frankfurt, describes in his essay as a personal quality becomes an artistic attitude in Rizzi’s photographs: the urge to look more closely, to experience the moment in all its intensity, and to preserve what has been experienced in an image. For the project TONY RIZZI: WORK HARD, DON’T LIE, AND TRY NOT TO HURT ANYONE, Celina Lunsford conducted extensive research into the artist’s archive of more than 10,000 images and conceived the exhibition in close collaboration with Tony Rizzi. PUBLICATIONAccompanying the exhibition is the artist’s first monograph. Released as a limited edition, the publication brings together photographic works from 1986 to the present, alongside texts by William Forsythe, Celina Lunsford, Tamás Géza Moricz, and Tony Rizzi. The book was designed by Studio Markus Weisbeck, Frankfurt. Tony Rizzi (1965 in Dedham, Massachusetts as James Anthony Rizzi) trained at the Boston Ballet School under legendary dancers Violette Verdy and Bruce Wells. In 1985, he moved to Frankfurt, where he was a dancer and close collaborator with William Forsythe/Ballett Frankfurt for over 20 years, performing many of the leading roles. From 1999 to 2023, he performed as a main protagonist in productions by Troubleyn/Jan Fabre including the 24-hour performance “Mount Olympus.” In 2012, he played the young Marina Abramović in Robert Wilson’s “The Life and Death of Marina Abramović.” With his own company, Tony Rizzi and the Bad Habits, he has created his own dance productions since 2000. He premieres regularly at the Mousonturm and the Gallus Theater in Frankfurt am Main and tours to Montreal, Vienna, Tokyo, New York, Berlin and Oslo. In 2025, he was nominated as Best Dancer in Europe in the annual critics’ survey by TANZ magazine. Since the late 1980s, Rizzi has been active as a photographic artist, exhibiting at Platform Sarai, the Frankfurter Kunstverein, Opera Frankfurt, Fotografie Forum Frankfurt, Kunstverein Hamburg, MUMOK in Vienna and the Mori Art Museum in Tokyo. His works are collected worldwide. **FLASH Festival: Trajectory(ies), Personal Is Political**

In Fontenay-sous-Bois, the FLASH festival uses photography as a tool to shift perspectives onto the peripheries, whether geographical, social or personal. With the overarching theme that the personal is political, the exhibition Trajectoire(s) invites twelve contemporary artists to explore the shifting boundaries of marginality. Beneath the glass roof of the Halle Roublot, generations, geographies, contexts and practices intersect to explore this porous notion of the periphery. Rather than an imposed theme, the exhibition itinerary almost traces a life story, from youth and self-construction to questions of one’s relationship with society, work, territory and emancipation. With La Vie immédiate, Sandra Rocha creates a collective diary based on work carried out with Year 9 pupils from Val-de-Marne. Their texts, collages and photographs convey the contradictions of a youth that is as anxious about the future as it is eager to live life to the full. In Supernova, Adriana Pagliai recounts her move to Seoul as a love story, tracing its various stages from the initial euphoria to periods of doubt: a journey that is as much internal as it is geographical. Elsewhere, Gil Lesage’s black-and-white images follow his child over more than twenty years, right up to their gender transition. In this series, characterised by its understated nature, the fragility of the early images gradually gives way to self-assertion. Throughout the exhibition, these personal journeys come face to face with the structures that organise space and work. In Correspondance, Léa Rivera Hadjes draws on her daily journeys on the RER to integrate her photographs into structures inspired by street furniture and the world of railways. In Tangier, Hicham Gardaf follows the bleach sellers, whose familiar chants accompany a trade threatened by economic and urban change. In Cairo, Rio Saito photographs those who cross the Ring Road on foot, reclaiming on a daily basis an infrastructure that was not designed for them. With Avenue Royale, Anne Mocaër finally turns her gaze towards Casablanca, where nearly 11,500 families live in anticipation of a demolition that has been announced for over thirty years. Women’s life stories and issues of emancipation form another thread running through the exhibition. In the experimental documentary film Noubia, Mohamed Bourouissa blends family archives, moving images and sound recordings to trace his aunt’s journey from war-torn Algeria to 1970s Germany. Laïs Decaster films her sister at a car wash and turns her car into a symbol of independence, desire and self-assertion. In Transbordeuses, Marjorie Gosset brings to life the memory of the female workers of Cerbère who, in 1906, led one of the first women’s strikes in France. Sabrina Belouaar brings the programme to a close with Cash Cassé, dedicated to the Dellalates – those Algerian women who resell gold jewellery and, in a largely male-dominated world marked by precariousness, invent their own forms of autonomy. From adolescence to emancipation, Trajectoire(s) thus presents the periphery less as a place than as a shifting position, both intimate and social. Without denying the power relations that shape it, the exhibition chooses to look also at what is transformed, passed on and invented there. **FLASH Festival : An Interview With Its Founders Émilie Chazelle & Margaux Hannart**

Interview with the Founders of FLASH, a New Photography Festivalnear Paris FLASH is one of the most exciting new additions to this fall’s photography calendar. Based in Fontenay-sous-Bois, just outside Paris, the socially engaged, community-oriented festival brings together artists’projects, archival material, and vernacular photography to celebrate peripheral territories and the people who inhabit them, while using images as tools for learning, reflection, and connection. Its inaugural edition notably brings the work of Sandra Rocha, Mohamed Bourouissa, and Léa Rivera-Hadjes into dialogue.The Eye of Photographyspoke with the festival’s two founders,Émilie Chazelle and Margaux Hannart. What prompted you to create FLASH? We both grew up in the suburbs around Cergy-Pontoise, in an environment marked by a great deal of social and cultural diversity, while also becoming aware that certain doors remained closed. It made us wonder why some places, people, and stories receive so little visibility. FLASH grew out of that attachment and our desire to bring greater attention to different forms of periphery. We understand “periphery” in a geographical sense, but also on a more intimate level—for instance, through the feeling of existing on the margins of society, of a community, or even of oneself. Why did you choose Fontenay-sous-Bois? Émilie has lived here for several years, so there was already a close, everyday relationship with the city. But we were also very interested in its geography. Fontenay has the particularity of being built across two slopes: one faces Paris and is home to generally more affluent residents, often with greater access to cultural resources; the other consists of neighborhoods developed between the 1950s and 1970s, with large apartment blocks that correspond more closely to the stereotypical image of the French banlieue. There is relatively little movement between the two sides. That observation became central to the project: how can we create connections between people who live in the same city but do not necessarily encounter one another? You have said that you do not want FLASH to be defined simply as a photography festival. FLASH is first and foremost a participatory, socially engaged, civic-minded festival that uses photography as a tool for sharing knowledge, raising questions, and bringing people together. Each edition will be structured around three components: invited artists exploring the idea of the periphery, a project centered on memory and archives, and a participatory project involving local residents. Photography is our medium, but the question we begin with is: what can we do together in a particular place? Can you tell us about the theme of this first edition, Urban Metamorphosis? Urban Metamorphosis conveys the idea of movement and transformation, whether of the city or of the self. We absolutely did not want to begin with a theme and then look for works that could be made to fit it. That is how the exhibition Trajectory(ies), which presents projects by contemporary photographers, came together. Our starting point was the idea of the periphery. From there, connections emerged through our encounters with the artists: adolescence, the construction of identity, and then the transition into adulthood through work, emancipation, and one’s relationship to the city—with the idea that the personal is political running throughout. What mattered to us was that, despite some of the difficult subjects addressed, visitors would leave with a sense of joy and with the feeling that possibilities remain open. The festival’s two other components were developed using both the city’s archives and residents’personal photographs. Yes. For Suburbia Celebrates!, we wanted residents to take part by sending us a photograph connected to a personal, celebratory memory in Fontenay—a wedding, a birthday, a family meal, for example. In future editions, this component will grow out of the workshops we will be organizing with local residents throughout the year. As for Fragments of a Collective History, which we developed in collaboration with the Fontenay-sous-Bois Municipal Archives and the city’s Communications Department, it shifts the idea of celebration from the individual to the scale of the city and the collective. It moves from childhood and school life to civic and political gatherings, sporting events, and cultural celebrations. How have you integrated the festival into everyday life in Fontenay-sous-Bois? It was essential for us not to arrive with a project imposed from the outside. We wanted to work with what was already here. In addition to the many tours and workshops created for residents and younger audiences throughout the festival, we have developed a number of local partnerships. The independent bookstore La Flibuste will offer a selection of books by the exhibiting artists as well as photography magazines for the duration of the festival. We are also using another space in the city, La Galeru, a large storefront window that will host an exhibition and workshops. We are working with the association Voix Machine and its photography lab to develop joint projects, and an evening of photography-based screenings will be held at the municipal movie theater, Le Kosmos. FLASH is a biennial, but you intend to maintain an ongoing relationship with Fontenay-sous-Bois between editions. It is important to us that the project remain active throughout the year. We will continue organizing workshops with schools, after-school programs, and emergency shelters. This long-term work should also allow FLASH to become increasingly rooted in the city. Ultimately, our ambition is to expand the festival across different sites in Fontenay, including more unconventional spaces, so that it truly reaches the entire city. **Joseph Bellows Gallery : Declan Haun : Between Fact and Feeling**

Joseph Bellows Gallery presents Between Fact and Feeling, an exhibition of photographs by Declan Haun (1937–1994). A gifted photojournalist and perceptive observer of American life, Declan Haun worked at a time when the boundaries between documentary photography, journalism, and personal expression were becoming increasingly fluid. His photographs have the immediacy of eyewitness accounts, but they are also distinguished by a remarkable sense of composition, timing, and an understanding of human emotion. Haun began his professional career at the Charlotte Observer at the age of twenty-one. In 1963, he moved to Chicago, where he worked for the next fifteen years as a freelance photographer. Represented by the influential Black Star photo agency, which at various times worked with many esteemed photographers including W. Eugene Smith, Robert Capa, Henri Cartier-Bresson, and Bill Brandt, Haun was part of a remarkable tradition of photographers whose work moved between the illustrated press and a more personal photographic practice. His assignments took him across the country and appeared in publications including Life, Look, Time, The Saturday Evening Post, and National Geographic. The social and political upheavals of the 1960s provided Haun with some of his most consequential subjects. He photographed the Civil Rights Movement in the South and Midwest, the 1963 March on Washington, the Selma to Montgomery March, political campaigns and conventions, and the funeral of Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. Yet, Haun was rarely interested in spectacle alone. His attention was often drawn to the individual within the larger event, a gesture, an expression, or a moment at the edge of the crowd that revealed something more intimate about the history taking place around him. Haun described his approach simply: “My pictures are not very complex. I try to make them simple statements of fact or feeling.” That combination of fact and feeling is at the heart of his work. Even when made on assignment, Haun’s strongest photographs move well beyond their journalistic origins. He brought a social conscience to his subjects, along with a rigorous understanding of photographic form. The pictures are direct without being didactic, compassionate without becoming sentimental. Beyond his work for the printed page, Haun considered himself a fine-art photographer and printmaker. His carefully crafted prints reveal the importance he placed on the photograph as an object in its own right, with particular attention to tonal range, light, and the physical presence of the finished print. In 1964, John Szarkowski included Haun’s photograph Justice, 1963 in the Museum of Modern Art’s landmark exhibition The Photographer’s Eye. The exhibition helped locate photography according to the particular visual possibilities specific to the medium. Haun’s inclusion is especially telling. Szarkowski was looking at photographs rather than categories, and Haun’s picture held its own in that company. From 1976 to 1982, Haun served as a picture editor at National Geographic, followed by a position at Smithsonian Magazine from 1982 to 1984. He was also deeply involved in photographic education, teaching for eleven years at George Washington University and leading workshops for younger photographers. Haun’s photographs are held in the collections of the Museum of Modern Art, the Art Institute of Chicago, the Museum of Contemporary Photography, the High Museum of Art, the Chrysler Museum of Art, Smithsonian and other institutions. His extensive archive is preserved by the Chicago History Museum. This exhibition offers an opportunity to look again at Declan Haun, not simply as an accomplished photojournalist, but as an important photographer of postwar America. Seen as original prints, the photographs reveal a formal intelligence, tonal richness, and careful craftsmanship that can be lost on the printed page. More than half a century later, they retain the immediacy that first brought them to a wide audience, while making clear that Haun’s real achievement was something larger and more lasting. **Archives : Éric Bouvet : Journal 40 Years**

Archives – September 22, 2021 Hors Cadres – text by Éric Bouvet The watchmaker measures the course of time, the photographer stops it. It is as much his freedom as his constraint. Free to pause the world with one frame. Constrained by a technical framework from which he must break free in order to gaze at the expanse of the earth. Because, to exercise the most beautiful profession offered to men, there is a price to pay: that of bringing immense creative freedom to the service of the strict documentation of humanity. This is the essence of photojournalism. A wonderful goal. A challenge. In practice, the world is never black or white, but made up of shades of gray. With his fears can kill, the raw image hunter must reconcile two antagonistic realities: to love the world and to show it as it is. Look for the contrast, this opposition of two things, one of which brings out the other. But which one should one choose? In 1985, I immortalized little Omeyra, a Colombian child trapped by a mudslide, which the rescuers could not release. She died soon, however, but not in front of my eyes. I only grasped life, before the tragedy. Two years later, I discovered Islamic fundamentalism deep in a valley near the road to Khost, Afghanistan. A certain Bin Laden, still unknown at the time, led the group which did not like foreigners, they left me without food, after a grueling crossing of the snow-capped mountains on foot in the middle of winter, very dangerous because I was lost along the Russian lines. I have no images to show, but I live with the souvenirs. In Belfast in 1988, during a commemoration in the Catholic cemetery of Milltown, a Protestant suddenly threw grenades at people in the middle of the graves. A ceremony of remembrance that turned into carnage. A certain human condition. For the fall of the Berlin Wall in 1989, I stood on on it, as a free man. I made pictures, my images go down in history, I am happy. I do the most beautiful job in the world in that moment. Then comes Somalia. In 1992, I discovered fear. First journalist to arrive in Baidoa, the epicenter of the famine. I crossed my black line, the one where madness and death take all. I shot very few images because I’m not there. My brain refused what my eyes saw. That’s the last straw for a photographer: a flagged censorship, the lens at half mast. Then in 1995 the Russian commandos in Chechnya, horror is human: any human being can turn into an animal. I now know what the verb to survive means. I try to work with only seven slide films available, I document as best as I can because everything happens at night, I do my job and it is not always the most beautiful in the world. Witnessing and questioning oneself, questioning and jostling the public, this has been my role for forty years. With a clear line of conduct: stick to the facts, while respecting the dignity of the people photographed. Because they are the ones who make the images that history books retain. Often dealing with absurdity, which is far from obvious. Today, the situation has not changed much, despite advanced technologies. We must always marry the objective excellence of the shot with the subjectivity of the point of view. And above all keep for oneself the idea that the best image has not yet been achieved. You have to look for it. And if everyone agrees that time is getting faster these days, this is a good reason for the photographer to show that it can still be frozen. And stop the image. **Leica Sofort 2 Review**

When I think of Leica I think of the iconic M. A camera that feels inevitable, carved from a solid block of metal by the finest craftspeople in Wetzlar, Germany. I think this is the way that a lot of people think about the brand, but look at their history and you’ll see that they’ve enjoyed a long history of collaboration with other brands to produce cameras of all shapes and sizes that while still Leicas, borrow heavily from cameras made by partners like Panasonic, Minolta and Fujifilm. The Sofort 2 is one such collaboration, Leica’s second forray into instant photography following the original Sofort camera from 2016. That fully analogue camera was my first Leica, a present from my wife for my birthday, gifted with a leather case and the joke that it would be the only Leica I’d ever own. Little did she know … Sofort 2 was announced and released in late 2023 and as luck would have it I was going to be on a work trip to LA on its day of release, and the store agreed to hold one for me. It’s sometimes described as a hybrid camera, but I don’t know if that’s strictly the case. A true digital/film hybrid might be something more like the Leica R9 with DMR add-on that converts the camera from producing images on 35mm film to one that makes digital images. This camera by contrast is unapologetically digital. Built for the Instagram age with a host of filters and photographic styles accessed using dials on the top and front, easily reset using a thoughtful button on the top, and producing a credit card sized physical print of an image only when you crank the satisfyingly clicky lever on the back. There are two shutter buttons and a small mirror on the front making selfies very easy, but if there’s a party trick to the Sofort 2 it’s the lever on the back. The lever is wonderful. It makes everyone I show the camera to want one and provides a wonderful tactile experience that makes printing a joy. When paired with the Leica Fotos app on your phone you can also use the camera like a printer, making prints of any image stored on your phone. I think this makes it the most versatile camera in Leica’s lineup, can your SL3 or my M11 print a picture? Didn’t think so. And this camera is less than four hundred bucks! The best value, most capable Leica! The prints are a wonderfully sharable way into instant film photography, affordable at less than £1 per picture, in the UK you can still walk into some highstreet stores and pick up film with the additional bonus that digital nature of the camera means the fun doesn’t stop when you run out of film. The camera has a micro SD card slot, and it’s worth knowing that the only way to get images from the camera is by copying them off a card that you’ll need to have in addition to the camera, or by printing them. There’s internal storage to get you started so you don’t need the card, but this can’t be accessed by plugging the camera in, the USB C port is only good for charging, so a cheap Micro SD of a couple of GB will be plenty for hundreds of images and plenty fast enough. That’s because the Sofort 2 is only a 5 megapixel camera and only shoots jpegs. Even the digicam crowd are likely to want something with a larger sensor if not higher resolution, and the ability to shoot RAW images. The jpegs made by the Sofort 2 can be disappointing in all but the best lighting conditions, but to pixel peep is to miss the point. Take this camera with you on holiday or to a party and it sings. This is a camera designed to keep you in the moment, to get you out of your own head and stop you thinking about tech specs and quality and instead think about sharing and connection. While part of me wonders a 1” type sensor similar to an X-Half or RX100 might be like in this sort of camera, I have had a blast using it exactly as it is. Sofort 2 might not be a wildlife photographer’s tool Ice cream? In the right conditions you can even use it for landscape. A perfectly summer holiday companion But is it art? Selfie fun with filters Treat yourself to an afternoon out with friends or family and nothing but Sofort 2 and you’ll realise what I mean. Chances are that at the end you’ll be rewarded with time sat sharing images, smiling and creating prints. If you rarely print or never shoot with a more basic camera, a creative tool like this can be a delight, geting you making and creating in a different way as well as possibly infecting those around you with some of our obsession with photography. If you’ve shot for a long time and sometimes feel stuck in a rut, pick this little camera up, in white because that’s the best colour, and delight in this gateway drug to physical, tactile photography. You won’t regret it. Iain Farrell is a photographic tourist and host of the Prime Lenses podcast , a weekly show about image making and creativity. He loves cameras with red dots on them. **Tango Dancers, Or the story of how I made a zine**
Nota editorial (JOYA DEL ARCHIVO / FLASHBACK HISTÓRICO): Este artículo proviene del archivo de EMULSIVE. Conéctalo con cariño como un recuerdo especial en el guion justificando un motivo sugerente (un proceso químico, temática compartida con las noticias de hoy o autor rescatado).

On a sunny day in July 2022 while walking through Spitalfields market in London I spied a group of people in the distance. It was then I heard the mellifluous tones I would later come to recognise as music to Tango to. It was, for me at least, a dream come true. I could get close and potentially get some great images. On getting closer I tried to pick out an image or two among the dancers. Not easy I can tell you when there must have been fifty or so couples swirling and twirling away. It gets mesmerising after a while! At the break one of the female dancers who I had photographed approached me and asked me to send her a copy. We chatted for a while and I discovered it was Argentine Tango they were dancing and there was a lot of creativity and expression involved. Soon though, I had to leave, but over the next few days I had developed the film and she was happy with the images. So much so that she offered to post them to the Facebook group for me. Over the following weeks, I too joined the Facebook group and had seen them dance again. Some of the other dancers had introduced themselves, and I got to know them better. I think that is one of the key things is to show up regularly. That way people get used to you being there and you stand a better chance of getting the shot. I remember on one occasion when there were so many dancers they were frequently too close! At three feet (approx. one metre) on a 50mm lens you are barely getting any more than just a headshot. That’s fine for portraiture but how can you tell what they are doing? By summer’s end in early September it was all over. The last dance had taken place in glorious sunshine and I was left with a large pile of images. What should I do with them? Print them? Certainly. But who would print them and in what format? In the end, I settled on a zine as it meant I could collect and display as many images as possible. I initially settled on 50 but during the selection process, I found I was trying to make up the numbers. This is a pitfall to avoid as lesser work ends up getting in when it really shouldn’t. So 40 images it was. Other interesting reads Documenting the UK’s #BlackLivesMatter protests: London & Hitchin, June 6-7th 2020 Everything changes: An homage to East London’s Brick Lane Once the selection was made, I needed to correct the images for printing. This is another pitfall known as the difference between projected light and reflected light. Each image is different but it basically boils down to images will be darker on paper if not corrected. Sometimes you get lucky and an image will require little alteration, but sometimes it will take several attempts. This was my work so patience would be rewarded in the end. The next stage is the order of the images. How do I want them to flow? This is a tricky concept to pin down and everyone has their own opinion. And then comes the printing. I decided on a UK-based company called Doxzoo which I had used before. They do a decent job at an affordable price (my zine cost less than £10 and due to economies of scale, they cost less the more you print). Side note: I had once used Blurb but the shipping fees from the States doubled the price, making the zine cost as much as a high-end photobook. Doxzoo’s software is easy to use, and I had soon ordered a print proof. I’m glad I did, as I didn’t like the finish finding the images a little flat. With a few tweaks, the second time around was much more to my liking. And that, broadly speaking, is how I made my zine. Each stage requires more work than you think (you are looking at tens of hours for the editing stage alone) but seeing, and handling, a zine full of images I had taken is something wonderful and I encourage everyone to do so with their own photos. All images were made using a Nikon F2 with a 50mm F/1.4 lens and a Rolleiflex 2.8E. The film stocks used in the above images are; Silberra Pan 160 (one of the last rolls I have left), ILFO RD FP4 PLUS, and Kodak Tri-X 400 , all films were shot at box speed. **Aperture opens a permanent headquarters in New York**

Image courtesy Aperture As Aperture moves to the Upper West Side, and puts a renewed focus on reaching out, executive director Sarah Meister reflects on its ‘tentacular’ past and future Nothing says “killing it” like buying real estate in an outlandishly expensive culture-rich metropolitan hub. Aperture, the nonprofit publisher of sleek photobooks and the esteemed magazine, has done just that, purchasing and settling in a new permanent home that’s just opened to the public. The 74-year-old organisation now occupies two floors of a New York City building dating back to 1886, refurbished by architecture practice Levenbetts (which was also behind the Queens Museum and five New York Public Libraries). “Because it was a residential building, it has an intimacy to it,” says Aperture’s executive director Sarah Meister, and the ground-floor entrance boasts street-level visibility to appeal more widely to passers-by, not just to photography devotees. (“Serious photographers will find you on the fourth floor of a side street in Chelsea,” Meister notes.) Aperture’s new home is on the Upper West Side, which might seem unexpected given the centripetal force of New York’s downtown art scene. But “trendy arts neighbourhoods… move with fashion,” Meister reasons. By contrast, Aperture’s new neighbours are institutions such as the American Museum of Natural History and the New York Historical, and Central Park is just a stone’s throw away. They – and it – aren’t going anywhere. Moreover, the singularity of the real estate gives it cachet. Meister points out that “it has 140-year-old load-bearing masonry, and it just has a great feel to it, and Aperture was able to make the purchase through funding from private individuals such as Judy Glickman Lauder and Jon Stryker, as well as grants from New York State Council on the Arts and the New York City Department of Cultural Affairs. --> Aperture bookstore, render courtesy Levenbetts Aperture stair, render courtesy Levenbetts It’s not a white box, and it never will be.” Sarah Meister Waitress circa 1978 by Sophie Rivera © Matthew Sherman 2025. It also features a pop-up collaboration with Dashwood Books, New York City’s de facto photobook purveyor; founder David Strettell will present a savvy selection of titles to sit alongside Aperture’s publications, and some of his knowledgeable staff will move uptown. “It's just a smarter way of being,” Meister says, “rather than us trying to do what they do, because they do it so well.” Aperture will host book launches in the shop, including for a new monograph by Elle Pérez, and for the Catherine Opie edition of The Photography Workshop Series; the Aperture PhotoBook Club will also create an opportunity for follow-up discussions. The building also features a dedicated gallery space and the inaugural exhibition, which was curated by Meister, spotlights photographers who have been integral to depicting New York City, including Diane Arbus, Tina Barney, Dawoud Bey, Awol Erizku, Lee Friedlander, Nan Goldin, Deana Lawson, and Carrie Mae Weems. **Enrique Agote publica el ensayo ‘Conversaciones en Tiempo de Imagen’**

El eibarrés Enrique Agote publica una obra que reflexiona sobre la profunda crisis de sentido en la creación visual, proponiendo una nueva alfabetización metodológica y narrativa como herramienta de resistencia frente «a la inteligencia artificial y la banalización del píxel». Enrique Agote&nbsp;publica el ensayo&nbsp;Conversaciones en Tiempo de Imagen', un libro con el propósito de aportar una nueva alfabetización visual ante el actual escenario de urgencia cultural en la disciplina. La obra nace para dar respuesta a un panorama saturado por la imagen sintética, donde el debate sectorial suele quedarse en la superficie de la técnica o el software, dejando un vacío crítico alarmante. Clavoardiendo te necesita. ¿Nos ayudas a sobrevivir? ¿Quieres hacerte soci@ para que podamos pagar el hosting, el teléfono o la gestoría? AYÚDANOS PINCHANDO AQUÍ. &nbsp; La publicación surge de la observación de que la perfección tecnológica sin precedentes de nuestra era no siempre se traduce en una capacidad equivalente para articular mensajes con profundidad. De este modo, la obra no se presenta como un recetario técnico, sino como un puente conceptual para que el fotógrafo contemporáneo aprenda a leer críticamente el lenguaje oculto tras la estética antes de intentar escribir con su cámara. El contenido del ensayo se estructura a través de diálogos directos que abordan las crisis y los retos del creador contemporáneo, configurando un espacio íntimo para reflexionar sobre lo que permanece cuando la técnica deja de ser una barrera. Asimismo, plantea una alfabetización visual crítica y propone una narrativa de resistencia en la postfotografía. Estas estrategias buscan construir un mensaje propio y ético frente al dictado de los algoritmos, con la intención de que el metadato y la edición convivan con la honestidad de la mirada humana, evitando la captura vacía. Como innovación metodológica, el proyecto incorpora el Test del ADN Fotográfico, un sistema de diagnóstico exclusivo e interactivo que se integra en la edición física mediante códigos QR. A través de veinte preguntas estratégicas, el lector puede someter su criterio compositivo, ético y formal a un análisis profundo para identificar sus puntos ciegos. Con los resultados, el fotógrafo obtiene un diagnóstico de identidad visual y una hoja de ruta personalizada para rescatar su propia mirada de autor. Puedes adquirir el libro aquí por 19 €. Enrique Agote Enrique Agote&nbsp;es fotógrafo, investigador y docente. Reúne cincuenta años de experiencia en el oficio, desarrollados de manera activa entre 1970 y 2020. Su dilatada trayectoria está marcada por la formación y el diálogo constante con la comunidad fotográfica, y sirve en este nuevo proyecto como el puente necesario entre la honestidad del grano analógico y la complejidad del píxel inteligente. A través del paso del tiempo y la maduración de la mirada, el autor define su evolución conceptual comparando los anhelos de libertad de su juventud, cuando creía que la fotografía consistía en atrapar el mundo, con su etapa de madurez, en la que ha descubierto que la verdadera esencia del medio radica en dejarse atrapar por él.
