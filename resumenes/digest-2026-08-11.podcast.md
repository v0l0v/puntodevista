**Joel Byron on Filming Reggae duo The Tribulizations in Brighton with the Joseph Petzval 55 f/1.7 Art Lens**

UK-based director, photographer and videographer Joel Byron set out to capture a gig in his home town of Brighton by Reggae duo, The Tribulizations. He took along the Joseph Petzval 55 f/1.7 Focus-coupled Bokeh Control Art Lens, and let the looseness and personality of the lens capture the mood of the event. Joel talked to us about his experiences working with this unique bokeh lens and how letting go of your creative control can lead to some unexpected results. Video 1 Welcome to the Magazine, Joel! Could you give us a quick introduction? I’m a filmmaker and photographer based in Brighton, working through my studio BigPlus. Most of what I make sits somewhere between documentary and something more constructed. I’m not that interested in clean coverage. I’m more drawn to how something actually feels when you’re inside it. I move between digital and analogue depending on the project. Film has always been a constant within my work, particularly Super 8 mm. I’m drawn to the texture, unpredictability and physicality of the format, and often collaborate with musicians and artists where those qualities become part of the final piece itself. I’ve been working closely with Reggae duo The Tribulizations, building a body of work around their night Moon-Hop at Komedia. It’s a good space for me. There’s rhythm, repetition, and a certain looseness that suits the way I like to shoot. Recently, I’ve been collaborating closely with them, documenting their new live night; Moon-Hop at Brighton’s Komedia and creating a Super 8 mm music video for their latest single, WON’T STOP! Stills from The Tribulizations Live at Moon-Hop by Joel Byron ,  Shot with the Joseph Petzval 55 f/1.7 Focus-coupled Bokeh Control Art Lens Tell us about your time with the lens. Where did you take it and what did you end up shooting? I took it straight to one of their nights and shot across the full run of the event. It doesn’t start until late, so most of it was captured between 11 pm and early morning. I kept the approach simple – moving between the stage, the edges of the room, and the crowd. “Flare creeps in, edges soften, contrast shifts depending on how the light hits the lens. It feels less like a tool for recording and more like something that interprets what’s in front of it.” At one point, I was shooting past the DJ booth as the lights cut from red into a cold white burst, and everything briefly flattened before dropping back into color again. Those shifts became the structure. It felt like the right environment because nothing holds still. You’re reacting all the time. Stills from The Tribulizations Live at Moon-Hop by Joel Byron ,  Shot with the Joseph Petzval 55 f/1.7 Focus-coupled Bokeh Control Art Lens What were your first impressions? How would you describe its personality? It has a presence to it. You’re aware of it in your hand, and that changes how you shoot. It’s not interested in being neutral. It pushes the image in certain directions. Flare creeps in, edges soften, contrast shifts depending on how the light hits the lens. It feels less like a tool for recording and more like something that interprets what’s in front of it. You have to accept that fairly quickly or you end up fighting it. Stills from The Tribulizations Live at Moon-Hop by Joel Byron ,  Shot with the Joseph Petzval 55 f/1.7 Focus-coupled Bokeh Control Art Lens Were there any particular moments – certain lighting setups or subjects – where the lens really came alive for you? When the stage lights caught the front element at an angle, especially through smoke, it started to break the image apart in a really interesting way. I love the opening shot with the Komedia sign and also some of the MC, as he is framed with interesting light throws. There was a moment where the MC stepped forward into a wash of magenta and green, and instead of resolving cleanly, the light wrapped and bled across the frame. His outline held, but everything else shifted. That felt like the lens doing its best work. It responds to light hitting it imperfectly. Stills from The Tribulizations Live at Moon-Hop by Joel Byron ,  Shot with the Joseph Petzval 55 f/1.7 Focus-coupled Bokeh Control Art Lens How did the bokeh character influence the mood or style of your shots? It pulled the background away from being descriptive and turned it into movement and color. I wasn’t trying to isolate subjects cleanly. I wanted things to drift. People moving through the frame, lights breaking up behind them, details appearing and then dissolving again. The bokeh helped with that. It gave the images a kind of rhythm rather than a fixed point. It felt closer to how the night actually reads when you’re there. Stills from The Tribulizations Live at Moon-Hop by Joel Byron ,  Shot with the Joseph Petzval 55 f/1.7 Focus-coupled Bokeh Control Art Lens Any tips you’d share with someone curious to try this lens? Don’t over-correct it. If you try to make everything sharp and controlled, you’ll lose what makes it interesting. Let the flare happen. Let things fall slightly out. Also, put it somewhere with contrast and movement. Mixed lighting, smoke, people crossing in and out of frame. It needs something to react to. If you treat it like a standard lens, you’ll probably miss what makes it interesting. I’m currently working on a year long orchard project, documenting the cycle from blossom through to harvest using a mix of 16 mm, Super 8 mm and digital. I’d love to return to this lens in daylight and see how it responds in a completely different environment. **When in Rome: The Corners of the Eternal City with Ugo Leonetti and Johannes Beilharz**
Fotógrafos: johbeil, corinthian, angelinatacinelli

What's it like to be a film shutterbug living in some of the most well-known, iconic cities in the world? Our new series explores these cities through the eyes of local Lomographers, who share emerging, classic, and underrated art, music, food and historical spots, for anyone looking to immerse themselves in the soul of the sprawling metropolis. We now have over 100 (and counting...) Travel Guides on our website, full of photography and travel inspiration tips around the globe. This time, we thought that we would let Lomographers themselves give us all the pieces we needed to create a Travel Guide for the romantically-ancient city of Rome. [](https://www.lomography.com/homes/johbeil/photos/29542604) [](https://www.lomography.com/homes/corinthian/photos/25009907) [](https://www.lomography.com/homes/johbeil/photos/29542742) [](https://www.lomography.com/homes/corinthian/photos/25009906) Credits: johbeil&corinthian Benvenuti a tutti to Rome, a city with a country inside its borders (yes, you read that right), the home of the first-ever shopping mall and a place where stray cats call the Colosseum home. A place full of history, where Gladiator sweat was once used as skincare and going to the toilets was a social activity. Oh, and don't forget about the nearly three-millennia-old Roman ruins you can discover, all with a film camera. Let's dive into this city where you can walk the steps of warriors and enjoy a creamy carbonara on the same day, with the help of Lomographers Ugo Leonetti (@corinthian) and Johannes Beilharz (@johbeil). We reached out to them after finding their LomoHomes in our global Community. We sent over a list of questions and asked for their recommendations for the city they call home. [](https://www.lomography.com/homes/corinthian/photos/24789647) [](https://www.lomography.com/homes/johbeil/photos/25806864) Credits: corinthian&johbeil Hi, Ugo and Johannes, welcome to Lomography Magazine. Where do you currently reside, and how did you start taking film photography? Ugo: I live in Quadraro, a working-class neighborhood in the southeast of the capital. I’ve been shooting on film since 2017, when my father gave me his Nikon FM after I had gone quite a while without taking photographs. It was also a period in my life when I needed to slow down and rethink things. Film photography became a kind of zen experience for me: it taught me once again the pleasure of looking at the world through a pentaprism and, above all, the value of cultivating patience and practice. Johannes: I currently reside in Rome, Italy. My first encounter with film photography dates back to the early 1970s, when this was the only type of photography available. I still have photos and negatives from trips and other occasions from those days. With the advent of digital photography, I stopped taking photos on film for several years until 2015, when I took my father’s old Leicas out of their boxes and started using them again, thinking they were too good to remain unused. Since then, I’ve added many more film cameras, including medium format and pocket cameras. Credits: corinthian – Tangenziale Est, Via Prenestina What is it like becoming a film photographer in your city? Would you say there’s a strong film culture? Ugo: Rome has a vibrant cultural spirit, shaped by the many influences and communities that come together there from across Italy and around the world. It is also, I would say, one of the most photographed cities in the world, so it's not uncommon to see someone carrying a small film camera around their neck, especially in the more “hipster” neighborhoods. For instance, Rome is home to the main headquarters of Ars Imago, a shop specializing in analogue photography, and it's an easy place to meet fellow enthusiasts and strike up a conversation. There are also many venues that offer darkroom facilities, as well as courses in film developing and printing. I, for example, learned how to develop film in the basement of a Sapienza university social center that made its darkroom and equipment available to anyone interested in learning and practicing the craft. Johannes: There is a film culture, as witnessed by some shows I’ve been to in different parts of the city. The lab in Torrino, where I have my films developed, does a substantial amount of analogue photo work. There is another thriving photo shop in Montagnola which sells and repairs mainly analogue cameras and offers film development. [](https://www.lomography.com/homes/johbeil/photos/29542601) [](https://www.lomography.com/homes/johbeil/photos/29542635) Credits: johbeil What’s your gear like? Are there any cameras or film stocks that stand out to you, especially in relation to photographing your city? Ugo: The main thing is to travel light. I like to carry just one SLR and, at most, a point-and-shoot. Usually that means the aforementioned Nikon FM, a Pentax ME Super, or a Nikon FE, which has recently joined my collection thanks to an uncle who gifted it to me ;) along with an Olympus XA2. In the past, if I was heading out to take photos, it wasn't unusual for me to bring two 35 mm camera bodies, a medium format camera, and a compact camera as well. Over time, though, I've realized that I prefer traveling light and focusing more on photography itself rather than on the gear. As for film stocks, I think I've gone through miles of Fuji 200, Kodak ColorPlus, and Cinestill 800. I'm also very fond of motion-picture film stocks and films that produce distinctive effects, such as Lomography's LomoChrome Metropolis and LomoChrome Purple. Credits: corinthian – Piazza di Porta and Rione Esquilino Johannes: My main cameras are Leicas from the 1940s, 1950s, 1970s and 1980s and a variety of Olympus models dating from the 1960s to the 1980s. In addition to that, I have various Lomography cameras, e.g. Diana F+, Diana Mini, Sprocket Rocket, Fisheye, and cameras from other brands, e.g. Rollei, Seagull, Adox, Lubitel, Pentax, Voigtländer and Agfa. Apart from mainstream films from Kodak, Fuji, Foma, Agfa and Ilford, I love experimental and unusual films like Adox Color Implosion, LomoChrome Purple and LomoChrome Turquoise, Lomography Redscale XR, Harman Red, Harman Azure. In the past, I also used slide films, mostly for cross-processing, which have unfortunately largely disappeared from the market or have become ridiculously expensive. I occasionally try my luck with expired films. [](https://www.lomography.com/homes/johbeil/photos/29542746) [](https://www.lomography.com/homes/johbeil/photos/29542699) Credits: johbeil Where do you usually hang out? Could you share favorite spots which might be considered underrated? Ugo: I love Pigneto for the hectic and colorful lifestyle that somehow managed to remain authentic, same for Piazza Vittorio, that is considered the multicultural “Chinatown” of Rome. Johannes: My most common photography hunting ground is my (expanded) vicinity, which means Roma Laurentino, EUR, Ostiense, Testaccio, Garbatella, Montagnola, San Paolo, all in the south of Rome. Of course, I also venture to the center and other parts of the city occasionally, e.g. Trastevere, the Jewish Quarter, the area around the Colosseum, Piazza Navona, to name just a few. And I love to explore other towns and places in Lazio, Abruzzo, Umbria, Molise, Puglia and their landscapes. [](https://www.lomography.com/homes/johbeil/photos/29542599) [](https://www.lomography.com/homes/johbeil/photos/29542600) Credits: johbeil What is your favorite photo walk route in your city? Ugo: When I'm out of ideas, the route between San Lorenzo, Esquilino, Piazza Vittorio is a go-to and never disappoints you if you are willing to take some street photos. Johannes: Most often I walk the “literary” streets of my vicinity – they are all named after writers, e.g. Marinetti, Silone, Pavese, Kafka, García Lorca, Melville, Baudelaire, Joyce, Woolf. Earlier this year, I published a book with some black and white photos taken in these streets. Contrary to the names, these streets are partially far from grand but run-down and therefore not very picturesque, but lend themselves to documentary or street photography. The crumbling brutalist architecture is also of some social significance. Credits: corinthian – Devotional Wall, Largo Preneste Have you taken any photos of your city that you consider your favorite? What’s the story behind it? Ugo: Any pictures of forgotten and distant suburbs. I've managed to find something interesting and hypnotic in the ordinary, and somehow commonly considered ugly places I would never imagined to find something poetic in it. Johannes: Some of my favorites are actually photos of more or less mundane motifs in the neighborhood or other parts. I’ve walked the same streets for years now but still occasionally manage to discover interesting details to photograph that I hadn’t noticed before. In some cases, expired films, camera or operator misbehavior have produced surprisingly interesting results. **Surveying community and resistance in LGBTQ+ nightlife**

Sex, Clubs Dissent — A new book collates queer nightlife photography over the past 50 years, and the people who found refuge and safe spaces after dark. For over a century, queer nightlife thrived in the margins of society, driven underground by the criminalisation of sexuality and genders beyond the binary. Here in bars, clubs, ballrooms, warehouses, bathhouses, dungeons, cruising grounds, and strolls where sex is sold, generations have come together as an act of love and resistance against a world that shunned them. In those clandestine spaces, queer culture took root, becoming the lingua franca of music, style, beauty, glamour, and attitude – to say nothing of political activism and mutual aid that has saved lives and changed laws. Progress, however, can be a double-edged sword with the price of conditional freedom being cultural appropriation by a mainstream all too willing to erase the source. Ballroom colloquialisms like ‘the tea’, ‘kiki’, ‘shade’, ‘slay’, ‘reading’, ‘gagging’, and ‘giving’ are often mislabelled as ‘gen Z slang’ while contemporary and historic Black, brown and LGBTQ+ creators are paid dust. “There’s an acceleration of ubiquitous hyper capitalism and absorption of queer culture,” says author Amelia Abraham, on the phone from the streets of London. She’s just had a pop into The Photographers’ Gallery Bookshop, where her latest book, Sex, Clubs, Dissent: Visualising Queer Nightlife (MACK), is set among the new arrivals. The book’s red cover beckons with a keyhole glimpse of unbridled amour, revealed to be Meryl Meisler’s photograph of two women on the floor of Les Mouches, a members-only queer nightclub on Manhattan’s west side in the late 1970s. It’s a delectable promise of things to come. Read next: Inside Parkuir: the art project queering sport, space and our bodies In Sex, Clubs, Dissent, Abraham explores the intersections of community, creativity, desire, and liberation, spotlighting the originators and innovators who blazed their own paths over half a century. Bringing together the work of artists including David Wojnarowicz, Sunil Gupta, Mariette Pathy Allen, Bruce LaBruce, Reynaldo Rivera, Linda Simpson, Lyle Ashton Harris, Clifford Prince King, and Chloe Sherman, the book is a panoramic landscape of the power and pride that’s only found when the night comes alive. Although Abraham started clubbing as a teen, it wasn’t until she discovered The Joiners Arms at the age of 21 that she finally had a place to call her own. “It was really chaotic. The floor was sticky with beer, the toilets were flooded, and it felt lawless and exciting to me,” she remembers fondly of the fabled Shoreditch dive bar. “It was loads of people from different subcultures coming together, a real melting pot, like the 60-year-old trans matriarch Stephanie, who always sat at the bar. It was one of the first places where I really fell in love with queer nightlife.” Drawn to the intergenerational aspect of Joiners, Abraham struck up a friendship with photographer Jeffrey Hinton, whose portrait of dancer Michael Clark, appears in the book. “Jeff is in his 60s and I’ve had the pleasure of hearing his stories about the nightlife that came before,” she says. “He has the same kind of attitude that Joiners had, which is quite DIY, egalitarian, and messy. That’s what we’re talking about with the word ‘dissent’ – a kind of disorderliness. Fuck it! Just do it – and do it for the love of it!” Read next: An intimate window into New York’s ’70s lesbian scene It is this ethos that Abraham brings to the book, crafting a tour de force that is as much a photographic history as it is a family album, particularly in light of the criminalisation of queer sexuality around the globe. “The Gay Rights Movement started with visibility. Before that, the camera was dangerous,” she says. “Photographers from that time talk about how they couldn’t show their photos because someone might lose their job or their family. And that’s true for now for certain people.”Sex, Clubs, Dissent: Visualising Queer Nightlife (2026) by Amelia Abraham published by MACK.
