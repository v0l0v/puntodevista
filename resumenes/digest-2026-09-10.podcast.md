**Baths of Caracalla, Rome : Frank Horvat**

Las Termas de Caracalla en Roma acogen la exposición “Frank Horvat”: un conjunto de 72 imágenes divididas en tres momentos significativos que exploran la obra del gran fotógrafo. La exposición está promovida por la Superintendencia Especial de Roma, dirigida por Daniela Porro, y organizada por Electa. Cuenta con la curaduría de Fiammetta Horvat y Mathilde Oudin (Studio Frank Horvat) y de Nunzio Giustozzi, y podrá visitarse del 11 de septiembre de 2026 al 10 de enero de 2027. Frank Horvat (1928-2020) fue un fotógrafo ecléctico que, a lo largo de una trayectoria de más de setenta años, abordó con gran intensidad una amplia variedad de géneros: desde luego la moda, que lo consagró, pero también el reportaje social y de viajes, el paisaje, los retratos y su relación con el arte; una búsqueda constante marcada por la libertad y una delicada manera de mirar. «Horvat era una personalidad cosmopolita», señala Daniela Porro, superintendente especial de Roma, «un italiano nacido en Abbazia —actual Opatija, en Croacia— que vivió en Lugano, Milán, Londres y París, y que viajó por todo el mundo refinando su mirada y su sentido del tiempo. Sus imágenes en las Termas de Caracalla rinden homenaje a un intelectual que recorrió todo el universo de la fotografía: desde la moda y el reportaje hasta la investigación artística, la exploración del tratamiento digital de la imagen y los viajes a lugares lejanos o cercanos, como Sicilia, a la que se dedica una sección de la muestra». «Hemos elegido exponer tres series afines a Horvat», explica su hija Fiammetta Horvat, quien gestiona el archivo junto con Mathilde Oudin, antigua asistente del fotógrafo, «que ilustran su curiosidad a la vez que encajan perfectamente en este sitio milenario, creado para celebrar el cuerpo humano y la belleza». En uno de los gimnasios de las Termas de Caracalla, un original recorrido reúne algunos de sus mejores retratos dedicados al universo femenino, el tema que, por encima de cualquier otro, revela la clave para comprender la obra del fotógrafo. Con un enfoque personal, Horvat logró subvertir los códigos de la fotografía de moda, liberando a las modelos de las poses convencionales. Las retrató en la calle con una Leica y luz natural, tejiendo a menudo con ellas relaciones de amistad y complicidad. Desde finales de la década de 1950, la cambiante idea de la elegancia, el auge del prêt-à-porter —cada vez más accesible para las mujeres de clase media— y la evolución del estatus social femenino avanzaron de la mano: ya no eran solo actrices o bailarinas, escritoras o intelectuales, sino también madres tiernas y mujeres trabajadoras, por fin independientes, que exigían mayor libertad y el derecho a ser ellas mismas. Por lo tanto, lo que le interesaba era la «mujer real», como también demuestran los reportajes documentales que realizó en numerosos países de todo el mundo, desde Pakistán e India hasta Brasil, Senegal, Egipto y Japón. Un apartado específico está dedicado a los encargos fotográficos que Horvat realizó en Roma a principios de los años sesenta para Harper’s Bazaar, tras la invitación de Irene Brin y Gaspero del Corso, de la Galleria L’Obelisco. En ellos, célebres modelos de la época aparecen junto a figuras de la cultura y el cine como Alberto Moravia, Luigi Barzini y Marcello Mastroianni. La figura femenina también ocupa el centro de la segunda sección de la exposición, con fotografías de la serie Vere sembianze, presentadas en los espacios abovedados del monumento. Aquí Horvat entabla un diálogo competitivo con la pintura, estableciendo una estética dinámica con los grandes retratistas del pasado mientras revela las verdades íntimas de las mujeres modernas. La sección final, Goethe en Sicilia, dialoga de manera extraordinaria con los monumentales restos de las termas. A principios de los años ochenta, Horvat se midió con las memorables páginas del Viaje a Italia de Goethe, descubriendo una profunda afinidad con la visión romántica del escritor alemán. Fascinado por la cultura griega de los templos, por las tradiciones rococós y por las costumbres populares, Horvat centró su atención en la inconfundible naturaleza y en los paisajes de la isla, lugares marcados por una historia atemporal. **Windows, Tripods and Strobes: Lighting Indoor Portraits on Film**

An overriding goal in my photography this past year has been to expand my ability to use lighting to create expressive indoor portraits, whether in a studio or on the fly. Practically speaking, this has meant translating what I learned in Ethan Hill ‘s 2025 lighting course at the International Center for Photography—a class aimed primarily at digital shooters—to the far less intuitive world of film cameras. Bangaloreans, 2026. An example of a fairly high-key, Avedon-style studio portrait, large softbox at camera left. Rolleiflex 2.8F, Kodak Tri-X 400. Suman, 2026. A more intimate portrait using the same lighting scheme. A slight turn away from the keylight deepens shadows. Rolleiflex 2.8F, Kodak Tri-X 400. Documentary Filmmakers Tom Casciato & Kathryn Hughes, 2026. This portrait was made in a hallway with window light coming from camera left—handheld, f/4 at 1/30th of a second. Rolleiflex 2.8F, Kodak Tri-X 400. Cheese!, Upstate NY, 2026. Photographed through an opening from the outside, metered for her freckles… (!) Rolleiflex 2.8F, Kodak Tri-X 400. My Daughter in the Studio, 2026. A moodier, low-key setup. Softbox is up and left of camera axis. Deardorff 5×7, Sironar-N 240mm f/5.6. Ilford HP5+. Ash, 2026. Direct window light with the highlights just barely under control. Rolleiflex 2.8F, Kodak Tri-X 400. First, I must acknowledge that many excellent photographers, including quite a few 35mmc stalwarts (such as Alexandre Kreisman) never use strobe, indoors or out, and are natural light purists in the manner championed by Henri Cartier-Bresson. In addition to pooh-poohing color photography, Cartier-Bresson famously disdained artificial lighting, which he felt flattened photos and drained them of soul. This does not, however, mean that he passively accepted whatever lighting presented itself when making portraits. His provocative 1971 photo of the disgraced poet Ezra Pound is a case in point (click to see the full-resolution version at the Fondation Henri Cartier-Bresson or at New York’s MoMA ). This portrait, which Cartier-Bresson’s wife, photographer Martine Franck, describes as arising from an almost brutal standoff between her husband and his controversial subject , is remarkable also for the way that Cartier-Bresson chooses to use the light entering from a window outside the frame on camera left. Favoring the midtones with greater exposure—a choice a different photographer might reasonably have made—would likely have brightened the image, giving a more conventionally journalistic sense of the surroundings and perhaps rendering Pound’s face and eyes more legible. But doing so would have deprived the light of its starring role. In the portrait that light becomes a subject in its own right, an elemental flash in a darkness that seems to engulf, or perhaps emanate from, Pound. (Interestingly, the MoMA print from the 1980s does have slightly more open midtones than the 1971 frame in the Paris exhibition catalogue and website, for reasons unknown to me. This difference emphasizes the role of often anonymous printers and editors in shaping iconic images.) I seriously doubt that an artist of Cartier-Bresson’s caliber needed to worry much about how he was using light in such scenes. What had long been automatic for him is something I will always have to work at, muttering aloud to myself in the process. In addition to questions of metering—do you favor the highlights, shadows, or midtones?—there is the Inverse Square Law , which has to do with the physical proximity of the subject to the window or other light source. Closer generally gives more intense brightness, as one might imagine, but also more abrupt falloff; further away means more even illumination and gentler transitions. This photo appears to have been taken with flash, but was actually made with the baby close to a window in a darkened room. The inverse square law explains the abrupt transition from his illuminated face to the dark background. Rolleiflex 2.8F, Kodak Tri-X 400. My first two portraits of Evan (the featured image and the next one) show what a major difference a slight shift in placement vis-à-vis the same south-facing light source can make in a portrait session. Although Evan leans his body loosely toward the window in the featured image, he is distant enough from it and angled toward the camera in such a way as to allow the light to softly envelop him. While there is some falloff between the light and dark sides of his face, the major shadows occur in the lower quarter of the photo, near a dark wood floor where the window light scarcely penetrates. Evan II, 2026. Rolleiflex 2.8F, Kodak Tri-X 400. In the second frame, on the contrary, the light appears harder. It’s the same light—I took the photo minutes after the first—but it feels more intense owing to the fact that Evan now stands inches from the glass. I embraced the falloff by metering for the highlights and by printing the portrait at a relatively high contrast grade, selectively burning the shadows to deepen them further. The resulting image has a markedly bolder, more graphic feel. The most radical departure in my Evan session is this final image, taken in the same studio but this time using a diffused strobe located camera right, overriding the window light. As with the featured image, I made this photo with a Deardorff 5×7, but this time positioned the camera on the tripod above Evan and used front tilt to create a soft, glowing vibe. (I did not want the high-key sharpness of an Avedon-style portrait in this instance). I hoped to further that dreamy feeling by feathering the light from the edge of the softbox diagonally in such a way as to allow it to graze Evan’s shoulders and face, creating gentle modeling and leaving no shadow on the wall behind. Evan III, 2026. Deardorff 5×7, Rodenstock Sironar-N 240mm f/5.6. Ilford HP5+. Which Evan photo works best? With the Ezra Pound portrait, you can easily see how Cartier-Bresson’s somber lighting serves a deeper artistic purpose, given the American poet’s treasonous collaboration with the Axis powers during World War II. With Evan, a Leica enthusiast (he uses an M2 with a gorgeous 1960s Summilux 50), there is no public story to speak of—no biographical data by which to evaluate the photos or the way the light works in them. So the choice of favorite image is, as with most photographs, ultimately subjective. Rounding out this brief discussion of lighting in indoor photography, I’ll present a portrait of Molly, another young photographer who, like Evan, graciously agreed to model for me. While I made some more traditional studio frames of her against a black backdrop, the picture that speaks most to me from our session is the following, taken with Molly reclining in an adjacent room. Molly, Radiator Portrait, 2026. Rolleiflex 2.8F, Kodak Tri-X 400. My goal with this photo was to have it feel both natural and somewhat contrived, a found moment in a private life that is also a bit of a performance. I could have taken the photo on a high-speed film with the Rolleiflex on a tripod and gotten some of the look I was after using available light, but this would’ve been an iffy proposition given the room’s dimness and the inconvenient location of the two windows (above the radiator behind Molly). As an alternative I positioned my strobe high in a doorframe above me, directing the light with an umbrella and keeping the unit between six and eight feet from my subject. I wanted the light to spill over the room, creating the feel of a high north-facing window without having to rearrange furniture or chisel out brickwork. At least one photographer friend of mine wondered if the photo had been made with window light, which is gratifying… though I’m not convinced that Cartier-Bresson would be on board with that assessment. And, dear reader: what about you…? Thanks as always for having a look. Roy in Assisted Living, Pennsylvania, 2026. Window-light portrait. ### C41 Magazine

**Walter Pfeiffer: ‘I Don’t Believe In Nostalgia’**

If you don’t know Walter Pfeiffer by name, you will almost certainly recognise his images. Something about their high-contrast, terribly colourful, tightly framed lexicon probably feels utterly familiar… If you agree, but cannot quite put your finger on the reason why, a clue lies in an essay by the co-curator of Pfeiffer’s latest retrospective titled In Good Company, published in the accompanying photobook by the same name. In it, Nicola Trezzi describes Pfeiffer as a ‘prophet of image-making,’ and outlines how the artist anticipated many of the visual codes that define social media-era fashion imagery today. Pfeiffer, then, feels remarkably familiar because we constantly encounter images like his on our Instagram feeds, where—for better or worse—culture has become increasingly cyclical and self-referential. A good part of contemporary photography seems to exist in a suspended state, somewhere outside of “the contemporary, if such a term still retains any meaning. The persistent relevance of Pfeiffer's work raises an obvious question: why do these images continue to be synonymous with our ultra-digital present, when they were made by an artist now in his eighties? Perhaps the answer lies in a paradox. While Pfeiffer’s photographic language has been so readily adopted in the digital sphere, its origins are resolutely grounded in the physical. ‘I like paradoxes,’ Pfeiffer wrote by email, ‘they make life more exciting!’ The artist began by photographing friends, and friends of friends who dropped by his apartment in Zurich during the 1970s and 1980s. ‘I treated the people I photographed as if they were stars,’ Pfeiffer tells me over email, ‘They were beautiful and they appeared even more beautiful in my pictures. I felt I must chase and capture beauty.’ The emphasis on connection follows Pfeiffer throughout his career and forms a central pillar of both his recent retrospective and publication. The title of each, In Good Company, speaks to the artist's collaborative process, his reliance on friends as subjects, and the cherished objects and things that populate his world. Personal connection and tactile reality emanates from these images. At times, one feels almost able to hear a loud billowing laugh while looking at them; they are that evocative. By the time the digital turn came to be, however, Pfeiffer was already in his sixties. After years of toiling in relative obscurity—certainly as far as outside of Europe is concerned—he received some of his earliest fashion editorial commissions from Dutch magazine publishers Gert Jonkers and Jop van Bennekom in the early 2000s, who would later launch Fantastic Man in 2005 and The Gentlewoman in 2010 and continue to commission Pfeiffer. From there, the number of commissions only continued to grow, along with the prestige of the publications seeking his work; French Vogue, Dazed, Self Service, and i-D all embraced Pfeiffer’s irresistible blend of humour, vibrancy, and seduction, recognising that his work had something utterly contemporary about it, despite the fact that it wasn’t quite as contemporary as one might think. It’s a visual style the artist had developed—and stayed true to—for the preceding thirty-odd years. Meanwhile, other photographers and countless publications appear to reference Pfeiffer continuously online. This points towards another—and perhaps more intriguing—reason for his enduring influence. Not only do his images evoke the tactile realities for which we may long, they also fit neatly within the algorithmic framework of the attention economy. To borrow photo editor Emily Keegin’s viral breakdown of the subject on the podcast The Cutting Room Floor, fashion photography today largely favours cropped subjects captured in frames that are ‘just about clothes and space.’ These visually clean and quickly captivating images perform better on feeds. They do not require prolonged attention, like a detailed landscape photograph or narrative scene might. Effective simplicity reigns. It certainly does in Pfeiffer’s images, which rarely feature more than two people, and which hardly ever are zoomed out enough for spectators to see both a subject’s face and the lower half of their body simultaneously. The digital life of Pfeiffer’s connective images also play into our collective sense that our world seems to have lost something. The nostalgic or “backwards-looking” language of contemporary photography in the post-digital era is well documented; as today, hoards of historians of photography attempt to grapple with the reasons why photographers and spectators alike continue to turn to previous forms of the medium, and to value the analogue over the digital. Of nostalgia, Pfeiffer writes, ‘I do not believe in nostalgia, and even today, with all that is happening, I want to keep in contact with the real world. I love to connect. Technology is just a tool, we use it the way we want.’ Perhaps, however, it is precisely because many very powerful people are not using new technology in a way some of us want, that we look to nostalgic forms of image making for clues as to how to access previous ways of living, relating and existing. In this way, nostalgia in our culture is rather positive, as the past can be viewed as an opening. Trezzi believes a slippery relationship with time and the contemporary is another prophetic aspect of the artist’s work; ‘he made sure that dates would never rule over the image, leaving us to wonder whether an image was taken in the 1970s or the 2000s. In a time of suspended temporality (in the same way the term contemporary art has been dragged around for decades now), this becomes a seminal condition,’ writes Trezzi. Pfeiffer’s retrospective also unfolds thematically, as opposed to chronologically. Pfeiffer clarified to me over email that ‘[Nicolas] writes that although I know all the dates, he believes I do not write them because I wanted to keep things open to let people wonder and imagine.’ No matter the reason, commissioning fashion houses and magazines don’t seem to care. The artist is perhaps more solicited than ever before. ‘As I get older, I feel I have less time,’ Pfeiffer tells me. ‘Therefore I work all the time. I want to achieve as much as possible, considering that time is limited, especially for me.’

**The Contax 645: Not just a wedding camera**
Nota editorial (JOYA DEL ARCHIVO / FLASHBACK HISTÓRICO): Este artículo proviene del archivo de EMULSIVE. Conéctalo con cariño como un recuerdo especial en el guion justificando un motivo sugerente (un proceso químico, temática compartida con las noticias de hoy o autor rescatado).

Six months ago I changed cameras. Deciding that I needed a new medium format camera was for me, both a difficult and easy choice. There are no prizes for guessing what I eventually went for, it is after all in the title of this post, but first with the appropriate level of respect, here is a bit of background as to why, after many years I gave up my beloved Hasselblad camera. I had been shooting with a square format Hasselblad 500 c/m since 2012 and I believed it would be my lifelong photographic companion. So, imagine my distress when a couple of years ago, I started feeling a sort of shooting frustration when using it. This wasn’t related to image quality or reliability; it was something else entirely. Contax 645 – Cover Over the years as my eyesight deteriorated, I found myself needing to wear glasses. No big deal, but it kind of was when shooting. Using the waist-level finder, initially, I composed my shots with my glasses on. However, when I lifted the camera and adjusted focus through the magnifying loupe, my glasses had to come off. Then, I had to put them back on to recompose after lowering the camera. Taking a step or two back or forward, and the whole laborious dance was repeated. I felt that I was spending more time fiddling with my glasses than taking photos. What was once a minor annoyance gradually transformed into a genuine frustration. I attempted a solution by using an eye-level prism finder, which seemed to resolve one problem but highlighted another: ergonomics. With the viewfinder attached to the Hasselblad, it felt like I was holding a brick against my face and an ugly one too. All of this was only amplified each time I used my 35mm cameras; they showed me that being able to shoot comfortably at eye level through a viewfinder was what I wanted, even when shooting medium format. So, after fighting these feelings I felt I had finally reached the end of the road with the 500c/m; it was time to consider another camera. I’ve always hankered after a CONTAX 645 there had only been one thing stopping me in the past, format. I had become used to shooting squares and going to 4:3 concerned me. When a square composition works it really works, add that classic feel you get from square format and 6×6 photos can be absolutely glorious. On the flip side, square compositions can be compositionally limiting and sometimes feel unnatural and so the idea of shooting vertically and horizontally in medium format was just about attractive enough at this point in my photography for it not to stand in the way of my decision. There were other reasons that I felt this was the right camera for me, quite apart from a proper viewfinder and ergonomic design it also has interchangeable backs and lots of other modern features like autofocus. But really what made me want this camera is the Carl Zeiss Planer ƒ2 80mm lens that is attached to it, moreover this lens’s ability to make beautiful portraits wide open that was so attractive and exciting. I took it for granted that it would work stopped down for all my other work! That might sound a little glib, but look on the web and nearly everything you see from this camera is wide open breezy pastel wedding photography and wedding portraiture. You would be forgiven for thinking that’s all they are good for. For sure, they must be good, because each day hundreds of pro photographers worldwide put their trust in these 20+ year-old cameras to function and produce professional results for their clients. All this must be a testament to the camera’s qualities and despite its disputed reputation for being delicate the prices of these cameras have since the 2010s always been elevated. Ownership is certainly a commitment and risk; with its 20-year-old electronics, it can be expensive and difficult to repair. So, when I got mine, I took my friend, and ironically given the sub title of this blog post, wedding photographer, Viola Tavazzani’s advice, and found one locally in the best condition possible, like her, resisting the temptation of importing one from Japan. Contax 645 – Front The CONTAX 645’s reputation among professional wedding photographers is undeniable, but it was not just designed to do that job. Back in 1999, what now could retrospectively be considered the beginning of the end of mass professional film photography use, Contax made what was arguably the world’s most advanced medium format film camera system. Together with its range of excellent Carl Zeiss autofocus lenses, interchangeable vacuum film backs, and extensive accessories, it became a popular camera for many professional photographers. The CONTAX 645 was always a high-end professional system, so, when Kyocera pulled the plug on the CONTAX brand only six years after its introduction in 2005, a legend was born. Having used the camera now for some months the one word I would use to describe it is – Fast. I think this must have been one of the main design philosophies behind its conception. Unlike other medium format cameras, the CONTAX 645 has autofocus lenses, automatic film advance and wind-on as well as interchangeable backs for swapping film stock mid-shoot, replacing a finished roll can also be achieved even more quickly by simply opening the back, ejecting and loading a new pre-loaded film magazine. Add to this the bright viewfinder and great ergonomics with its main control dials gathered together above the comfortable grip that makes handling and using this balanced camera straightforward. This is where the 2CR5 battery is housed. Each battery is good for between 14-16 rolls of film. It’s a good idea to carry a spare battery because although there is a low battery warning feature in my experience it dies with very little warning. Rather than LCD panels the design uses traditional dials, each turn with a positive click and are dressed with Contax’s typical pyramid textured grip. The 645’s top plate layout has the speed control, exposure control, exposure compensation dial and shutter all together above the grip. There are all the usual flash photography features, which I’m not going to delve into mainly because I don’t use flash! Other controls such as mirror-up, DoF preview and drive mode are on the body. On the back of the grip within thumb reach is the focus control, a rotary switch with a central button. I use it in M manual mode by pressing the button to autofocus but it is possible to use S single autofocus or C continuous autofocus both controlled by a half depression of the shutter button. Focus confirmation is confirmed with a solid green • in the viewfinder and any fine-tuning of focus can always be achieved by turning the focus ring on the lens as well. The body houses a vertical-travel metal focal plane shutter that operates between 8 seconds and 1/4000 of a second. Used in aperture or shutter-speed priority it goes down as low as 32 seconds. At the top of the body is the removable focusing screen, with mine being the standard MFS-2 full matte. There is a central circle to indicate the spot meter area that appears within a rectangle that shows the focusing area, the screen is bright and final focus is easy to see. There are two other screens available with the system a split micro prism and a grid screen. The body also exposes reference data along the film rebate edge on the right side of each frame, this data includes frame number, aperture value, shutter speed, mode, lens and film type. I really like this analogue ‘metadata’ feature that permanently records information on the film, in particular the aperture and speed information of each frame. Contax 645 – Right Contax 645 – Left THE BACK The standard back on the CONTAX 645 has all the usual features of a modular system camera, a counter, ISO dial, multi-exposure lever and a slot for safe storage of the dark slide. As well as the standard back a rare Polaroid back was also available and of course, several digital backs were made by various manufacturers such as Sinar and Phase One. Although eye-wateringly expensive these old digital backs are still available on the used market and they offer an intriguing alternative capture method to film, one that I would love to maybe try one day. The back connects to the body with a slightly awkward twist and push button that unlike any of the other buttons and switches on the camera never feels quite right. Having said that, unless you are changing film type mid-shoot removing the holder body completely is not required to change film. At the rear of the back, there is a door that flips down to gain access to the film magazine. There are two types of magazines for the 645 and both are familiar to any photographer who has used this type of system camera before with little of note. Unfortunately, the vacuum back version, which uses the same ceramic based technology as the RTS III camera is limited to 220 film only, which is regrettably no longer available. Having said that, both magazines are easy to load with film and are well-made locating into the holder with a satisfyingly positive click. Once in the holder, a press of the shutter and the motor winds the film to the first frame and each frame thereafter. In continuous drive, the motor can advance up to 1.6 frames a second. Finally, after frame 16 the motors winds the remaining portion of the roll ready for removal. THE VIEWFINDER For me, this element of the design is probably the most attractive part of the camera. It sort of reminds me of H.R Giger’s Alien head but without the jaws. Long and sweeping back it is made from the same material as the body. The prism finder is elegant and distinctive with CONTAX embossed across its low front. On top is the flash shoe and a switch to swap between centre weighted and spot metering. There is a built-in +1 to -2 diopter control and an eyepiece shutter switch to stop stray light on long exposures. The eyepiece rubber is wide and comfortable. The screen has a magnification of 0.8% with a 95% field of view. Below the screen in green LED are the battery warning, exposure counter, meter mark, flash mark, focus confirmation dot, aperture value, shutter speed and exposure meter strip. As well as the MF-1 prism finder the CONTAX 645 can be fitted with the MF-2 waist-level finder. Obviously, given my reasons for wanting a 645, I have the MF-1 and it’s my favourite part of my camera except for the lens. Contax 645 – Front Right THE LENS Okay, so this has got to be the best part of the camera and it looks like I’m not alone in saying that. I even read somewhere that ‘were it not for this lens nobody would put up with that body’. I don’t agree but I get what the writer was trying to say. The standard Carl Zeiss Planar T 80mm ƒ2 is undeniably the best lens I have ever shot. Once again I’m not alone in feeling this way about it and like most other photographers who have used one it’s for the same reasons, plus a couple of my own. Look! If you have read about this camera before then you know all about the lens’s capacity to shoot sharp photos wide open at ƒ2 and how it makes painterly portraits each subject standing out with beautiful 3D separation from dreamy backgrounds. Not to mention all that stuff about wonderful bokeh. Yep, it does all that and it’s impressive for sure, so impressive a whole industry uses it as a signature look, but that’s another story. However, there are some other features that this lens also has that make it special, it renders contrast and tone really well with both excellent highlights and shadow detail. It handles colour and clarity superbly too, that and it’s straight as well, when shooting buildings and lines I have noticed very little distortion. I mentioned earlier that I took for granted the lens’s ability to perform well stopped down after all shooting urban landscapes at ƒ2 is not ideal. **William Meyers: Beyond the Bully Pulpit**

News - September 9, 2026 by Guénola Pellen In a Brooklyn subway station, William Meyers captured in 2015 a scene of deceptive ordinariness. Beside an elderly man lying on the platform, victim of an epileptic seizure, a woman kneels over him, watching. Her expression holds both urgency and solicitude. How many such gestures go unseen every day, for lack of a witness? Film and full frame, negative border included: William Meyers’s method is inflexible. He forbids himself any cropping, entrusting his negatives to Chuck Kelton, a master printer whose New York laboratory is one of the last true sanctuaries of traditional silver printing. The monochrome asserts itself. “Black and white, because it’s a somewhat archaic technology, contributes significance,” he says, “like playing Bach on period musical instruments.” A successive naval air intelligence officer in the United States Navy Reserve, staffer on a Senate subcommittee on poverty and employment, businessman and photography critic for the Wall Street Journal , William Meyers came to photographic practice in his fifties. This passage through American public life permeates every frame. The photography critic Vicki Goldberg, whom he readily quotes, wrote: “Vision probably draws as much from the life as from the eye, from the heart as well as the brain, from the complexities of personality more than from ingenuity or mastery of craft.” Only a man who has served, deliberated, and moved through the back rooms of public life could photograph democracy with this particular intimacy, neither naive nor cynical. From this emerges a radical formal decision: to stand in the ranks rather than face the podium. In the essay accompanying the work, journalist Nicole Gelinas notes that the images that persist in her memory are peopled with “backs of heads and shoulders.” William Meyers explains: “By having someone between the camera and the action, I put the viewer of the picture in the audience, recreating the experience of being there, not just showing what it looks like.” City Council hearing, City Hall, Civic Center, August 8, 2017. From ‘Civics’ by William Meyers published by Apollo Publishers At the City Council hearing in August 2017, a massive silhouette interposes itself in the foreground, while the room opens behind it onto portraits of George Washington and the Marquis de Lafayette, television cameramen, journalists, and rows of citizens come to testify. It is not power that William Meyers seeks to document here, but the vigilance of those who watch over it. This perspective produces some of his most striking images. On Broadway, a homeless man sleeps on the pavement in front of the closed window of a Victoria’s Secret lingerie store: the face of a mannequin, behind the lowered blinds, looms over the scene. On Madison Avenue in the snow, an inflatable union rat, symbol of American labor protest, doubles itself in the reflection of a glass facade, its claws seemingly threatening a passerby. “An important aspect of any art is the significant juxtaposition of key elements,” notes William Meyers. “I work at it.” While he claims the influence of Henri Cartier-Bresson, sharing his full-frame discipline, he puts it in service of a civic grammar rather than a mere poetics of the moment. Civil Society at Work Civics is organized around seven thematic chapters, two of which did not exist when the project was first shown at the Nailya Alexander Gallery in New York in 2016: “Social Capital” and “Civic Markers.” “I came to believe that democracy was as much a cultural phenomenon as a political one,” the photographer confides, “the markers, both formal and casual, remind us of what we have to be grateful for.” In “Social Capital,” five of the thirteen images are shot in a place of worship, a synagogue or a church, during a blood drive or a CPR training session. Homeless men are also sheltered on cots. None of these scenes take place in a government office. When the State falls short, citizens step in. Further on, in the newsroom of the New York Daily News , photographed in March 2015, journalists work at their screens while historic front pages line the walls: “Ford to City: Drop Dead,” “Men Walk on the Moon,” “Who’s a Bum!” Past and present of the news superimpose themselves within a single frame, a reminder that journalism too is a pillar of civic life, fragile, threatened, irreplaceable. From ‘Civics’ by William Meyers published by Apollo Publishers Two images, taken seventeen years apart, trace the book’s horizon. The first, shot in January 2026 on the steps of the Moynihan Courthouse in Manhattan, shows a woman draped in an American flag: she has just been naturalized as an American citizen and looks, says William Meyers, toward “new tomorrows.” The second, taken in May 2009 in Riverside Park, shows Memorial Day wreaths battered by a nocturnal storm. “The wind-blown memorial wreaths suggest the fragility of their sacrifice,” the photographer adds, “how tenuous their victories and how needful for the living to be mindful.” The future and memory, side by side, without hierarchy. “I have my own political preferences and prejudices,” concedes William Meyers, “but my aim in Civics was not to argue for any particular viewpoints but to concentrate on processes.” With the same attention and the same distance, he has photographed members of the Westboro Baptist Church, an American sect notoriously known for its hate-filled demonstrations, and a rally in favor of the Ground Zero mosque; the anti-capitalist Occupy Wall Street movement in Zuccotti Park and a march by the deeply conservative Tea Party; a Black Lives Matter banner on Broadway and an assembly standing at an official NYPD promotion ceremony. Of the Westboro church members, whom he says he detests without reservation, he retains one precise image: “The critical component of the picture is the cop in the foreground who is protecting their right to be despicable. That, and the pedestrians who are exercising their right to ignore them.” Democracy, in this light, is not agreement on values. It is the procedure that allows incompatible convictions to coexist without destroying each other. Manhattan Marriage Bureau, Louis J. Lefkowitz State Office Building, Civic Center, August 23, 2018. **El retrato de la niña afgana**

Si estamos hablando de un taller de retrato, tenía que llegar el momento en el que habláramos de este retrato. Seguro que lo conoces, vamos, es muy raro que si te interesa la fotografía no lo hayas visto nunca. Es un retrato de una niña que lleva un manto rojo sobre la cabeza y que, con unos ojos verdes enormes que parecen dos faros, mira a cámara muy seria. No es solo una mirada seria. Es una mirada impactante, llena de crudeza, es una niña de 12 años, pero la mirada es de alguien que ha vivido mucho y puede que terrible. Steve McCurry, La niña afgana, Pakistan, 1984. En efecto, la niña afgana, cuando fue retratada en 1984 por Steve McCurry, se encontraba en el campo de refugiados de Nasir Bagh durante la guerra de Afganistán. La foto fue portada del National Geographic y, en 1985, fue nombrada por la misma revista como la foto “más reconocida”. Lo que nos impacta de la foto, aparte de la mirada de la niña y la historia que nos cuenta, es sin duda, el color. Para hacer esta fotografía, Steve McCurry utilizó un carrete Kodachrome 64. Intuyo que en aquel tiempo, con aquella película y aprovechando la luz natural, al fotógrafo le bastó para obtener un resultado tan impactante. Pero… Qué pasaría hoy en día si el mismo McCurry fotografiase a la misma niña en el mismo lugar pero con una cámara digital? La cosa se complica. Porque en digital, para obtener unas fotografías de retrato con unos colores impactantes pero que respeten la piel del retratado, hace falta conocer la teoría del color y saber aplicarla a este tipo concreto de fotografía. Estos días te he hablado de la importancia de la luz, pues el color pasa a ser la 2º cosa más importante dentro de una foto. No lo olvides. Si la idea es irte a hacer fotos de retrato a Afganistan, quizá el taller de fotografía de retrato en estudio del 17 de octubre no es para ti.
