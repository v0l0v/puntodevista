Photos by David Jones ,  Shot with the LomoChrome Turquoise 120 ISO 100–400 These photos look more like Aerochrome Infrared and have a beautiful color palette. Can you tell us how you achieved this alternative look and why you decided to try this? I knew I wanted to go for a surreal look shooting in this location, and the idea of a color infrared style (Aerochrome) was very appealing, especially thinking in terms of the greenery and the effects it would have. With the obvious difficulties of getting color infrared film these days, I started looking around for ideas, and I was led to LomoChrome Turquoise. The colors of LomoChrome Turquoise work perfectly for this, with no extra editing needed. All from one slider in editing – couldn’t be simpler! With the added bonus of having two looks in one film. **Rolleiflex & Kalloflex – A Tale of Two Twins**

The TLR is a type of camera I have used regularly over the past 50 years or so. After prices tumbled post-millennium in the early days of digital and just after I retired, I was able to try quite a few more. I had never, however, been able to run to the top end makes, in particular the better Rolleiflexes or the quite rare Kalloflex, known as the poor man’s Rolleiflex. I did have a well used Rolleiflex Automat, probably the 1951 version with Synchro Compur but no light values. This was in the early digital era but I felt it was mostly little different from its near identical, later copy, the Yashicamat, introduced in 1957, that I had used for quite a few years back in the 1970s. The Rollei had the sensing bar that triggered the frame counter and the addition of a finder lens and mirror for focussing through the direct vision finder but neither feature was particularly significant for my purposes. At risk of putting everyone to sleep, I have to come back to the twin lens reflex. After taking a deep breath and exceeding my usual limit by three or four times I currently have an example of two similar but very different cameras to compare. One, the Kalloflex Automat K2, already reviewed, and the other, a recently acquired Rolleiflex T, first version, both in very good to near mint condition with the “T” which is a one-off within its family and the Kalloflex equally so but for different reasons. The design and finish of the two wind levers reflect the design objectives. Despite many similarities and based on the same basic design they are very different cameras. The Rollei is aimed at the keen amateur or casual, serious shooter, pitched at a price point mid-way between the expensive, top of the line Rolleiflexes and the simpler but more affordable Rolleicords. The Kalloflex on the other hand is a sturdy working tool, intended to appeal to the professional and press photographer. As a result, the Rollei’s design aims to express modern quality and refinement, the Kalloflex remaining closer to the older design with rugged practicality its most important feature. This gallery gives a closer look at details. The flash connections and the shutter release locks contrast in particular, the Kalloflex being almost identical to the original design. The strap attachment also shows more modern thinking with the Rollei, though their connection design was always easier to attach/detach than passing the strap through a simple slot and fixed to a buckle. A further example of the practicality of the Kalloflex is the provision of an accessory shoe with a securing thread for secure attachment. Both cameras have their roots in the early Rollei designs, particularly the post WWII double bayonet equipped Automats, the one I had. The T has developed this basic concept and refined some details to give it a more modern feel for the buyer of the day. The beefier Kalloflex is much closer to the original in many details with a few innovations of its own to fit with its intended professional user. The shutter release locks – open top. locked lower. The LV shutter scale and the adjusting lever which is pulled out to disconnect to adjust exposure settings separately and left in to change matched exposure settings. Apart from the Rolleimagics, the T is the only model that departed from that early basic design and only in small ways even then. Apart from the Rolleicord style film loading, shutter release/flash connection and the light value (LV) shutter everything else about it is straight from the standard parts catalogue. Even so the changes were apparently too much for the accountants and its designer’s tenure at the manufacturer, Franke & Heidecke, was very short I understand. Reflections on the two cameras. My often expressed criticism of the ergonomic design of the Rolleiflex is the need to switch hands between the focus/frame/shoot stages and the wind on/shutter cocking operation. This is because the wind lever is on the opposite side of the body to the focus control forcing a change of grip to wind on. I admire the Kalloflex because their designers combined the wind and focus at one location, the two mechanisms sharing a common hub. For myself, I have always found the Rollei arrangement tends to break one’s concentration in a situation where continuous exposures are being made, action or studio work for instance. This is particularly true when you are using the magnifier for critical focus and don’t want to take the camera away from the eye. Using the Kalloflex supports this completely and the process flows very smoothly. This style of working was being overtaken by the rapidly developing 35mm SLR with instant return mirror and motor drive though, especially for press work, cutting short its usefulness. Of the cameras I have used the ideal designs to my mind have been the Fexaret Va in particular and the Minolta Autocord, almost as good. The Flexaret Va release is in an excellent position, just ahead of the wind knob and placed at the same level on the front panel. The Minolta’s release is less conveniently placed lower down but still operated with the right hand.They both have lever focus below the lens which allows the left hand to support the camera and focus with no shuffle between exposures. Final thoughts Whenever I get hold of a new film camera, two things immediately strike me. Firstly how well it has been looked after and secondly the quality of its design and construction. Both here are very well made, in good condition and still operate well in all respects. The Rollei has apparently led a very sheltered life, probably on a tripod to judge by its condition and the accompanying Rolleifix tripod mount it came with. The Kalloflex has definitely been used quite a bit but with care. This is evidenced by its condition and the loss of the shutter release cap and the collar that have to be removed to fit a Leica type cable release, both easily misplaced in a working situation for a pro. I have never really collected cameras, they just seem to have accumulated over recent years. I have always used one extensively and appreciated the ones that produced good results and handled the best, I am also old enough to have experienced the rapid rise and refinement of the SLR in the later 20th century and then the digital camera which probably is why design and operation figure so highly with me. It was my youthful ambition to be an industrial designer, now called product design I believe, before being channeled into architecture. I started out with a box camera and then a folding Kodak, both with waist level viewing which I think is why the TLR appeals to me so strongly. It allows a certain detachment and a considered approach. These two examples are at the opposite ends of the user spectrum commercially though both are capable of dong the same work. But the Kalloflex is almost purely functional, building on the original design and refining it for a specific purpose. It can be appreciated for this alone and its really excellent build quality. The Rolleiflex T on the other hand is a refined and developed commercial design that built on the original concept by the maker who first produced the modified stereo camera and introduced the roll film version of the type to the world in 1928. TLRs had existed previously of course but the Rolleiflex brought the type into the easily hand-held category with convenient roll film as the sensitive material. The Heidoscope origins. There is no doubt that the small format cameras, 120 and 35mm, made more serious photography accessible than was possible with the simple box camera. George Eastman and the people who followed did that for film and people like Franke and Heidecke, Barnack and Hasselblad among others did the same for the equipment that used the films that became available. Nowadays the lens makers seem to be continuing the traditional way of working but even then with, no doubt, considerable help from computer technology and still basically refining existing optical designs. **The Photographers Who Shaped Our Picture of New York**

The inaugural installation at Aperture’s new space, Aperture Loves New York pays tribute to photographers who have shaped—and been shaped by—the city. Showcasing artists anchored in Aperture’s publishing history, the presentation brings together photographs alongside corresponding Aperture books that have advanced the medium of photography and captured the complex spirit of New York. Organized by Sarah Meister, Executive Director, the installation features photographs by more than twenty artists ranging from past to present—including Diane Arbus, Awol Erizku, Robert Frank, Justine Kurland, Stephen Shore, and more. Tina Barney, The Reception, 1985, from Family Ties (2024)Courtesy the artist and Olney Gleason, New York Tina Barney As she so often does, Tina Barney stages a familiar social ritual as a quiet psychological drama. In a wood-paneled room hung with paintings and ornamented with flowers, figures in formal dress seem gathered for celebration. Yet their gazes slide away from one another, and the seating arrangement feels oddly tense. What is happening beyond the frame that the women are paying attention to? Barney is known for large-scale color photographs that look effortless but are carefully constructed, often using the language of the family portrait to reveal what manners can conceal: hierarchy, intimacy, impatience. Made in New York in the mid-1980s—an era of sharpened wealth, display, and social choreography—this picture turns privilege into a subject, and the “nice” occasion into something charged and unsettled. Ernest Cole, Harlem, ca. 1968, from The True America (2024)© Ernest Cole Family Trust Ernest Cole Ernest Cole captures the city in motion: two women stride through the frame in bright patterned clothing, their faces crisp against a gentle smear of traffic and storefront signs. The slight blur of the street behind them and the one woman’s knowing look make Harlem feel fast, lived-in, charged. The neighborhood offers public space as both a backdrop and a pulse. Cole had arrived in New York only a short time earlier, after fleeing South Africa in 1966 to publish House of Bondage (1967), his devastating photobook on apartheid (reissued by Aperture in 2022). Living in exile, he turned his camera toward Black life in America, attentive to style, dignity, and the everyday theater of the sidewalk. Seen in the turbulent late 1960s—after civil rights victories, at the height of the Black Power movement, yet amid ongoing struggle—this photograph suggests both joy and urgency. Awol Erizku, Ruth E. Carter’s costume design, from Malcolm X, two embroidered kufis for Elijah Muhammad, Malcolm X’s sometime mentor, and one kufi for an assistant of Muhammad’s. The New Yorker, from Mystic Parallax (2023)Courtesy the artist and Sean Kelly, New York Awol Erizku In this image made for The New Yorker, Awol Erizku depicts three kufis designed by Ruth E. Carter for Spike Lee’s Malcolm X. Set against a roiling, clouded backdrop, the hats read like small constellations: carefully patterned, slightly luminous, and charged with the weight of history carried by everyday objects. The figures appear in profile, as if the camera has caught a passing formation rather than a posed tableau. Erizku moves fluidly between editorial commissions and a studio practice that quotes and reroutes art-historical forms, pop vernacular, and spiritual iconography. He may be best known for his 2017 portrait of Beyoncé announcing her twin pregnancy, in which almost archetypal symbols—the pose, the flowers, the veil—make contemporary fame feel like a myth already living in our collective imagination. Robert Frank, New York, 1955–65, from The Americans (1968/2024)© and courtesy The Robert Frank Foundation Robert Frank In this image, Robert Frank turns a street corner into a study of attitude. Three young people lean against what may be a storefront; one meets the camera head-on, one half-hides behind a hand, and one looks caught between performance and fatigue. Behind them, a sign warns “DON’T MISS,” as if the city itself were constantly advertising the next spectacle, while spikes on the railing in front of them hint that the spectacle could have an edge. Frank, a Swiss-born photographer living in New York, made this picture while developing the restless, off-kilter style that would permeate his 1958 book The Americans and reshape documentary photography. (The book was published in English the following year, and first published by Aperture in 1968.) He gravitated toward everyday scenes, recasting them as part of a searching, unsentimental portrait of the country. In the mid‑1950s, the Lower East Side was a dense, immigrant neighborhood, where life played out on stoops, sidewalks, and thresholds like this one. As Frank said in a 2015 interview, “When I was young, I could walk around, day after day, and hunt for pictures.” Advertisement Advertisement Nan Goldin, Trixie on the cot, New York City, 1979, from The Ballad of Sexual Dependency (1986/2021)Courtesy the artist Nan Goldin In Trixie on the cot (1979), Nan Goldin photographs a room with startling directness: a hard overhead light, a bar cot, and a figure resting on it in a dress and heels. The cluttered floor and improvised furnishings carry the atmosphere of hardscrabble downtown life: makeshift, intimate, often inscrutable to outsiders. Goldin began photographing as a teenager and developed her approach within the subcultures she lived alongside—first in and around Boston, then in New York after moving there in 1978. Rather than looking in from the outside, she made pictures from within a “chosen family” of friends and lovers, recording desire, tenderness, glamour, and collapse with the immediacy of lived experience. Those pictures were turned into slideshows that were accompanied by music, and later into her landmark book The Ballad of Sexual Dependency, a visual diary that helped redefine documentary intimacy. He has consistently been drawn to communities and places under pressure. Here, the uniforms and headbands are part of the performance. The picture also captures an important New York continuum—girls’ games and street culture alongside the early hip-hop era’s emphasis on crew, stance, and self-making in public. **Stephen Shore on How to Sharpen Your Mind’s Eye**

Walker Evans was given a major exhibition at the Museum of Modern Art in 1971. That same year, Evans and his friend Leslie George Katz, publisher of Eakins Press, collaborated on an extensive interview. At this retrospective moment of his life, Evans wanted to record his thoughts on photography, his work, and the influences on him. He wanted to codify his legacy. In addition to the interview, Evans selected and commented on eight photographs that spanned the arc of his life’s work. While the interview and the photographs appeared at the time in Art in America, the project didn’t see book form until 1995, when Eakins Press published Walker Evans: Incognito. Charles Baudelaire, one of Evans’s greatest influences, wrote, “The observer is a prince enjoying his incognito wherever he goes.” This suggests that the observer, like a prince traveling in disguise, has a privileged position: They can move through the world anonymously, seeing everything while remaining unseen themselves. The picture of a gutter was the most recent and least-known photograph in Evans’s selection. It is from 40 Studies of Street Debris, Manhole Covers, and Trash Bins, New York City (ca. 1968). Here is Evans’s accompanying text: While removed from nature, this picture of a gutter is in a sense very human. It is Baudelairean. I wish Baudelaire was alive to see it. The secret of photography is, the camera takes on the character and the personality of the handler. The mind works on the machine. When Evans wrote “Baudelairean,” he was referring to this paragraph, describing a chiffonnier, a ragpicker, from Baudelaire’s essay “On Wine and Hashish”: Here is a man whose task it is to pick up all the rubbish produced on one day in the capital. All that the great city has thrown out, all that it has lost, all that it has disdained, all that it has broken, he catalogues and collects. He consults the archives of debauchery, works through the lumber-room of rubbish. He sorts things out and makes a selection, chooses astutely; like a miser, he collects the muck which, once more digested by the Gods of Commerce, will become objects of utility or pleasure. Shop Now Like the ragpicker, a photographer “catalogues and collects”—a photographer “sorts things out” and “chooses astutely.” Here, Evans looks at a city’s debris, a most common and lowly subject, and in the image he offers a taste of his mind. As Evans elaborated in the interview, “The mind works on the machine—through it, rather.” Writing now in my late seventies, I know that a time may come when a person reviews their past actions and experiences and recontextualizes them in the light of the perspective of a lifetime. In his interview with Katz, Evans looked back on the experience of his hot streak in the 1930s. He called the experience “a very exciting, heady thing,” adding that “it goes like magic” and “I use the word transcendent.” Evans is describing what I call a gift state. Many artists and writers have the feeling, for a period of time, of something taking over, of something working through them. Evans was now connecting the transcendence of his experience making the work with the transcendence conveyed by the images. They were imprinted with a quality of his mind. Focus is the nexus that connects the photograph, the world, and the mind. It is the door to the passage from the surface to the interior. The only time I had the opportunity to hear Evans speak was at a lecture he gave at the Museum of Modern Art in the early 1970s. He showed Corrugated Tin Façade (1936)—a deadpan photograph of a dilapidated contractor’s office in Alabama, sunlight shimmering off the corrugation—and, by my recollection, called it a “transcendent document.” I knew that he had previously described “documentary style” as having the qualities of “severity, rigor, simplicity, clarity . . . without artistic pretension.” I was not expecting him to describe his work as transcendent. He was now indicating that below the surface of a “document” could lie an interior depth. He chose Matisse’s quote “L’exactitude n’est pas la vérité” (Exactitude is not truth) as the epigraph for his book Message from the Interior (1966). He is saying not to mistake the surface exactitude of documentary style for truth, but to look beneath the surface. Truth may be buried in its interior, the message from the interior. Truth can reside beneath the surface of the mundane, the quotidian. This recalls an Arab maxim, “The apparent is the bridge to the real.” Advertisement Advertisement An Evans photograph in which I feel this depth, this undercurrent, an image that moves me most deeply, is Breakfast Room at Belle Grove Plantation, from March 1935. In an early act of urban exploration, Evans, accompanied by his future wife Jane Ninas and her friend Christine Fairchild, broke into the Belle Grove mansion. They shared a picnic breakfast in this space he would later call the “breakfast room.” Before making the photograph, Evans arranged the shutters in the center of the picture, allowing just a crack of light to seep through. The Belle Grove Plantation in White Castle, Louisiana, was built by the sugar planter John Andrews in 1857. The seventy-five-room Greek Revival mansion was one of the largest residential buildings in the American South during the antebellum period. Belle Grove exemplified the grand architectural ambitions of Louisiana’s sugar aristocracy and stood as a testament to the economic power generated by the brutal system of enslaved labor that perpetuated this culture. Without enslaved labor, the plantation could not be sustained. It changed hands several times after the Civil War and was eventually abandoned. By the early twentieth century, the mansion had fallen into serious disrepair. Walker Evans, Breakfast Room at Belle Grove Plantation, White Castle, Louisiana, March 1935© Walker Evans Archive and The Metropolitan Museum of Art and courtesy SCALA/Art Resource, NY Giulio Romano, The Fall of the Giants, Palazzo Te, Mantua, Italy, ca. 1532–34Courtesy Erich Lessing and Art Resource, NY When I see the decaying Corinthian columns in the breakfast room, I think of Giulio Romano’s Sala dei Giganti (Hall of the Giants) in the Palazzo Te. Here the giants are trying in vain to hold up crumbling columns, massive columns tilting and breaking apart, the classical world disintegrating around them, as Zeus and the gods crush the giants for their hubris. Look at the photograph Breakfast Room at Belle Grove Plantation. Slowly, deliberately, move your attention, your focus, along the side walls to the columns, then to the floor of the small vestibule, and then to the shutters. Notice the depth of space from the columns, across the vestibule, to the shutters. Do you have a sensation of your eyes changing focus? Do you sense Evans’s experience? Do you have a taste of Evans’s mind? Focus is the nexus that connects the photograph, the world, and the mind. It is the door to the passage from the surface to the interior. It is a way of connecting with the mind that works through the machine.
