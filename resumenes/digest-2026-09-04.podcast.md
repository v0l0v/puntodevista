**What’s New ? Interview with Bastiaan Woudt and Vincenzo Maccarone  by Nadine Dinter**

I first met the photographer Bastiaan Woudtthrough gallerist Benjamin Jaeger, and was immediately drawn to his singular way of combining minimalism, portraiture, and mystery. With Bastiaan’s recent pilgrimage to Japan, and his first exhibition in Italy on the horizon, I wanted to look back at his story while catching up on both old and new work. I also had a chance to chat with his gallerist, Vincenzo Maccarone, co-founder with Claudio Composti of MC2 Gallery in Milan and Montenegro. Enjoy the read. Nadine Dinter: Bastiaan, your upcoming show is calledThe Shape of Absence – what a deep and philosophical title. Could you tell us how you landed on it, and what it’s about? Bastiaan Woudt: The title is Vincenzo’s. He proposed it for the show and I kept it. I would not have found those words myself, but they describe what I have been doing for fifteen years. Black and white came first, so the color was already gone. Then it became one figure instead of a scene. The background is empty, dark or light, so there is nothing to look at except the person. From a shoot of three hundred pictures, forty are left. The less I show, the stronger the image becomes. I notice it in the studio all the time. We build a picture up too far, and it gets better once we start removing things. A hat that covers half a face. A body that turns into a line. I want to leave something for the viewer to fill in. HENRO is where it went furthest. On Shikoku almost nobody wanted to be photographed. So the people in those pictures are seen in passing, or from behind, or too obscured to identify. I did not plan that. It is what the road gave me, and the pictures are better for it. The same thing is going on in the portraits and in the new work, which is why the title carries the whole show. There is a subtle tension between your poetically charged titles – such asPortal, Solace, Pearls, and Revolve – and the minimalist style your images are best known for. Is that a deliberate move, layering into something more romantic to enhance the storytelling? BW: The titles come after the picture. I do not photograph towards a word. I think the story is always secondary to the image for me. In some projects the narrative really matters, but a studio picture does not have to tell anything at all. I leave that to the viewer. People see whole stories in my work that I never thought of and never intended, and when that happens the picture has already done its job. I am closer to a painter building an atmosphere than to a writer telling a story. So a title is a name. It gives the work a handle and it stops there. Nothing in the show is captioned and nothing is explained. Portal is the one I would point at. I made it and then kept it on my list for years, because I wanted to be sure I understood it myself first. A black circle held inside a white form, the face appearing only at the edge. There is almost nothing in it, and that is the whole point. Your exhibition is composed of three segments: known work, previously unseen work, and your new seriesHENRO. How do you decide on the choreography of a show like this? What matters most when selecting the works, and is there a common thread running through all three segments? BW:There are eighteen works in the show and they come from three parts of my practice. The portrait work people know from me, a group of pieces I made this year that have never been shown anywhere, and a selection from HENRO. That is the first time HENRO leaves the Netherlands. I like working with a gallery that has taste and a good curatorial eye, and a selection is something you arrive at together. The gallery usually starts it. In this case Vincenzo put forward a group of works and I responded to that, and after a few rounds back and forth you have a selection you are both happy with. That is how these three groups came about. The new work, the HENRO pictures, and the older ones people already know. It is my first solo show in Milan, so we wanted it to be varied. You want to show your best work and the work you are known for, and at the same time something new like HENRO. It is a good opportunity to have all of that in one room. What connects them is the thing that connects everything I make. A sculptural way of looking at the human body, as a form within a space rather than as a story. Vincenzo, withThe Shape of Absence, you’re not only presenting Bastiaan’s work in Italy for the first time, but also kicking off your collaboration with him. What’s most challenging for you, as a gallerist, about introducing a new artist? Vincenzo Maccarone:Introducing a new artist is always a profound and exciting process, but with Bastiaan, there was a special kind of awareness: both Claudio and I have been following and admiring his work for many years. In the relationship between a gallerist and an artist, there is an almost energetic timing, a precise moment when trajectories finally align, and both feel completely ready. Not earlier, not later, but right now. Presenting a new visual language in a culturally rich and demanding context like Italy is undoubtedly a challenge. However, the core role of a gallery goes beyond simply showing artworks; it is about guiding and educating viewers toward new, more intimate visual perspectives. With The Shape of Absence, our goal was precisely to create the ideal context to convey Bastiaan’s overall poetics. We wanted to feature not only his renowned sculptural and constructed approach to photography – the main body of work that brought him international recognition – but also to place this production in direct dialogue with HENRO, his series created in Japan, which reveals a much more intimate dimension. The title itself, The Shape of Absence, stems from this very idea: absence is explored and interpreted in two fundamentally different ways. In his studio portraits, absence is minimal, controlled, and achieved by deliberately stripping away elements. In HENRO, on the other hand, absence is just as central, yet it is organic, suspended in the air, and shaped by the unpredictability of the journey itself. Bringing these two distinct expressions of absence together and letting them resonate within the same space was both the most exciting challenge and the true core of this exhibition. #### What makes Bastiaan’s work stand out among the other artists on your roster, and what do you personally and professionally find most intriguing about it? VM: What sets Bastiaan apart within our roster is an almost extreme formal rigor, combined with a unique sensitivity toward materiality and light. His visual language resides in a subtle territory between photography, painting, and sculpture: he does not merely capture an image, but constructs a physical, tangible presence – a process deeply rooted in meticulous research into materials, such as the use of Japanese washi paper and piezography printing. From a professional standpoint, what intrigues me most is his extraordinary capacity for visual synthesis. In today’s contemporary landscape, there is a frequent tendency to overload images with elements and narrative. Bastiaan, by contrast, works entirely by subtraction. Through an almost radical gesture, he strips away everything unnecessary to arrive at the absolute core. This formal purity gives his work an iconic, timeless power, while remaining firmly grounded in the language of contemporary fine art photography. On a personal level, I am deeply moved by the balance he achieves between mystery and delicacy. An artwork should never be overly explanatory or impose a closed narrative. The strength of Bastiaan’s imagery lies precisely in its sense of suspension: these works are fully realized in their execution, yet they remain open and quiet, allowing the viewer the mental and emotional space to complete the picture. It is this poetic tension, this ability to evoke so much through absence and light, that makes his practice so deeply captivating. Bastiaan, let’s talk more about your new series,HENRO. It was shot in Japan during a cycling trip with your brother along theShikoku Henro, one of the oldest Buddhist pilgrimage routes in the world. Tell usabout the project’s genesis, and how it all came together. BW: In March 2024 my brother Martijn and I went to Shikoku, an island in the south of Japan. We took our bicycles and our cameras and not much else. There was no plan and no shot list. We cycled the route, we did not walk it. After a few days everything starts to look the same. You get up, you pack, you ride, you arrive, you sleep, and you begin again. There is a lot of silence in that. I photograph people, and on Shikoku that did not work. Normally I have a place to make portraits and someone with me who can open a door, an interpreter or a local. I had neither. I carried a note in Japanese that explained who I was and that the work was meant as an homage to the pilgrimage, and I showed it often. Almost nobody wanted to be photographed. So the people you see in the series are photographed in passing, or from behind, or in a way that leaves them unrecognizable. That is simply what was possible there. Halfway around the island I stopped working the way I always work. I photographed everything instead of being selective, and I think that is why this project does not look like the others. I made the pictures in a few weeks. It took me two years to work out how they should exist. You had already started working with Japanese paper in some of your earlier work, and now the material and your latest series,HENRO, seem to have found each other. Does this less abstract, more poetic series feel like it reflects another side of your inner world? BW: I started using Japanese paper before HENRO, on the series Echo from Beyond. So it has been in my work for a while. With these pictures it works better than it ever has. The prints are piezography, made by hand on Japanese washi. The paper has a texture and a warmth of its own, and you see that in the print. I am not sure I would call it another side of me. The images are fragments. They do not explain where I was. For two years I worked on how they should be presented. The paper, the printing, the book, the exhibition space. The work just needed that time. Will we see more impressions from other countries in your future work, or was this a one-time “excursion” into another world? BW:There will be more. I have worked in Morocco, Uganda, Nepal, Zambia, and now Japan, so this is the fifth project of this kind. I love working abroad and stepping into new cultures. My curiosity for the world is enormous, and it keeps shaping the projects I take on. Going somewhere I do not know does something to the way I look that I could never achieve in the studio alone. HENRO follows Peak, which I made in Nepal. Peak was about altitude and endurance. This one turned inward. I go without a plan and without a shot list, and I end up with whatever the trip gives me. That was true in Uganda and it was true on Shikoku. There will be another one. I am not saying yet where. Bastiaan and Vincenzo, what makes an artwork appealing, and what vision do you each follow – as an artist, and as a gallerist? BW: The hardest part of making a strong image is simplicity. Building the most powerful picture out of as few elements as possible is genuinely the hardest thing there is, and when it works the satisfaction is enormous. An image has to raise questions. It has to feel right, and sometimes it should pull a little. In the studio we often go too far in building a picture up, and it gets stronger once we start taking elements away. An appealing artwork, for me, is the one you walk past and feel compelled to stop for. You look once, you look again, and you keep looking. It makes people walk back, ask questions, get emotional. That is what I am after. Trends barely register for me. I just walk my own path and work from what I find beautiful and interesting. My work has its feet in the past and its eyes on now. VM:I fully share Bastiaan’s perspective: a successful artwork should never provide easy or comforting answers; instead, it must raise questions and challenge the viewer’s gaze. For me, both as a gallerist and a collector, what truly makes an artwork captivating is its ability to withstand time and never exhaust itself upon first viewing. A powerful work possesses an internal layering that does not reveal itself all at once; it triggers an emotional and intellectual short-circuit that compels you to stop, step back, and re-read it every time from a new perspective. If an artwork continues to question you and move something deep inside years later, then it has achieved its truth. This connects seamlessly to my overall vision as a gallerist. My search is entirely unconcerned with fleeting market trends or passing fads, which so often prove to be superficial. I look for artists who possess an authentic, rigorous, and consistent voice – creators capable of digging deep into their own visual medium. The role of a gallery is not merely to display or sell, but to build a solid, cultural bridge between the artist and the viewer, guiding the collector’s eye toward a beauty that is complex, quiet, and enduring. What is next for you, Bastiaan? BW:The show travels. After Milan the work goes to ArtVerona from 9 to 11 October, and then to Roma Arte in Nuvola from 20 to 22 November, both with MC2 Gallery. The same framed works make all three trips, so it is really one journey. I will be in Verona as well. HENRO comes down in Alkmaar on 20 September. That is the last chance to see the full series in the Netherlands, and after that it is abroad. The book is at the gallery in Milan. HENRO is published by 1605 Collective in an edition of 750. And I am working on something I have wanted to try for years. My photographs already feel sculptural to me even though they live in two dimensions, and I am looking at whether I can turn that into actual three-dimensional work. It is early. I am not showing anything yet. **Jean-Marc Martin du Theil : Une vie, des photographies**

A photographer based in Geneva, Jean-Marc Martin du Theil has self-published a book, printed by Printer Trento in Italy, that tells the story of a life—his own—in photographs. Drawing on these words by photographer Raymond Depardon (from Voyages, Éditions Hazan): “I would tell a young photographer that they must photograph everything, have the awareness and the courage to photograph their life. It can be difficult because one will be accused of talking about oneself, but I believe that when you talk about yourself, you also talk about others,” Jean-Marc Martin du Theil has conceived a book in which photography follows a life, both professional and personal. The book brings together more than 500 images—both black and white and color—rooted in the tradition of humanist photography. It combines private and family photographs with nearly 50 years of theatre productions, from the TNP to theatre stages in the 1990s, as well as reportage work and travels, particularly in Asia. A carefully composed, deliberate, living memory—that is the aim of this photographic autobiography. As a journalist in Lyon (Le Progrès de Lyon, Le Monde, Rhône-Alpes/Suisse romande, cultural edition), Jean-Marc Martin du Theil worked with Marcel Maréchal at the Centre dramatique national in Lyon, with Roger Planchon and Patrice Chéreau at the Théâtre National Populaire (Lyon–Villeurbanne), and later with Georges Lavaudant at the Maison de la Culture in Grenoble. He also served as administrator of the Nouveau Théâtre de Poche in Geneva and as a civil servant for the City of Geneva. Alongside this, he pursued a career as a photographer with Marc Enguérand’s agency in Paris—an agency specializing in performing arts coverage—for which he photographed theatre, dance, and music from 1972 through the 2000s. The photographic archives documenting this work were transferred in 2013 to the Performing Arts Department of the Bibliothèque nationale de France (BNF) in Paris. His photographic work from Asia was presented in an exhibition in Geneva in 2013, featuring prints made by Thomas Consani, Marc Riboud’s printer. Franco-Swiss, born in Angers in 1941, Jean-Marc Martin du Theil has lived in Geneva since 1982. He is a member of the Société des auteurs des arts visuels et de l’image fixe (SAIF, Paris). He has self-published more than twenty books through Blurb, including documentary reportage projects (from Auschwitz to urban development sites around the Geneva region), books on writers such as Nicolas Bouvier and Maurice Chappaz, travel books on Asia and Europe, projects documenting the urban environment (graffiti), nature (trees and landscapes, as well as flowers from a garden), and the French theatre scene One Life, PhotographsJean-Marc Martin du Theil Self-published – €48 580 pages, 550 images, 19.5 × 22.5 cm Information: www.jmmartindutheil.com

**MAPh : 2026 Wai Tang Commissioning Award : Sarah Rhodes : The inner sky**

The Museum of Australian Photography (MAPh) presentsThe inner skyby Sarah Rhodes, for the2026 Wai Tang Commissioning Award. Sarah Rhodes’s body of work The inner sky uses analogue darkroom techniques to create layered photographs that speak to an experience of our inner and outer worlds. The artist created these images as the recipient of the Wai Tang Commissioning Award, and one work from the series will be acquired into the MAPh collection. Rhodes aims to translate the atmosphere of interiority into a two-dimensional image. She cuts, tears and folds paper to create apertures onto the expanse within and beyond. Using papers with different translucencies and textures, she investigates what happens when light passes through these layers: inviting the viewer to consider the interplay between what is known and what is imagined. Across the exhibition, shifts in scale alter the intimacy and intensity of this experience. At the centre of the exhibition is a figure shining a torchlight through a piece of semi-translucent paper. The figure is neither fully concealed nor fully revealed. The shadow cast by the figure works like a photogram made in the darkroom. Rhodes returns to this image repeatedly, remaking it through different layers of paper, translucencies and exposure times to explore how light can conceal, reveal and transform the image. Speaking to her process, she says, ‘I am interested in the photograph as an intimate, enclosed space, and in how cuts, layers and scale can draw us inward while opening onto something more expansive’. A finalist in the 2026 National Photography Prize, Rhodes continues to investigate complex processes both in the darkroom and in the environment. We are delighted to present her latest body of work in this solo exhibition, The inner sky, which considers whether our inner life can ever be separate from the spaces we inhabit. Sarah Rhodes lives and works in Lutruwita/Tasmania, where her practice explores how enclosed spaces shape inner experience. Using experimental analogue processes including photograms and shadow-based projections, she investigates relationships between body, atmosphere and interiority. Rhodes is drawn to spaces that heighten awareness of interior life. In her work The inner sky, she considers enclosure across the body, photographic frame, paper, caves and architectural space. The darkroom is central to this inquiry, functioning both as a studio and as a way of thinking. The song by Joni Mitchell contains the much quoted line “you don’t know what you’ve got till it’s gone”. Every time I came back from town I passed within a couple of metres of “County”, a small, old and battered, tracked bulldozer / tractor. Portra 400 test, badly processed and scanned by a commercial lab So what’s my point? Exactly what Joni sang: there may be a particular object, building or even person we pass or see regularly, something just a little beyond the ordinary that attracts our attention, one day it’ll be gone and it will be too late to make a record. If something gets your attention regularly, record it, record how it relates to the environment around it, find out why it attracts your attention. This is something I failed to do – “County” attracted my attention enough to use it as a test subject when I was trying out different films, cameras or lenses, but I failed to find out something about its history, why it was there and how or why it related to the other objects around it. This is something I am very bad at, often I’m just collecting an image, and I suspect many other photographers are like me. In many cases that is sufficient, but not all. We deserve to give the unusual, anything that is unusual enough to grab our attention or become part of our daily lives, not just a photograph but a story. Fill in the ‘how’, ‘why’, ‘when’ etc., and make a documentary about the object, then something meaningful will remain when the object has gone. I’ll try to listen to my own advice in future! **Meeting the Revolution, One Face at a Time**

News - September 3, 2026 by Marina Vitaglione For Amin Yousefi, the project began with a photograph that shouldn’t have mattered. Working through an archive in Tehran during the pandemic, he paused over an image of a student gathering from the years of the Iranian Revolution – not a defining historical moment, but an ordinary crowd. Among its faces, one person looked directly into the camera, carrying the unsettling sensation of being addressed by someone he could never have met. One photograph wasn’t enough to trust that feeling. It was only after encountering it again and again across the revolution’s vast photographic archive that the project began to take shape. Born in Abadan in 1996, Yousefi was thirteen during the disputed 2009 presidential election and the protests that became known as the Green Movement – old enough to witness one rupture in Iran’s history, but to inherit the revolution itself only through photographs, documentaries and annual commemorations. Rather than retelling its history, he followed that intuition, rephotographing archival images through a magnifying loupe until the encounters themselves became the work. “For me,” he says, “it was more about this encounter.” Untitled, Eyes Dazzle As They Search for the Truth © Amin Yousefi Untitled, Eyes Dazzle As They Search for the Truth © Amin Yousefi Untitled, Eyes Dazzle As They Search for the Truth © Amin Yousefi Untitled, Eyes Dazzle As They Search for the Truth © Amin Yousefi Untitled, Eyes Dazzle As They Search for the Truth © Amin Yousefi Untitled, Eyes Dazzle As They Search for the Truth © Amin Yousefi Photographs made to record a revolution begin, almost against their own nature, to behave like portraits. What emerges is the quiet reciprocity of photography: anonymous crowds give way to individuals who actively acknowledge the photographer’s presence. For a moment, the revolution recedes behind something quieter: the brief acknowledgement between photographer and subject, before history carries them both away. One enlarged face eventually led Yousefi to its owner: pioneering Iranian photojournalist Hengameh Golestan, captured looking towards American photographer David Burnett as he photographed the revolution. That ambiguity runs throughout the book. Here, the people gathered wanted to be seen; contemporary protesters, he observes, often seek the opposite. The book’s design quietly reinforces that tension. Each enlarged photograph faces a page of solid black, isolating the gaze before the sequence gradually pulls back to reveal more of the surrounding scene, until individual faces dissolve once again into the crowd. The experience feels less like turning the pages of a photobook than adjusting one’s own eyesight – moving between intimacy and distance, lucidity and doubt. **Pollobarba publica ‘VIVA LA FOTO’, un libro para que disfrutes de la fotografía**

El fotógrafo madrileño y experto en redes Pollobarba lanza su primer libro, VIVA LA FOTO', con el objetivo de recuperar el placer de fotografiar, alejándose de los discursos técnicos, académicos y solemnes. Pollobarba publica oficialmente este sábado, 5 de septiembre, a las 11:11 horas en su propia página web , el libro VIVA LA FOTO', una autopublicación con la que celebra su 50 cumpleaños y en la que proponer al público una aproximación más amable a la creación visual. El libro se presentará el jueves 24 de septiembre a las 19.30h en la librería Railowsky de Valencia y estará presente en Fiebre de la mano de Fuego Books. VIVA LA FOTO' se compone de 91 fotografías y 758 palabras que parten de la sospecha del autor de que nos estamos tomando la disciplina demasiado en serio. “La fotografía ha muerto. Ya están hechas todas las imágenes. O las puede hacer un robot. Sólo queda disfrutar”. Así comienza&nbsp;la publicación que anima a quitarse presión de encima, sugiriendo que no hace falta saberlo todo, poseer un estilo definido, buscar el triunfo o tener clara la intención detrás de cada imagen. A pesar de rehuir de la solemnidad, el volumen también trata cuestiones como la historia, la narrativa, la autoría o la responsabilidad a la hora de retratar a terceras personas. Todo ello se articula a través de textos breves, preguntas e imágenes. El proyecto ha sido diseñado y escrito por el propio autor, quien también firma las fotografías. La digitalización y la preimpresión han corrido a cargo del Estudio&nbsp;Paco Mora, y la impresión del ejemplar ha sido realizada por La Imprenta CG. Con VIVA LA FOTO', Pollobarba pasa a formar parte del selecto club de autores y autoras imprescindibles que reflexionan sobre la fotografía, entre los que se encuentran Joan Fontcuberta, Carmen Dalmau o Eduardo Momeñe, afianzando así su candidatura al Premio Nacional de Fotografía, que espera conseguir antes del 2035. © Pollobarba Pollobarba Pollobarba&nbsp;(Madrid, 1976) es fotógrafo y activista de la fotografía. Licenciado en Publicidad y Máster de Fotografía EFTI, ejerce como especialista en redes sociales y asesor de profesionales, entidades y proyectos fotográficos diversos. A lo largo de su trayectoria ha impartido conferencias y talleres en escuelas, másteres y festivales, y ha participado en iniciativas como la Plataforma Centro de Fotografía, #nosinfotografas o la Beca Fuego. **Solo tienes que apretar el botón**

Hoy a las 23:59 hora de cima Colladareta acaba el plazo. Te lo llevo contando muchos días así que si has decidido no darte la oportunidad de sacar esa preciosa cámara y ser feliz de una vez y para siempre disparando (por fin) en manual, tu sabrás por qué. Aun tienes la oportunidad, digamos que hoy es jornada de reflexión. Yo te voy a contar otra historia muy chula relacionada con la fotografía que es lo que me gusta escribir por aquí y te dejo pensando. Amoallá. Desde el principio de la fotografía y de los procesos fotográficos, hubieron personas que se desmarcaron de la parte artística de la fotografía para buscar el lado económico y comercial. Lo lógico para un ser humano, nada que criticar. Piensa que desde que dejamos de ser nómadas y comenzamos a asentarnos y a cultivar cereales, ya empezamos con el comercio. Nuestro cerebro esta diseñado para buscarnos el pan lo mejor que podamos. Dos grandes exponentes de esta búsqueda comercial fueron: Disderi y sus Cartes de Visite En 1851, el amigo se inventa una cámara con 6, 8 o 12 objetivos que permite sacar hasta 12 fotografías con una única placa. La utiliza para hacer retratos de estudio. Fue un gran éxito comercial. Pero el puto amo, el que creó la primera gran multinacional relacionada con la fotografía fue el grandísimo George Eastman. Empezó en la cocina de su madre fabricando placas, como los de Silicon Valley pero 100 años antes. En 1881 comienza a producir el rollo de película y crea la empresa Eastman Kodak. Oh my God!!! Varias fotografías sin tener que cambiar placas ni hacer nada!!! Un equipo fotográfico transportable con comodidad!!! El paraíso. Y no contento con eso, empieza a hacer publicidad… Anuncio de la primera cámara Kodak con el eslogan: You press the button, we do the rest. La gente compraba una cámara, hacia las fotos, y la enviaban para revelado a la planta de Eastman Kodak. Los de Kodak revelaban las fotos y devolvían la cámara nuevamente cargada, más los negativos y las fotos. Una absoluta genialidad. Diferencia así dos tipos de publico: fotógrafos profesionales comprometidos con la fotografía como arte y fotógrafos aficionados que lo único que buscan es conservar recuerdos.
