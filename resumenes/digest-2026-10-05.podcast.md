**Exploring the Joseph Petzval Métamorphose 35 f/1.8 Art Lens with Thin Thinnarat**
Fotógrafos: nweerapat

Meet Thin Thinnarat, a familiar face in the Lomography Community, who's back with the new Joseph Petzval Métamorphose 35 f/1.8 Focus-Coupled Bokeh Control Art Lens. This time, he took it out into nature for a series of portraits exploring the lens's distinctive character and unique effects. See what the lens made of it. © hwaung.kwa ,  Lens: Joseph Petzval Métamorphose 35 f/1.8 Focus-Coupled Bokeh Control Art Lens ,  Model: chanisara.no Hello and welcome back! Could you briefly introduce yourself to our readers again? Hi, everyone! I’m Thin, Thinrat Boonek, the creator behind the Facebook page Mr.Thin and Instagram hwaung.kwa. What were your first impressions of the Joseph Petzval Métamorphose 35 Lens? My first impression was that it’s a 35 mm lens with a very distinctive bokeh character. You can use it to create images with a different style, and I found it easier to use than other Petzval lenses I’ve tried, whether when focusing or experimenting with the bokeh and other effects. © hwaung.kwa ,  Lens: Joseph Petzval Métamorphose 35 f/1.8 Focus-Coupled Bokeh Control Art Lens ,  Model: chanisara.no What do you like about this lens, and did it take time to adjust to its character? I like that it’s a 35 mm lens. It allows us to play with the lens’s bokeh effects and make them more noticeable compared to lenses with longer focal lengths. It’s another Lomography lens with a strong character, which is something I’ve always enjoyed. And it didn't take me long to adjust, as I already regularly work with lenses that have unusual and distinctive characters like this. © hwaung.kwa ,  Lens: Joseph Petzval Métamorphose 35 f/1.8 Focus-Coupled Bokeh Control Art Lens ,  Model: chanisara.no Could you tell us more about the concept behind this shoot? What did you photograph, and how did the lens perform? I wanted to create images using natural light, so I took the lens to a grass field and waited for moments when the light came through. It was quite stormy that day, so I had to wait for the right moments when the sunlight came through the trees. The lens performed really well, especially when it came to focusing. I found it easier to focus than the other lenses from the Joseph Petzval series that I’ve used before. © hwaung.kwa ,  Lens: Joseph Petzval Métamorphose 35 f/1.8 Focus-Coupled Bokeh Control Art Lens ,  Model: chanisara.no How does this lens differ from the Joseph Petzval lenses you’ve used before? The main difference is that it offers three different characters. The first character has an effect similar to a projector lens, with the effect zooming toward the center and making the subject in the middle of the frame stand out. The second creates a swirly bokeh, while the third produces a bubble-like bokeh that gives the images an old-fashioned, vintage feel. It’s also different from the Nour Triplet, which I own, because of its 35 mm focal length that allows you to capture a wider perspective. © hwaung.kwa ,  Lens: Joseph Petzval Métamorphose 35 f/1.8 Focus-Coupled Bokeh Control Art Lens ,  Model: chanisara.no What kind of environment do you think this lens works best in? Personally, I think it works really well for daytime portraits with plenty of light. You can adjust it depending on what you want. Whether you want a sharper image or more pronounced bokeh, you can easily adjust it to suit your needs. Do you have any advice for photographers who would like to try the Joseph Petzval Métamorphose 35 Lens? For anyone looking to create something new and bring a different look to their work, I’d recommend giving this lens a try. © hwaung.kwa ,  Lens: Joseph Petzval Métamorphose 35 f/1.8 Focus-Coupled Bokeh Control Art Lens ,  Model: chanisara.no A big thank you to Thin for trying out the Joseph Petzval Métamorphose 35 f/1.8 Focus-Coupled Bokeh Control Art Lens and sharing his experience with us! **The Questionnaire : Jérôme Trichet by Carole Schmitz**

Jérôme Trichet : The mystery of the moment There is, in Jérôme Trichet’s photography, a particular attentiveness to what escapes the immediate gaze. Neither entirely documentary nor strictly staged, his approach explores that uncertain space where reality eludes its own apparent clarity. A fleeting gesture, a silhouette, a reflection in a shop window or a light that reshapes the contours of everyday life become fragments of a reconfigured reality, one whose meaning is never fully disclosed. Informed by the aesthetics of Helmut Newton and the sensibility of Paolo Roversi, Jérôme Trichet maintains a rigorous relationship with composition, lines and geometry. Black and white, which gives definitive form to most of his photographs, strips the subject of the anecdotal to reveal its structure, tension and, at times, its strangeness. Yet beneath this formal command, his work is driven neither by aesthetic demonstration nor by the pursuit of the perfect frame. Instead, he favours a receptiveness to the unexpected, an ability to embrace what unfolds and discover, within the resulting image, something beyond what the eye initially perceived. It is precisely in this gap between intention and revelation that one of the distinctive qualities of his approach resides. Photography becomes less an instrument of capture than a means of questioning perception, of allowing emotion to emerge without defining it, of suggesting rather than imposing. The unseen, silence and ambiguity play a decisive role: they open the image to multiple interpretations and leave the viewer free to extend the narrative. Balancing intuition and construction, Jérôme Trichet thus develops a restrained photographic language in which attentiveness becomes an aesthetic stance in its own right. His images seek not so much to depict the world as to subtly shift our perception of it. A way of reminding us that to photograph is also to learn to see differently, and that the power of an image sometimes lies less in what it reveals than in what it leaves unresolved. Instagram: @jerometrichetphotographieWebsite: www.jerometrichet.kabook.fr Your first photographic revelation?Jérôme Trichet: As a teenager, I was captivated by vinyl record sleeves, which were probably the first photographic images I encountered outside my immediate personal world. David Bowie’s album covers in particular, especially Heroes, with its strikingly graphic portrait of Bowie by Japanese photographer Masayoshi Sukita. I also remember an album cover by the Berlin Philharmonic Orchestra featuring a powerful portrait of its conductor, Herbert von Karajan. Before I ever started taking photographs myself, I think I was first drawn to the images that accompanied music. I still am today, and I continue to enjoy browsing through the vinyl sections in record stores. A photographic memory from your childhood?Jérôme Trichet: Family photographs. Pictures of celebrations and holidays, which my mother carefully arranged in albums. And then there were the slide evenings, when my father would project holiday photographs and sometimes a few Super 8 films he had shot. Two very simple rituals centred on images and memories. The camera of your childhood?Jérôme Trichet: I don’t remember having a camera as a child. I did, however, draw a great deal: houses, façades, gardens, floor plans… At the time, I dreamed of becoming an architect. Looking back, I can perhaps recognise something of my photographic eye today in those early drawings. The camera you use today?Jérôme Trichet: A Leica D-Lux 8. My iPhone as well, which I always carry with me. And, occasionally, a Polaroid. The man or woman behind an image who inspired you?Jérôme Trichet: Helmut Newton was probably the first. I was drawn to the way he glorified the women he photographed while retaining something intriguing, ambiguous, and sometimes even unsettling in his images. The image you wish you had taken?Jérôme Trichet: An album cover that I could then discover, with pleasure, in the record racks. The one that moved you the most?Jérôme Trichet: Photographs of my children when they were little still move me deeply. If I think of a photograph that is not part of my own personal history, I would choose Paolo Roversi’s 2003 portrait of Natalia Vodianova in Paris. Her gaze seems to reveal something profoundly inward, almost intimate. Roversi once said that exposure time allows you to “give the soul time to surface.” That is exactly what I feel when I look at this photograph. And the one that made you angry?Jérôme Trichet: I can’t recall a photograph that has ever made me angry. Which photograph changed the world?Jérôme Trichet: The photographs of the fall of the Berlin Wall, particularly those by Raymond Depardon. I lived in West Berlin in the early 1980s. When I returned to Paris in 1989, these images had a particular resonance for me: I had known a city divided in two, and I could grasp the significance of the sudden disappearance of a border that so many people had tried to cross, some losing their lives in the process. And which photograph changed your world?Jérôme Trichet: A photograph I took almost spontaneously one evening in 2019. I was having a drink with a friend on the terrace of a Parisian brasserie. She stretched out her arm, and I instinctively photographed a fragment of that gesture. When I looked at the image, I surprised myself: I saw something in it that I hadn’t perceived at the moment I pressed the shutter. That photograph has stayed with me. Looking back, I believe it marked the starting point of the photographic approach I would go on to develop. The ability of photography to surprise me is something that continues to fascinate me just as much today. A defining image in your personal pantheon?Jérôme Trichet: More than a defining image, it is a defining format: the square. The Polaroid format, for example. I particularly like fashion Polaroids, sometimes taken on the spur of the moment and annotated with references or notes relating to the shoot. I have a particular appreciation for Helmut Newton’s Polaroids. I always keep the book Pola Woman, which brings together more than a hundred of his Polaroids, close at hand, and I regularly return to it with great pleasure. What interests you most in an image?Jérôme Trichet: Its evocative power. I like a photograph that doesn’t reveal everything, that leaves room for uncertainty, ambiguity and what lies beyond the frame, giving the viewer enough space to imagine their own story. What details do you look for in a face, a landscape or an object?Jérôme Trichet: Lines and geometry. I like it when a sense of balance emerges, when a certain beauty of form appears and holds my attention. Elliott Erwitt said, “Colour is descriptive. Black and white is interpretive.” Do you agree?Jérôme Trichet: Instinctively, yes. I feel that colour tends to anchor an image more firmly in reality, whereas black and white can distance it from pure documentation and open up a space for interpretation. This is one of the reasons why I work in black and white. But I see it more as a guiding principle than a rule, because there are obviously exceptions. In your view, can technique take precedence over emotion in photography?Jérôme Trichet: I don’t think so. A photograph can convey emotion without sophisticated equipment or highly developed technical skills. Technique remains a means to an end. What matters above all is the eye and the intention behind it. Besides, isn’t the best camera simply the one you happen to have in your hands when you feel the urge to capture a moment or a situation? Is beauty in photography purely aesthetic for you?Jérôme Trichet: No, not exclusively. Of course, I attach great importance to the aesthetics of a photograph: its subject, composition, framing and a certain beauty of form. But its beauty can also lie in what it keeps in the shadows, in the uncertainty, tension and ambiguity it creates, and in the space for interpretation it opens up beyond the frame. Ultimately, what a photograph does not show matters just as much as what it reveals. What elements can make silence visible in a photograph?Jérôme Trichet: Space, first and foremost, but also emptiness. And everything that prevents an image from being too explicit or too direct: a shadow, a silhouette, a profile, a posture rather than a face shown frontally. The less a photograph reveals, the more it can, I believe, allow silence to be heard. Does the uniqueness of a photograph come from the moment or from the staging?Jérôme Trichet: From the moment. A staged image can always be reproduced, at least to some extent. A captured instant, on the other hand, can be entirely unique and sometimes even surprise the photographer themselves. For me, it is this element of unpredictability that makes a photograph unique. Can a photograph be truer than reality?Jérôme Trichet: Sometimes. When a truth is revealed through the particular attention a photographer pays to their subject. Photography invites us to observe and see things differently. That attention can bring to light something that might otherwise have escaped our first glance. Can a photograph change our perception of an event?Jérôme Trichet: Absolutely, because a photograph is necessarily a subjective interpretation of reality. I think this question is particularly relevant in photojournalism and documentary photography, for example, where the photographer carries a genuine responsibility. Is photography a form of testimony or a form of manipulation?Jérôme Trichet: Photography is a form of testimony and, like any testimony, it results from choices and inevitably conveys a point of view. That doesn’t necessarily mean it is manipulative. We can, however, speak of manipulation when an image deliberately seeks to steer or distort our perception of reality. A risk that is obviously taking on a new dimension with the development of artificial intelligence. What makes a good photograph?Jérôme Trichet: It is obviously a very subjective and personal matter. For me, a good photograph is first and foremost one that is pleasing to the eye, if I may put it that way. This has to do, in particular, with framing, composition and a certain beauty of form. But it is also an image that challenges us, raises questions and leaves room for the imagination. In your view, what quality is essential to becoming a good photographer?Jérôme Trichet: Attentiveness. The ability to slow down, observe and perceive what might escape the first glance. How do you choose your projects?Jérôme Trichet: I’m fortunate enough to be able to choose my projects freely, since at this stage I don’t work on commission. For my more developed projects, particularly my series, I generally start with an intention and images I want to create. I put that intention into writing in a statement that helps me clarify what I’m looking for, as well as share it with the people involved in the project, a model, for example. I sometimes add a mood board and various sources of inspiration. That framework is never fixed, however. Once a project is underway, it is important to me to leave room for the unexpected and allow myself to be surprised by whatever may emerge. Alongside this, of course, I continue to take photographs very spontaneously whenever an opportunity presents itself. How would you describe your creative process?Jérôme Trichet: It often begins with something that catches my attention: a situation, a shape, a particular light, or sometimes simply an idea. It may trigger an immediate desire to take a photograph or, on the contrary, become the starting point for a project that I will allow to mature and gradually develop. In both cases, I also try to remain receptive to whatever may arise along the way. An upcoming project that is particularly close to your heart?Jérôme Trichet: Creating an album cover for a musician. Record sleeves were one of my earliest encounters with photography. Given the place music now occupies in my photographic practice, being able to create an album cover one day would inevitably have a special meaning for me. And I would also like to organise my first solo exhibition, following a few group exhibitions. The person you would most like to photograph?Jérôme Trichet: A musician, male or female. I would like to capture the relationship they have with their instrument, to try to make visible the very particular bond that unites them. More specifically, perhaps, a violinist or a cellist, because I find something in their gestures and postures that evokes the aesthetics of dance, as though the instrument sometimes became an extension of their body. That is the dimension I would like to capture in a photograph. The person you would like to photograph you?Jérôme Trichet: Apart from straightforward portraits for presentation purposes, it’s not really a question I’ve ever asked myself. An indispensable photography book?Jérôme Trichet: It depends on the period. At the moment, there are two books that accompany me. First, the catalogue of the Paolo Roversi exhibition presented at the Palais Galliera in 2024, which remains one of the most beautiful photography exhibitions I have ever seen. And then Lettres sur la lumière, which brings together the correspondence between Paolo Roversi and philosopher Emanuele Coccia on the subject of light. Two books I return to regularly and find particularly inspiring. What was the last photograph you took?Jérôme Trichet: As I speak, the last photograph I took was an image captured on my iPhone on my way home: reflections in shop windows. It is a subject I particularly enjoy photographing. Different planes overlap, ephemeral shapes emerge and come together to form an image that is sometimes geometric and slightly mysterious. On social media, are you more of an Instagram, Facebook or TikTok person – and why?Jérôme Trichet: Instagram, exclusively. It seems to me to be the platform best suited to photography, both for showcasing and promoting my work and for discovering other photographers and finding inspiration in what they do. I prefer to focus on a single platform rather than spread myself too thin. In your opinion, what has changed in photography since the rise of social media?Jérôme Trichet: Social media has considerably democratised the distribution of photography. It allows photographers, in particular, to show their work to a much wider audience and gain visibility that would previously have been difficult to achieve. The downside is the sheer abundance of images, which leads us to look at and consume much more quickly. For professional photographers, there is also much greater competition in a rapidly evolving market. This is also why I see a growing interest among photographers in organising exhibitions. They offer a more tangible relationship with photography: they allow people to pause, take their time looking and discover a selection of images in a way that differs from the endless scrolling of social media. My own experience of participating in exhibitions has reinforced this conviction. One Instagram account everyone should follow?Jérôme Trichet: Mine, of course! (Smiles.) It’s difficult to choose just one. On Instagram, I tend to discover accounts organically. I follow a great many photographers, but also galleries and, more broadly, artists working in very different fields. It is precisely this diversity of perspectives that interests and inspires me. What is your view on AI?Jérôme Trichet: I believe artificial intelligence represents a major development, the full consequences of which we probably have yet to grasp. It opens up considerable possibilities while, of course, raising many questions about its uses and limitations. Personally, I use it extensively as a research and assistance tool, and I find it extremely useful. However, I don’t use it as a creative tool. It can support my practice, but for me, it cannot replace the act of taking a photograph. Colour or black and white?Jérôme Trichet: Black and white, first and foremost. Almost all my images ultimately take their final form in black and white, even if they were originally shot in colour. That said, I do sometimes allow myself to be surprised by colour. Natural or artificial lightJérôme Trichet: Light obviously plays a major role in a photograph and can influence my decision to press the shutter. Whether it is natural or artificial, however, is not a criterion in itself for me. Which city do you find the most photogenic?Jérôme Trichet: I never tire of Paris. The city, country or culture you dream of discovering?Jérôme Trichet: Japan. The place you never tire of?Jérôme Trichet: My home in Paris. And ultimately, what I enjoy is leaving it so that I can return to it with renewed pleasure. The image that best represents the current state of the world?Jérôme Trichet: The photograph by Maximilien Lamy that went viral, taken last July, showing a couple sitting beneath a parasol facing a massive wildfire in the Gironde region. I interpret it as a symbol of inertia in the face of the crises our society is confronting today on so many fronts. In your opinion, what is missing from today’s world?Jérôme Trichet: Attentiveness. In a daily life saturated with demands and information, it has become a fragile and precious resource. Yet it is essential to our ability to discern, perceive nuances and notice everything that does not immediately reveal itself. All of these things are vital to understanding the world around us. **Bristol Photo Festival 2026 : Time Machine**

Bristol Photo Festival, the international biennial of contemporary photography, has announced the exhibition programme for its third edition, Time Machine. Opening this autumn, with the opening week from 14 – 18 October 2026, the Festival introduces nine major exhibitions and a landmark historical exhibition across Bristol’s leading museums, galleries, art centres, historic buildings and independent spaces. Drawing on the success of its second edition, which attracted over 115,000 visitors, it brings together internationally acclaimed artists alongside significant voices from the UK and beyond. All exhibitions are free to visit, with donations welcome. The programme spans a broad range of artistic positions and photographic histories. Major solo exhibitions include Joy Gregory’s exploration of migration, memory and identity; Akshay Mahajan’s investigation into folk, borders and material culture including new commission works in response to the British Empire & Commonwealth Collection; George Hallett’s powerful photographs of writers and musicians in exile curated by Christine Eyene; and Photo Kegham, the first commercial photo studio in Gaza founded by Kegham Deghalian Sr in 1944 and unearthed and newly curated by his grandson, Kegham Deghalian Jr. Alongside these are Michel Campeau’s celebrated photographic meditation on the disappearing analogue darkroom, Dark Tales, a group exhibition curated by Isaac Blease exploring the relationship between the Gothic and British documentary photography; Family Times by Annie Wang and Liu Hsing Yu, reinventing the family album; and No Pasarán, an exhibition marking the 90th anniversary of the beginning of the Spanish Civil War through the contemporary works of Anna Jornet and Julián Barón and a newly commissioned work by Natasha Christia about the International Brigades. The Festival also presents One of Many: An Alternative History of Photography, a major exhibition developed in partnership with the Institut français du Royaume-Uni that proposes new ways of understanding photography’s histories through overlooked narratives and alternative genealogies. Entitled Time Machine, the third edition of Bristol Photo Festival, explores time not as a linear progression but as something layered, cyclical and continually unfolding. Across photography, moving image, archives and installation, the featured artists consider how histories persist within the present, how personal and collective memories shape contemporary experience, and how culture can help imagine alternative futures. Rather than documenting fixed moments, the exhibition programme presents photography as a medium through which time accumulates, folds back upon itself and remains open to reinterpretation. Alejandro Acín, Festival Director, says: “Time Machine brings together artists and curators whose work reminds us that the present is never singular. Every photograph carries traces of multiple temporalities — memories that continue to shape us, histories that refuse to disappear and futures that remain open to possibility. At a time when cultural organisations across the UK face significant challenges, this edition is also a testament to the power of collaboration. It has only been possible through the commitment of artists, funders, partners and communities who believe that this festival can help us better understand the complexity of the world we inhabit.” The main cultural partners are: Arnolfini, Bristol Museums, Martin Parr Foundation, Watershed, University of the West of England, Brigstow Institute and University of Bristol alongside independent art and community organisations. **Galerie Roger-Viollet : Jean Marquis, arpenteur du quotidien – Photographies, 1951-1983**

Galerie Roger-Viollet explores the work of Jean Marquis for the first time, presenting one hundred and twelve modern prints, in a numbered edition limited to 30 copies, from October 8, 2026 to January 23, 2027. Françoise Denoyelle writes: In 1951, Robert Capa advised Jean Marquis (1926-2019), an actor and drama workshop leader, to look toward photography. He had the eye. Three years later, he joined the Magnum Photos agency alongside Robert Capa, Henri Cartier-Bresson, and Chim. From streets to fields, from factories to haymaking, from Liverpool to Budapest, from settlers in Algeria to the camel drivers of the Touggourt market and the Clos Salembier shantytown (1958), Jean Marquis roams the everyday: Pierre Mendès France reading the press in a train, bread being made in a village, Jean Renoir directing actors for French Cancan. Jean Marquis orders space, arranges his figures, frames with mastery: the hardworking crowd of Merlebach heading home, old ladies with devastating eyes on rue du Petit-Musc, a lone railwayman along the Deûle, his first major feature “initiatory” for Magnum. The photographs impose themselves with the self-evidence of their slice of life, symbols of the ancestral customs of the Corrèze, testimonies to the modernity of the Trente Glorieuses, documents of the permanence of struggles. Edward Steichen, director of the photography department at MoMA, spots him and selects him for the exhibition The Family of Man (1955). Numerous retrospectives in institutions in France and abroad will follow. Jean Marquis covers the social and artistic effervescence: sit-down strikes, rallies in workplaces, demonstrations of every kind in support of causes close to his heart. Support in Paris, place de la Nation, for the Rosenbergs on death row in the United States (1953), the funeral of the peace-in-Algeria demonstrators, victims of the repression at the Charonne metro station (1962), the social unrest of May 1968, a general assembly of farmers (1983), jazz, performances, film shoots: he is there when history is made. Eyes alert, he captures youth and its wave of protest in the Latin Quarter; André Malraux, utterly spent, taking his leave of the political stage at the last Gaullist demonstration at the Arc de Triomphe (May 30, 1968). The press appreciated him and published him. He began a regular collaboration with magazines L’Express, Science et Vie, Time, Sports Illustrated, Fortune Magazine, and Life. He had many covers: the actress Gina Lollobrigida for Robert Siodmak’s Le Grand Jeu in Match (1954), a former deportee from the extermination camps in a striped shirt for Life (1958), Brigitte Bardot, the global star, for L’Express (1962)… Aragon invited him to follow him on the wanderings of his anthology of poems. Il ne m’est Paris que d’Elsa (1963). Jean Marquis renders the animation of Parisian streets, Montmartre still as it once was, with its windmill. Europe, Iran, Senegal, Algeria: Jean Marquis travels as a special correspondent for the international press. In Lapland (1956), he is at the height of his art in the intimacy of the Sami, at the heart of their traditions and customs. He conveys the wild, tender happiness of children, the carefree spirit of their games, the radiance of their parents’ quiet beauty like a lifeline, a mark of his empathy, of a discreet but powerful ethic at the wellspring of his work. In 2011, Jean Marquis entrusted the distribution of his photographs to the Roger-Viollet agency; some of them came from the collection of the Bibliothèque historique de la Ville de Paris. **The Hulett Collection : Sebastião Salgado – Tirages Vintage**

The Hulett Collection shares a selection of signed vintage prints available from the estate of Sebastião Salgado, among the rarest and most special works we have offered. **Mapping the New Eastman Kodak Film Range**

At the end of August there was suddenly a LOT of new Kodak film packaging flying around Europe! Some of it had the same name as the films we know and love – Gold, UltraMax, Tri-X – and some had names that were recognisable but not as immediately familiar – Ektacolor, Kodacolor, Ektapan. So is this new packaging replacing the old? (No) Are the iconic Portra films about to disappear from shelves? (Also no) And if not – what is going on?? American readers have been living this brave new world for a while now, but with some conflicting information (and misinformation) swirling on Instagram I think it might be fun to summarise what’s happening! And our first step is to go back 150 years in time… The History Kodak – officially known as Eastman Kodak – was founded in America in the late 1880s by George Eastman and Henry Strong. I feel like we often focus on George Eastman, so here’s a photo of Henry instead. Strong in both name and facial hair. Kodak became THE key North American film manufacturer throughout the 20th Century – competing with European companies like Ilford, Ferrania, Foma and the Japanese giant Fujifilm. They also researched, developed and produced iconic cameras like the Brownie and the Instamatic, and their ‘Kodak Moment’ marketing would have spawned a million memes in the Internet age. Flash-forward to the 2000s and the advent of digital photography began to cause financial problems for both Kodak. Unfortunately bankruptcy hit Kodak in 2012 with an insolvency deal that allowed them to continue trading and restructure their debts to avoid all-out collapse. As part of this restructure the distribution of Kodak’s “still film photography” – different from the “moving film photography” a.k.a. Movie a.k.a. Hollywood – was moved to a separate entity called ‘Kodak Alaris’, based in Hertfordshire, England. Why England? Because one of Eastman Kodak’s biggest creditors was the Kodak UK Pension Fund! The expectation was that Kodak Alaris could continue to distribute still film around the world for the remaining years that film existed (!) and their profits would pay off the last of the money owed to the Pension Fund. Cut to 2026 and the forecast demise of film photography has turned out to be happily and totally incorrect. Human nature and the subliminal love of physical objects , the growing distaste for digital tech creeping into our lives (AI YUCK), and a passionate community of small labs, retailers and darkrooms around the world have combined to grow the global film photography market. Now I cannot say exactly what’s changed in the legal relationship between Eastman Kodak and Kodak Alaris – I doubt anyone who isn’t familiar with the documents can – but clearly something has happened. Either a clause has elapsed, there’s been a renegotiation, or a new commercial partnership has been formed. Regardless of the reasoning – EKC have begun to manufacture and distribute still photography once more, alongside the continued relationship with Kodak Alaris. Busting common myths based on internet discussions I’ve been brought into: Does this new film replace the old ones? NO they co-exist I’ve heard Kodak Alaris is a ‘bad’ company and we should support Eastman Kodak as much as possible? Kodak Alaris has spent the past 14 years building, supporting and maintaining a global network of film distributors, retailers, communities that’s allowed film photography to survive – I really can’t fault that. As for ‘which corporate structure is morally ‘better’’…ummmm private equity versus private shareholders…. that feels like a random rabbit hole to go down? #boycottAmazon is probably a better place to start! Are they the same emulsions? It certainly looks like it – but neither company will officially confirm, which is fair enough – and their right. The good news is that more people trying to sell their film should create some healthy competition for everyone interested in shooting film. Frankly EKC launching new and familiar product names in all-new, ‘uniform’ packaging is a really big deal – signalling that EKC’s renewed investment (in more than just 35mm format too) is continuing to give photographers more reasons to keep shooting film. I also hope that it supports their movie film business – which is actually where a lot of the volume comes from. Late 2025 saw KODACOLOR land in the UK and Analogue Wonderland customers were among the first to get their hands on the film in Europe. Then early in 2026 we saw EKTAPAN and EKTACOLOR PRO land with a limited offering. With this in mind, after these initial teases, we would have expected perhaps another top-selling offering coming out from EKC but the next move was to drop a wide choice across multiple emulsions and formats! From the staples of GOLD and ULTRAMAX to the absolute beauties of EKTAR and EKTACHROME and then the monochrome powerhouse of TRI-X – we were spoilt for choice and as we now head towards the autumn months we get to fully embrace all of the speeds to see the year out in true EKC style. Nowadays, more and more companies are selling bulk-loaded/rebranded Kodak Aerocolor 2460 for still photography. One of the first companies to do this was Silberra from St. Peterburg in spring of 2021. They brought out three iterations, Silberra Color 50, 100, and 160. According to them, all films with their own unique colours but in fact, they are all Kodak Aerocolor. This film is (or was) used in the Russian armed forced for aerial photography. It was said the Russian air force was basically the only customer for this specially coated film. Silberra could get their hands on surplus, which they brought to market under their own label in 135 and 120 format. The medium format roll I am going to share five pictures of in my first post here, was branded as Silberra Color 50. It is confectioned by hand reusing backing paper of spools of other manufacturers; being hand-rolled, the films lack the traditional edge markings. The film can be processed in C41 chemistry, but lacks the standard orange/red/brown mask. In recent months, Kodak Aerocolor also can be bought under multiple other labels. If this is because of supply chain problems at Kodak Alaris and Fujifilm, or because of the war that Russia started (and now Eastman Kodak needs other customers for already coated Aerocolor film) is not clear to me.
