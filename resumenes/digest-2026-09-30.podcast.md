**“Photopsia” by Photographer Owen Mar**

A personal series reflecting on the possibility of losing one’s vision by lens-based artist Owen Mar. As an artist Mar attends to elements of daily life that often go unnoticed. His recent photographic work focuses on vision and its unexamined role in shaping our attention. The photos in “Photopsia” were taken during a period of uncertainty regarding Mar’s own eyesight. Mar started to notice unusual flashes of light in his vision one week before he was meant to leave for a whitewater rafting trip in Idaho. His optometrist referred him to a retinal specialist. However, the specialist was only be able to see him after the trip. Contributing to Mar’s concern was the fact that his mother went blind in her left eye when he was a child: “Nervous that my vision had an expiration date, I bought six rolls of film, loaded up my camera, and took as many photographs as I could. Made across this period of uncertainty—from the east coast to Idaho—these images explore reflection, gestures, and shared moments to record the texture of my life before I no longer see it. I am grateful to say now that I remain able to see, but this experience has imparted me with a gratitude for my sight that I’ll never forget.” Owen Mar submitted this project through the submissions section of our website. It’s free to join the Booooooom Community and share your work with us!

I was photographing a state fair looking for feature shots all day with plans to move inside for the evenings concert by the 80’s band RATT. I caught these RATT fans waiting outside the coliseum for the doors to open. The couple sitting by them were at the fair to show their prize pigs. When I spotted the scene I knew not to look directly. A shot like this is like a wild animal, don’t make eye contact! I knew if any of them caught me looking it would change the scene. I moved to a position to frame the scene and knelt down on one knee turned 90 degrees away from them pretending I was changing film. When I had the camera focused about where I thought was right I quickly spun and shot. **Incamerapseudopanoramicwideanglephotomode – more Samsung Digimax L85 fun!**

Dedicated readers will know that I have already written about this remarkable 2006 digicam on 35mmc. On the recent August Bank Holiday weekend I found myself in Dawlish Warren, a popular seaside resort on the south Devon Coast. The L85 was in the back pocket of my cycling shirt so I thought I would make some images of this interesting area. The cycle ride was part of a scoping exercise for a more extended photographic visit in a couple of weeks. I often use the Digimax as a sketch book. It is light and easy to carry when cycling. The Warren, as it is known, is a sand spit at the mouth of the Exe Estuary. It is on the western side of the river mouth; Exmouth, it’s more developed neighbour, is on the eastern side. The sand spit is part fun fair, part seaside resort, part shopping mall, part food hall, part golf course and part nature reserve. The relative importance of these opportunities depends on one’s interests and priorities at the time of visiting. This diversity is more than can be possibly photographed in one brief excursion, but I hope that the following images give a flavour of the Warren. The Camera The Samsung Digimax L85 is an 8mp CCD digital compact camera. It was available in the mid-2000s and has largely disappeared into the dustbin of electronic history. At the time the camera received mixed reviews: some reviewers felt that it was a very good small camera with plenty of functionality; others felt it was a load of rubbish. Most felt that its best feature was the slightly oddball aesthetic. It seems that Samsung’s design department attempted to recreate a vintage rangefinder camera look in plastic. In this piece I would like to offer some insight into the wide picture function. I had not used this mode before so the expanses and chaos of the sand spit seemed well suited to exploring the wider image setting. When I got home from the ride I discovered that Gale Lee’s extremely thorough review of panoramic cameras, and the theory and practice of wideview photography, was the day’s featured article on 35mmc. (Readers can find the link at the foot of this article.) I hope that my humble attempt at panoramic image making will be of interest to readers as well. I know that 35mmc contributors and readers enjoy all topics panoramic! The existence of my piece and Gale’s detailed contribution is purely coincidental! The Wide Angle Mode Samsung’s user manual describes the ‘panoramic’ option as the wide angle mode. It is chosen by rotating the mode dial to the ‘W’ setting. At this point the camera enters auto mode and it is not possible to select aperture or shutter speed. The camera retains basic user choices such as ISO and white balance settings. The camera reduces the image size to 5mp by effectively chopping the top and bottom off the normal image. In the normal image mode the 8mp setting provides a picture with 3264 x 2448 pixels; in the wide angle mode this is reduced to 3264 x 1632. The width of the image is retained, but the height is reduced by 816 pixels. I am fairly sure that 408 pixels are removed from the top and bottom edges of the image. Interestingly, in the wide angle mode, the image ratio becomes 2:1. In Gale Lee’s recent piece this ratio is given as the minimum for an image to be counted as panoramic. I don’t really know whether this feature of the L85 generates genuinely panoramic images. I prefer to think of the function as a pseudo-panoramic mode created in camera using image cropping. The screen shows the cropped image and some of the basic settings. Usefully, the histogram display is retained so that the photographer has a rough guide to the exposure. The camera’s top plate and mode dial In the image above, the W option can be seen on the mode dial of the L85. In this mode, the panoramic function of the camera is used for image making. I prefer to do the best I can with the camera, but a light touch in post-processing is helpful. I converted the images to monochrome to try and recreate that authentic ‘real photography’ look. ### C41 Magazine

**Patterns of Transfiguration: Reading Signs in the Landscape**

In Patterns of Transfiguration, Artur Leão takes the natural landscape as his starting point to explore the way in which we attribute meanings, symbols and presences to our surroundings. The black-and-white images depict nature as more than just a backdrop: rocks, water, trees, animals and bodies come together in a visual system where reality and imagination intertwine. A rock cavity appears as a threshold; a weathered tree trunk evokes the contours of a body; whilst a spider’s web amongst the trees traces a delicate, map-like geometry. In other shots, a close-up of a mushroom conjures up a fossilised creature, and a human figure, bent over in the water, seems on the verge of merging with the surrounding environment. Leão explores precisely this instability: nothing is reduced to what it appears to be. Natural matter continues to suggest other forms and other meanings. The imagery draws as much on mythology as on the history of photography. The cave evokes one of its most ancient archetypes – a womb, a tomb or a passage to another realm – whilst water, rock and trees become elements of an almost animistic vocabulary. A sense of the uncanny emerges – that feeling whereby something familiar suddenly ceases to be so. The same occurs in the portraits, such as that of a dog bathed in an almost supernatural light, which seems to slip away from everyday life to take on the appearance of an apparition. The series is not merely a catalogue of symbols. Its power lies in the way these symbols remain open to interpretation. The photographs do not illustrate a defined mythology, but generate a new one—fragmentary and personal. The eye seeks figures in the rocks, presences in the darkness, signs amongst the branches, following the same impulse that for centuries has led us to project stories onto the constellations or deities onto natural phenomena. In Patterns of Transfiguration, the transformation also involves the viewer. The longer one lingers before the images, the more blurred the distinction becomes between organism and landscape, the real and the imagined, life and decay. Nature does not serve as a backdrop: it is the narrative itself, and we merely skim its surface for a moment.

**How-to: Convert an automatic Polaroid pack film camera to use manual shutter speeds**
Nota editorial (JOYA DEL ARCHIVO / FLASHBACK HISTÓRICO): Este artículo proviene del archivo de EMULSIVE. Conéctalo con cariño como un recuerdo especial en el guion justificando un motivo sugerente (un proceso químico, temática compartida con las noticias de hoy o autor rescatado).

Back in 1960s, automatic exposure was an amazing advance in photography. Polaroid, in its quest to make a really easy-to-use camera, made most of its cameras with only automatic exposure. I mean, who needed anything else? Fast forward to today and it seems automatic exposure has lost its mojo. Complete control over shutter and aperture is the order of the day. What do all these cameras have in common? Automatic exposure! I had in my possession a bunch of automatic cameras, so I got to thinking, “how hard would it be to give these wonderful cameras manual shutter speed capabilities?” I’m not an electronics engineer or anything like that, but I did like to take stuff apart when I was a child….so, I figured that sort of qualified me to dive into this one. This article covers the process of figuring out how to retrofit manual shutter control on a number of Polaroid Land cameras, as well as the creation of a hybrid manual and auto-exposure system. Here’s what I cover: Creating a digital shutter control circuit? Replacing the Polaroid Electric Eye Making a manual shutter speed selector Building the resistor array Adding faster, faster, faster shutter speeds! Using BOTH auto and manual exposure modes This Dongle Changed Everything… The cost The benefits Creating a digital shutter control circuit? I read about a way to do this with a microcontroller and some software, but the whole process seemed a little daunting to me: putting together a digital timing circuit, programming it, then basically tearing out the analog shutter circuit in the Polaroid camera and replacing it with the new digital one. I didn’t really like the way the program operated: keeping the shutter solenoid powered up after cocking the shutter until you released it. Besides, would I be betraying the underlying premise of “analog” photography by converting the shutter to digital? A digital shutter speed conversion Philosophical musings aside, I thought it might be a LOT easier (and way cheaper) to replace the Polaroid “Electric Eye”, which is basically a variable resistor, with either a potentiometer (another variable resistor) or a resistor array. The camera I was most interested in doing this on was being used to take paper negatives (check out this link for more on that) , and it had a piece of black hockey tape over its electric eye, which basically left me with the bulb option as the only shutter speed. The exposure had to be a second or more since it’s hard to try accurately controlling the shutter speed any faster than that with a cable release. Replacing the Polaroid Electric Eye It started simply enough: I took apart the lens body, removed the circuit board, de-soldered the Electric Eye, and attached leads to the terminals it was soldered to. The electric eye removed Leads soldered to the electric eye terminals After reassembling the lens body, I connected a potentiometer (POT) to the leads, and sure enough, turning the dial affected the shutter speed! I quickly discovered however, that the POT didn’t give me the shutter speed range nor the accuracy I needed, so I decided that a resistor array and a 12-point dial switch would work best. 12-point mechanical switches are readily available at the local electronics store, so I got a couple of switches and a package of resistors of varying values and went to work. Making a manual shutter speed selector I started by trying to get the shutter speed down to 1 second (my baseline). This ended up needing a resistor with a value of 190 kilohm. Excellent. Next, since 1/2 second is 1/2 of 1, I thought the resistor for that speed would be 1/2 of 190, or 95 kilohms…but it turned out to be 140 kilohms. This told me there wasn’t a linear relationship between shutter speed and resistance, at least at low shutter speeds, so I made a chart with shutter speeds and resistance values and found a linear relationship (between shutter speed and resistance) started around 1/8 second at 60 kilohms. Since I only needed shutter speeds up to 1/30 (as this was for paper negatives shot at ISO 3), I decided to fill in the values between shutter speeds since I had 12 points on the switch to play with. Trying out different resistors to see how they change the shutter speed I was doing the shutter tests using the open aperture setting on a Polaroid Land Model 450 camera. The camera has two aperture settings and thought I had the cat in the bag until I tested a resistor using the closed aperture setting — the shutter speed was slower. It made sense. The closed aperture setting is 2 stops smaller than the open aperture setting, so the shutter speed should be 2 stops slower — this was sort of the case. But as a result, my project was no longer a simple, fool-proof way of controlling the shutter. The aperture setting would need to be taken into account. Rather than getting discouraged about this wrinkle, I forged ahead and came up with the following chart showing resistor values plus the respective shutter speeds they translated to for each of the two aperture settings: kilohm 194 165 140 120 100 80 60 45 30 22.5 15 Open Aperture (speed/sec) 1 3/4 1/2 3/8 1/4 3/15 1/8 3/30 1/15 3/60 1/30 Closed Aperture (speed/sec ) 2 1.? 1.5 1.25 1 3/4 1/2 3/8 1/4 3/15 1/8 I concluded from this chart that I could get fairly accurate shutter speeds as slow as 1 second. This didn’t bother me because I would use the bulb setting to make any exposures longer than a second. I would have to remember to add 2 stops to the shutter speed when using the closed setting, but having gone this far, I decided this was workable for me! Building the resistor array My first attempt at building an array was a little clumsy. I simply soldered together resistors adding up to the right value to each switch terminal, and stretched them out to one side of the switch. Getting the right value isn’t straightforward since they don’t make resistors with values like 165K, so I had to get out a resistor calculator and combine resistors either in series or parallel (or both) to get the right value. For instance, two 330K resistors in parallel give a value of 165K. Then I slipped heat-shrinkable tubing over each resistor combination and soldered all the ends together. The two leads from the shutter led back to the battery compartment, where they were soldered to the common terminal on the switch and the ends of the resistors. One switch terminal was left empty for “bulb.” My first resistor array build Installing the array into the camera I had to gut the battery compartment to make the switch fit, then drilled a hole into the side of the camera and installed the switch. I also added a switch template with the shutter speeds on it to match the switch terminals. After testing (and retesting), I had confidence that it was going to work. Using this resistor array for paper negatives was so freeing! Now I could use larger apertures with shutter speeds of under a second and control the depth of field instead of being limited to smaller apertures (and exposures over a second) during the day. I also had the ability to choose shutter speeds in between the standard ones, which was a bonus since the Polaroid doesn’t have the ability to set the aperture 1/2 way between stops. Using f/8.8 and a higher shutter speed A shallow depth of field with a paper negative Adding faster, faster, faster shutter speeds! Of course, I couldn’t stop there. I wanted manual shutter control for the other cameras I use with high-speed film. The question was whether the resistance values and shutter speed kept their linear relationship faster than 1/30 second. Since I don’t have the ability to measure shutter speeds above that, I would have to do this with film. Once exposed, I developed the film, and the exposure looked good. I did the same with 930 ohms or 1/500 sec (4x less than 3.75K or 1/125 sec) and again, the exposure on the negative came out fine. I could now make a chart with a full range of shutter speeds from 1 sec to 1/1000 sec to use with high-speed film. But I was not satisfied with how the previous resistor array worked out. It was messy and required a lot of space. After researching it a bit, I found a much more compact method of building the array: with accumulated values. Starting with the lowest value, I could simply build the array using resistors in series, each adding to the value of the previous to give me the value needed for the particular shutter speed. Then I would connect the leads from the electric eye to the common terminal and the resistor coming off the 1/1000 sec terminal. Resistors are soldered from each switch terminal to the next, making it much more compact. The first resistor (470 ohms) is soldered to the first switch terminal and connects to one of the leads. The next resistor (470 ohms) is soldered between terminal 1 and two, and so on. Resisters configured in series with accumulated values Using BOTH auto and manual exposure modes For the next camera I did, I was wondering whether it would be possible to keep the electric eye, retaining the automatic exposure option AND my new manual exposure settings. Doing this would require a third wire from the shutter body and messing with the electric eye itself. Instead of removing it totally, I de-soldered it, bent one of the terminals sideways, and soldered a lead to the bent terminal. Then I reinstalled the electric eye and soldered the unbent pin to the circuit board, along with a lead, and soldered another lead to the other terminal. Then I installed a switch in the battery compartment which switches between using the resistor array and the electric eye. Here’s the wiring diagram and some work-in-progress images: Integrating the Electric Eye – wiring diagram Connecting the Electric Eye Connecting the Electric Eye The array installed into the battery compartment I loved the way this was working out! But I had 2 cameras that I had modified for instant film, which didn’t have room anywhere for a resistor array. It took me a while looking at these cameras trying to figure out where to put it, when I remembered a quote out of the opensx70 project blog in which he said, “… then the dongle changed everything. ” Of course! I could make a dongle and plug it into the camera using a jack that automatically switched it from auto exposure to manual when the jack was plugged in! This Dongle Changed Everything… Starting at the end: ultimately, this is the manual shutter solution I like best because it retains the option to use the electric eye, provides automatic switching between the two, doesn’t need any space inside the battery compartment (or holes drilled), and only requires one resistor array to control all my cameras, though I did make another array to mount on 100-300 series cameras easier. To create it, I soldered the three leads coming from the electric eye and its terminals to a 2.5mm jack I installed on the camera body. The 2 leads coming from the electric eye terminals were soldered to the two leads on the jack, and the third lead coming from the bent electric eye terminal was soldered to the switch terminal on the jack, which is normally closed. Here’s how it looks: A hole drilled for the 2.5mm jack The 2.5mm jack installed Wiring the jack to the circuit board How does it work? When there is no plug in the jack, the electric eye is connected to the shutter circuit as normal and provides auto-exposure. When a jack is plugged in, the electric eye is disconnected and the resistor array (my manual exposure selector) is used instead. This is explained in more detail in this guide I wrote on shooting paper negatives on Polaroid pack film cameras . The manual shutter speed is affected a full 2-stops by the scene selection switch when set to the “bright sun only” setting. You can find complete instructions and files to do this on my website . The cost The parts are cheap — around $10 for the 12-point switch, 11 resistors, a 2.5mm jack and a 2.5mm plug. Of course, you will need a bit of wire and some solder, along with a soldering iron. You could probably make a dongle out of book board if you don’t have a 3D printer, and you may age a bit under all the stress of modifying your camera, but it’s probably worth it! The benefits Simple-ish: A camera that has manual shutter capabilities sort of like the sought-after Polaroids 180 and 195! No longer do I have to trust what the Electric Eye tells the camera. Any errors in exposure are my fault and mine alone!
