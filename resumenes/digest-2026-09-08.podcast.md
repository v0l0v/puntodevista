**Creators in Focus: Filmmaker Justin Kaminuma on Embracing Experimentation and Creative Instincts**
Fotógrafos: francinegaebriele

Our Magazine series, Creators in Focus, highlights experimental minds around the world who are pushing boundaries, and finding new ways to live life more analogue. For Japanese-American filmmaker Justin Kaminuma, the creative process is anything but linear. Rather than following a strict formula, he believes in keeping an open approach – experimenting with different mediums, embracing every source of inspiration, and trusting his creative instincts to bring everything together. In this article, we're discovering the stories behind Justin’s work and the artistic philosophies that influence the way he creates. Credits: Scenes from I Still Exist (Directed by Justin Kaminuma) We’re happy to have you in the Magazine, Justin! Please share a brief introduction about yourself and your creative work to our readers. Hi, thank you for having me. I’m a filmmaker and visual artist who likes to express stories and feelings through all kinds of media and platforms. I love to bridge music and film in unique ways, and love experimenting with form and structure to find interesting ways of communicating what I’m trying to say. What first sparked your interest in filmmaking? Tell us how your journey with creativity began. I like to think that my interest in the medium itself stemmed from all the hours I spent watching music videos on MTV2 whenever I got grounded. I was never well-versed in films, but I could recall a classic music video, and that eventually translated into an interest in filmmaking for me. I think everything I make is fundamentally about re-creating those feelings for others, whether it’s in my films, music videos, or anything else I make, regardless of the media I’ve taken in along the way. Video 1 Credits: The Last Thing I Think I Saw (Directed by Justin Kaminuma) Your work often comes from a very personal place – inspired by your own memories, experiences, and thoughts. What’s it like to transform something intimate into a piece of art and share it with others? It’s always scary, but what I realized after years of making art is: anything that you fear or find scary, or too intimate or too exposing, is never like that at all for the audience. Art allows for this invisible barrier to be created between you and the viewer that protects you, and that’s an incredible thing – to be able to communicate something that is screaming inside of you to a complete stranger, and for them to understand without ever needing to truly know you. “Art allows for this invisible barrier to be created between you and the viewer that protects you, and that’s an incredible thing – to be able to communicate something that is screaming inside of you to a complete stranger, and for them to understand without ever needing to truly know you.” — Justin Kaminuma Where do you typically find inspiration, and how do you recognize when an idea is worth pursuing? I tend to find inspiration from quite arbitrary experiences and places. I can’t pretend that I’m making purely artful references to Kurosawa or Godard in my work, because in reality I’m pulling as many ideas from something I’ve seen scrolling TikTok, or a David Gray music video, or a Marvel movie. And I try as much as I can to lean into that, and avoid letting ostentation and insecurity cloud my instincts. I try to work on as many things as I can, because my ideas tend to be a collage of all those things coming together eventually. I usually know when it feels right. Credits: Scenes from Are You Watching and letter to a person who no longer exists (Directed by Justin Kaminuma) Could you briefly walk us through your typical creative process – from gathering materials to the final stages of editing? My creative process is very messy. I try to keep everything as open as possible, regardless of what I’m working on. Sometimes there’s a script, but even the script can be shaped around a visual I made and scrapped a year prior. There’s an almost magical moment in the process where something just clicks, and all these disparate things just pull together in a beautiful way. That’s what I’m always working towards. It always comes, I just have to keep chipping away and making things until I can get there. Looking back at your body of work, is there a particular project that feels especially meaningful to you? We'd love to hear the story behind it! “I Still Exist.” felt like a culmination of everything I was working towards. I hadn’t made a personal narrative film in over a year prior to that, so it came together so quickly and urgently. I was going through a period of turmoil and uncertainty when I made that short film, and to see it connect with so many people has given me a lot of purpose in continuing to create. Video 2 Credits: I Still Exist (Directed by Justin Kaminuma) You've created a series of song visualizers that consistently carry your distinct visual style. Could you tell us how you translate a song's atmosphere into imagery? I try not to overthink those. When it comes to visualizing music, I dislike anything that is overly dense in subtext and references. I always think of music as this visceral, almost primal art form, so I try to focus as much on the feeling a song evokes, the immediate imagery or colors that come to mind, and to translate that on screen. You’ve also directed music videos for artists like The Cure, Yungblud, Vance Joy, and more. What excites you most about working with musicians, and how does the process differ from creating your personal films? Well, I love music, and I love applying what I do to work with people who have incredible things to say, and beautiful ways of saying them. It’s a different way of creating art, something way more collaborative and mission-focused. I learned to love that part of making art. I enjoy making people happy. And in many ways, those collaborations have helped sharpen ideas in my own work, so I’ve never seen the commercial work I’ve made as a separate part of my art at all. **“See the Elephant” by Photographer Shawne Brown**

A series documenting the margins of the American Bible Belt by Nashville-based photographer Shawne Brown. Brown was born near Kingsport in northeast Tennessee and studied photography at East Tennessee State University. His work addresses the cultural and physical landscape of his home state, its connections to regional and national history, and resistance to the rapid changes in the area. From his family home to the Kansas heartland where his wife was born, “See the Elephant” is an attempt to come to terms with the place in which he grew up. The title is based on an expression used during the Civil War by soldiers whose heroic ideals of warfare were destroyed by the actual carnage of battle: “The phrase has since come to be used as shorthand for gaining knowledge through experience at the high cost of disillusionment. Disillusionment, however, can be recognized as a gift that allows one the possibility of seeing more clearly, even somewhere as complicated as the place you’re from.” Shawne Brown participated in our 2026 Book Award open call and made our shortlist.

**MAP Toulouse 2026, 16th of Its Name**

Desde su fundación, el festival de fotografía MAP Toulouse se ha desarrollado a contracorriente de un modelo confinado a museos y galerías. Su principio rector es diseminar la fotografía por toda la ciudad, aprovechando tanto los espacios públicos disponibles como los edificios patrimoniales o sitios que se enfrentan a una transformación inminente, o incluso a la demolición. A lo largo de sus ediciones, este enfoque ha entrelazado imágenes, arquitectura, la historia de los lugares y los relatos de un territorio. Durante dieciséis ediciones, MAP ha defendido una fotografía libre y accesible, presentada en entornos capaces de transformar la manera en que se perciben las obras. La Cartoucherie, el Espace Croix-Baragnon, el centro de clasificación de correo de Matabiau, la Salle des Illustres (Capitole), los museos Paul-Dupuy y Georges-Labit, y el Dôme de la Grave han servido, en distintos momentos, como escenarios para esta programación. El festival ha ampliado gradualmente su territorio, pasando de los espacios públicos y las exposiciones al aire libre a sitios patrimoniales, museos y edificios destinados a cambiar de función. Esta forma de ocupar temporalmente los emplazamientos de Toulouse ha llevado a que MAP sea descrito en ocasiones como un «urbanista de transición». La expresión remite a un método: identificar lugares emblemáticos, poco conocidos o en proceso de cambio, para luego hacerlos accesibles a través del evento. El festival opera, así, en espacios cuyo estatus aún no se ha estabilizado. En 2024, el festival MAP se convirtió en bienal. La edición de 2026 continúa en esta dirección al ocupar la Caserne Jacques Vion, un antiguo parque de bomberos utilizado hasta 2025 y ahora en fase de «transición». Diseñado por el arquitecto Pierre Debeaux, el cuartel posee una identidad arquitectónica totalmente original. Ostenta la distinción de «Arquitectura Contemporánea Notable», previa a su clasificación como Monumento Histórico en 2023. Su estado actual lo sitúa, una vez más, en un estado intermedio; tras la función que desempeñó durante tanto tiempo, perdura la memoria del lugar, mientras comienza a perfilarse su futura apertura a los residentes. Es en este intervalo donde MAP sitúa su programación de 2026. La elección del lugar sigue una lógica que el festival ya ha puesto a prueba: intervenir antes de que un edificio o un barrio entre en una nueva fase, y convertir ese periodo de transición en un contexto expositivo. Durante esta edición, el cuartel se convierte en un espacio de circulación entre la fotografía, la arquitectura y la memoria urbana. Esta orientación, sin embargo, no es solo una cuestión de sedes. MAP apuesta por una programación que abarca desde la fotografía documental hasta propuestas de artes visuales, desde narrativas íntimas hasta perspectivas sobre el mundo, sin descartar los enfoques históricos. Fotógrafos jóvenes y artistas consagrados se reúnen con el objetivo declarado de permitir la coexistencia de diferentes estilos y generaciones. Para los visitantes —aficionados, curiosos, recién llegados o conocedores del medio—, la fotografía se aborda como un lenguaje con múltiples códigos. De una edición a otra, el festival construye así su identidad en torno a una doble movilidad: la de las imágenes, extraídas de sus marcos habituales, y la de los lugares, a menudo capturados en el momento en que su uso está cambiando. En 2026, la Caserne Vion ofrece un nuevo terreno para este experimento. Atrapado entre el reconocimiento patrimonial y un plan de reapertura, el edificio concentra los interrogantes que han interesado a MAP desde sus inicios: cómo mirar un territorio mientras cambia y qué puede producir la fotografía cuando se convierte en parte de esa transformación, aunque sea de forma temporal. Dos exposiciones tendrán lugar fuera de la sede principal, en la Galerie Le Château d’eau, prácticamente al lado de la Caserne Vion: el Premio de la Fotografía Social, con sus tres galardonados, junto con PODA, una organización que distribuye fotografías enmarcadas de autores reconocidos a precios reducidos, en formato 9x12 cm. Cabe destacar: En un gesto conmovedor y oportuno, se dedica un espacio a Philippe-Gérard Dupuy, fotógrafo, galerista, profesor y editor de Toulouse fallecido el año pasado. Fundador de la galería fotográfica Voir, profesor en la ETPA y creador de espíritu libre. Autor de imágenes poco convencionales y mentor, Gérard, como le llamábamos, inculcó en innumerables principiantes (incluyéndome a mí) el gusto por cultivar su propia singularidad. **Patrick Remy Studio : Hans Neumann : Still Moving**

Publicado por Patrick Remy Studio, Still Moving es la primera monografía individual de Hans Neumann. Desarrollado a lo largo de varios años, el proyecto explora la memoria, el lugar y el sentido de pertenencia. Más de veinte años después de abandonar Perú, Hans Neumann regresó a Lima; no para documentar la ciudad tal como es hoy, sino para reconstruir el paisaje emocional de los años previos a su partida. Trabajando con una nueva generación de jóvenes limeños, Still Moving revisita la adolescencia a través de retratos, paisajes, interiores y escenas cuidadosamente escenificadas que habitan un espacio entre la observación y la invención. En lugar de recrear el pasado con fidelidad, las fotografías exploran cómo la memoria transforma los lugares con el paso del tiempo y cómo la pertenencia sigue evolucionando mucho después de haber dejado el hogar. Ni documental ni ficción, Still Moving plantea una pregunta sencilla: ¿Qué queda de un lugar una vez que nos hemos ido? **Les Douches la Galerie : Robert Frank – Mabou / John Cohen – Pull My Daisy**

Les Douches la Galerie presenta Robert Frank – Mabou / John Cohen – Pull My Daisy, una exposición que reúne dos cuerpos de obra centrados en Robert Frank, correspondientes a dos momentos muy distintos de su vida y su trabajo. Por un lado, las copias de la serie Mabou, realizadas por Sid Kaplan a partir de fotografías tomadas por Robert Frank entre finales de los años noventa y la década de los dos mil, revelan una obra profundamente íntima, nacida en el refugio de Nueva Escocia, donde el artista se estableció a principios de los años setenta. Por otro lado, las imágenes capturadas por John Cohen en el rodaje de Pull My Daisy (1959) dan testimonio de la efervescencia de la Generación Beat y de los inicios de Robert Frank en el cine. Amigo y vecino de Frank en el Nueva York de finales de los cincuenta, Cohen compartía con él una profunda afinidad artística. Fue el propio Frank quien le pidió que fotografiara la producción de la película, cuyo guion fue escrito por Jack Kerouac y que reunió, entre otros, a Allen Ginsberg, Gregory Corso, Peter Orlovsky, Alfred Leslie, Larry Rivers y David Amram. Un registro excepcional de Robert Frank en el corazón de esta comunidad de artistas y poetas. Con casi medio siglo de diferencia, estos dos cuerpos de obra dialogan entre sí y trazan, en negativo, la trayectoria de un fotógrafo y cineasta que nunca dejó de experimentar y de ampliar los límites de su trabajo: desde la energía colectiva de Nueva York hasta el mundo más solitario de Mabou, donde el paisaje, el tiempo y la memoria se convierten en la sustancia misma de sus imágenes. Esta exposición narra una historia de amistad y, más allá del círculo de allegados de Robert Frank, aborda el proceso creativo. En la primavera de 1958, el libro de fotografías de Frank, fruto de su viaje por Estados Unidos gracias al apoyo de la Fundación Guggenheim, fue enviado a la imprenta para ser publicado ese otoño por Delpire bajo el título Les Américains. Durante el verano siguiente, se dedicó a tres proyectos creativos. Recorrió solo los autobuses de Nueva York, fotografiando a través de la ventanilla; pasó la noche del 4 de julio en Coney Island, de nuevo en solitario, retratando a personas que dormían, sobre todo en la playa; y experimentó filmando a su familia y a un grupo de amigos en una playa de Cape Cod con una cámara de 16 mm. Al final del verano, descubrió que la práctica solitaria de la fotografía ya no satisfacía su necesidad de creación. Consideraba que las fotografías, incluso en grupos o series, eran demasiado mudas; les faltaban las palabras, el sonido y el movimiento. En aquella época, Frank vivía en el East Village, en un barrio de escritores, músicos y artistas de toda índole. Junto a sus amigos y vecinos, especialmente Alfred Leslie, Allen Ginsberg y Jack Kerouac, unieron sus talentos para realizar la película Pull My Daisy, adaptada de una obra de teatro de Kerouac. John Cohen, amigo y vecino de Frank, se había labrado un nombre como figura destacada del American Folk Music Revival a través de la recopilación y difusión de grabaciones de músicos veteranos, y en particular con su grupo, The New Lost City Ramblers. Más allá de su talento como músico, etnomusicólogo y cineasta, Cohen era también un sagaz conocedor de los textiles peruanos y un consumado fotógrafo. Frank había diseñado el arte de las portadas de los álbumes de los Ramblers; por lo tanto, era natural que le pidiera a Cohen ser el fotógrafo de rodaje de la producción de Pull My Daisy. Cohen era alguien de dentro. Mucho más que simples fotos fijas de rodaje, se trata de retratos íntimos e inéditos de colaboradores como Gregory Corso, Peter Orlovsky y Larry Rivers, sin olvidar a Leslie, Ginsberg y Kerouac. Aquí fotografió a la actriz francesa Delphine Seyrig en su primera aparición cinematográfica. Una de las fotografías muestra también a Mary, la esposa de Frank en aquel momento, y a su hijo Pablo. Todas estas imágenes fueron tomadas durante el rodaje de esta obra emblemática de la improvisación que llegaría a definir el New American Cinema. Pull My Daisy presenta muchas de las características de este movimiento cinematográfico: escenarios naturales, cámara en mano, actores no profesionales y financiación independiente. También ofrece un registro de su original método para promocionar la película. Alquilaron un coche de caballos en Central Park, colocaron carteles hechos a mano y recorrieron la ciudad repartiendo margaritas a los transeúntes. A diferencia de The Americans, que fue un fracaso crítico y comercial en su lanzamiento, Pull My Daisy recibió elogios generalizados, aunque no alcanzó un verdadero éxito comercial. Pero fue un estímulo suficiente para impulsar la ambición de Frank de hacer cine, hasta el punto de que, durante la década siguiente, dedicó su energía creativa exclusivamente a la cinematografía. En 1969, la vida de Frank sufrió un gran vuelco cuando dejó a su esposa, Mary. Al año siguiente, junto a la artista June Leaf, compró una casa y un terreno frente al mar en Mabou, en la costa norte de Cape Breton, Nueva Escocia. Frank y Leaf pasaron allí varios meses cada año, según las estaciones, durante el resto de sus vidas. Leaf valoraba la soledad de su nuevo entorno, mientras que Frank anhelaba la colaboración cinematográfica, difícil de conseguir desde Mabou. Al no disponer de cuarto oscuro, adquirió una cámara Polaroid de positivo/negativo. En 1976, ya trabajaba con las pequeñas copias instantáneas que producía la cámara, las cuales le proporcionaban simultáneamente negativos que más tarde podía ampliar en Nueva York. Frank regresó a la fotografía, esa actividad tan solitaria, pero a diferencia de antes, ahora le añadía palabras: escribiendo sobre la superficie, incorporándolas a la imagen o rayándolas en el negativo. También combinaba dos o más imágenes, por ejemplo, ampliando tiras de película, una práctica con la que había experimentado en los años sesenta como una extensión de su trabajo cinematográfico. Frank gestaba sus ideas para las imágenes en Mabou, pero solo las finalizaba verdaderamente tras trabajar en el cuarto oscuro con Sid Kaplan, su positivador y amigo de toda la vida. Frank llegaba con los negativos y, a veces, con un boceto para mostrar a Kaplan cómo combinarlos en una hoja de papel fotográfico. Sugería cambios y, en ocasiones, intervenía las copias, los negativos o ambos, más tarde en su estudio de Nueva York en Bleecker Street, antes de regresar al cuarto oscuro para continuar el positivado en colaboración con Kaplan. El carácter introspectivo de estas fotografías refleja mucho más que el aislamiento de Mabou, especialmente en invierno; habla de la madurez del artista y de sus reflexiones sobre el tiempo y la mortalidad. La hija de Frank murió en un accidente de avión en 1974 a los 20 años, y su hijo Pablo, que padecía una enfermedad mental, falleció en 1994. Frank reorganizó objetos encontrados en su propiedad, como piedras y madera, para construir monumentos a la memoria de sus hijos, monumentos que a veces aparecen en sus fotografías tardías. Aquí, a pesar del desolado paisaje invernal visible a través de la ventana, se puede distinguir este recordatorio: «Be Happy». Frank comenzó a trabajar con el maestro positivador y fotógrafo Kaplan en 1968. A lo largo de los años, Frank expresó su gratitud y rindió un homenaje especial a su amigo regalándole copias firmadas y dedicadas. A finales de los noventa, Cohen también encargó copias a Kaplan, pero las fotografías que aquí se presentan son copias de época (vintage) realizadas por el propio Cohen, procedentes directamente del legado del artista. Esta exposición destaca no solo por la rareza y calidad de estas copias de época de impecable procedencia, ni solo por las personales y conmovedoras dedicatorias, sino también por la historia de amistad que relata. **Voigtlander Vitomatic IIa**

This Voigtlander Vitomatic IIa, which I recently acquired, has some interesting features. It is what I think of as a typical Voigtlander – compact, very heavy for its size and with a set of controls that make good sense when you get used to them. This model has a built-in meter (no battery needed) and a feature I have not see before, an adapter to convert it into an incident light meter. This is a tiny piece of plastic that, miraculously (?) had survived held into the inside of the ever-ready case by a piece of elastic. It is a match needle system on the top of the camera. Fairly standard, but the tweak is that it is possible, if one’s eye is in the right position, if there isn’t a finger in the wrong place, and if the light is OK, to see a reflection in the viewfinder, so exposure can be adjusted when composing the image. Exposure is set by adjusting the aperture ring, which is coupled to the meter. Then, rather than set the shutter speed independently, you turn the rear knurled ring, with arrows pointing to shutter speed and aperture until you have the combination of aperture and speed that you want. Once the muscle memory is developed, and you turn the ring you think you are turning, it is a neat arrangement. Focus, of course, is the front ring. Inserting and film is again what I think of as standard Voigtlander with a double door to make inserting the cassette easy. Having loaded the film, you turn frame counter to F (for 36 exposures) and then wind till it shows 36, when you are ready for the first shot. A warning here. I lost 2 or 3 frames because I did not realise that if the rewind mechanism is out, as shown below, the shutter will not cock, and you will not know you are in a position to shoot. Why such a silly mistake when it seems obvious that this needs to be pushed down (the little catch on the left releases it)? I wanted to check that the film was actually winding on, and thought this would be more obvious if I left it out. One quirk of the Voigtlander Vitomatic IIa is that, to avoid accidents, you cannot fire the shutter or wind on unless there is a film in it, which makes testing a potential purchase difficult. What of the results? I have not done any systematic test, though I tried to use a good range of shutter speeds and there were no obvious issues with the density of the negatives. I haven’t tried printing them (as in darkroom) which would be the real test, but scanned them using a Sony A7R3 and a macro lens, now my preferred option, which will have compensated for any over/under exposure. The film was Kentmere 100, developed in ID-11 at 1+3 dilution. It may have been a tad over-developed because it was too hot to keep the tank at a constant 20 degrees. I tried to develop at room temperature and adjusted the time accordingly, but I think the temperature still varied a little during development. The grain bothered me on some images, so I have applied noise reduction in ON1 to some, though not all the images. First, some seaside images in South Wales. Next, a picture of a band playing at a local farmers’ market. And finally, a couple of flea fair shots. Overall the Voigtlander Vitomatic IIa a very usable camera – more usable than the Vito B that I inherited – because of the much brighter viewfinder and the built-in metering. These shots don’t show this, but I decided the rangefinder was reasonably accurate, though the patch that needs to be aligned is a bit small. It will be more than the “single use” camera that some “bargains” turn out to be. The limitation of the roll to me was the Kentmere 100, and my seeming inability to keep the horizon level. **Reap the Earth, Feed Kyoto**

Reap the Earth, Feed Kyoto — Blind Magazine Skip to content Facebook-f Instagram Search IN IMAGES Reap the Earth, Feed Kyoto Kyoto Farm to Table traces the living roots of Japan’s old capital, where the farmer’s gesture meets the plate with an almost sacred rigor. By Guénola Pellen. Photos by Viola Gaskell. This book springs from the meeting of two complementary voices. Viola Gaskell, an American journalist and photographer from Hawaii, and Momoko Nakamura, a Japanese food writer, joined forces to interview more than thirty producers, chefs, fishermen, and tofu makers across Kyoto. The city sits atop a singular vegetable heritage, the kyō-yasai, grown for over a thousand years in rich soil fed by exceptionally pure water. The round, plump kamonasu eggplant, the slender kujō-negi leek, and the mild shōgoin-daikon radish rank among the varieties most coveted by high-end kitchens. This land nurtures shizen shoku, farming free of synthetic inputs, where soil regenerates through compost and crop rotation rather than chemical fertilizer. That philosophy feeds in turn into washoku, the Japanese culinary tradition inscribed by UNESCO on the Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2013. Each figure met in this book found their own path toward that philosophy. For some, time abroad was the spark; for others, the Fukushima disaster struck like a warning bell, a call to reconsider the bond between land and body. Gaskell’s eye had already been sharpened elsewhere, on organic farming in China and on Hong Kong’s vanishing cha chaan tengs, Cantonese diners where Western influence meets local flavor. Kyoto offers her a new terrain of rare intimacy. **Beni Bischoff Psychobuch 2**

Beni Bischoff Psychobuch 2 Posted on September 7, 2026 September 7, 2026 by Brad Feuerhelm To you, Beni Bischof may be a monster. To others, he is an unofficial unsyndicated scribe for the madness of the last half-century of consumer hell that we wade through. They drew first Blood; it wasn’t me. To give context to Bishof’s sprawling and prolific practice from the outside would be an exercise in deep-fried futility. To observe it from within the spray-painted raclette and wurst-strewn Disney princess madness inside the walls of his distorted memory castle is to self-indict oneself as crazy as a shithouse rat , right alongside him. I’m not a convert; I’m clergy, here. Speak softly and carry a battle axe; speak battle and softly carry an axe; speak axe and softly carry the battle…or something. For the purposes of this review, I will be focusing on why the ritually unaccustomed stand little to no chance of infiltrating the world of pop-culture He-Man celebrity backwash that resides at the bottom of the Monster Energy drink upturned in the cultural polyglot’s studio. Of course, without acknowledging a history of absurdity in the context of ur-cult appropriation, jammed up the ass of Donald Duck, Richard Prince, the Internet, eBay, Tintin, Kippenberger, Dieter Roth, Paul McCarthy, and Rambo, it will be difficult to lead a donkey or dead hare to the rat-infested waters from which it is suggested it should drink the Kool-Aid. Slippery metaphors, run-ons, and inebriated slang are currency, not metaphors. What I get most from Bischof is a profound sense of unease in how he obsesses and fixates on culture and has found a prolific way in which to down-cycle objects such as magazines, tech company letterhead, and the apocalypse into a convincing YouTube mash-up filled with joke paintings, similarly culled from the provinces of Richard Prince’s Reich, a dominion less indebted to ideology than the minute examination of cultural prophylactics, all LOWERCASE, no lubnrication. What Bischof excels at is marauding through the canopy of the capitalist gangbang to find recognizable artifacts, once culled, which he then subverts through his playful intervention. In doing so, he reclaims the right to administer meaning to the debris through that which is often recognizable, like an inside joke, but just hermetic enough to understand that the language he is speaking is somewhat specific to his generation (and my own). Though he opts to include more recent developments in the post-9/11 world, he insists on digging up the corpses of our childhood memories to abuse and put context around the world we have voyaged since. It is seriously unserious, but to mistake his work for absurdity by itself is to dis-communicate the drive at its base. I might argue that his sometimes cynical arguments are as much a sign of the times and a wary warning of the end times, when the symbols shatter and what is seen as innocent or banal quickly becomes a harbinger of overconsumption, political agony, and disruption. He is the Cabbage Patch Kid, kidnapped, fed sugar, and kept in a dark basement (sorry, that’s a really Austrian thing of me to say) until he can break through his Snickers chrysalis and morph into the Garbage Pail Kid he was always meant to be, to shine in the unholy and uneven light of our last days. Bischof is the blotter acid messiah of maximalism. He exhibits a production-line sincerity focused on all manner of media, from sculpture to painting to the dead-eyed dream carousel of installations, culled from the carnival of comical souls collected from the ether and regurgitated on canvas, penny rides, and paper dreams. His work is what one might expect to find from an artist in Nevil Shute’s On the Beach , half-painting his way into and out of psychosis, locked in an eternal battle with an invisible and radiating sky-enveloping cloud of driftwood, eschewing sanity for documenting, in his own way, an inevitable end to the human colony. I mention On the Beach in particular as I could not think of a better literary adaptation of Bischof’s activities. I think about how tirelessly he must work to assemble this countercultural trove of material. His output is incredibly vast, and some of that must be down to the simple act of gesture he implies while making the work. Whereas some of his objects and digital re-imaginings of photographic appropriation must take a bit of time to produce, he also sees the value of smaller gestures. I often think that artists, paralyzed by gesture and paintstroke, must suffer uncontrollably at the weight of their output. Whereas that does not mean Bischof is unserious about his work, it also suggests that he is able to make works by reacting to what their initial form offers, and that this reaction is not always overkill. This keeps his production loose, his ideas expanding, and he does so in a manner that is entirely convincing. Psychobuch 2 follows his opus, Psychobuch , published in 2014 by the same great publisher, Edition Patrick Frey, and, like that volume, showcases the sheer amount of work the visual artist has been producing. As he works in zines and text, as well as artist books, there have been smaller offerings in between. I encourage people to check out his volumes of text-related books, also published by EPF, as I think they are a bit of a sleeper in his oeuvre. Psychobuch 2 is an essential continuation of the out-of-print volume one, and it shows Bischof’s progress, though one is left wondering about the gap between the volumes, given his consistency. The topics remain similar, but I think there is a deeper emphasis on painting over his previous focus on the photographic side of his practice, though Metacars, Rambo fetish, and tabloid appropriations do make an appearance. What I enjoyed seeing were his company letterhead drawings (?) from Google and other film and tech companies, which merge to create, even if as a secondary dimension, a discussion about the times we find ourselves in, battling against the machine, the surveillance state, and the rampant destruction of all life on the planet. Everything suggests a cavalier correspondence mimicked for the purposes of humor while keeping with contemporary topics, as it cannot be all Playboy caricature and He-Man inside jokes. Whether he intends it or not, he is a great caricaturist, an analog Balzac, a commentator on the contemporary times we wade through. Though humor is the conduit to his work, at its base, I think it’s much more serious than perhaps he would even acknowledge. Of course, it is with my highest recommendation that artbook-interested parties pick up a copy of this book sooner rather than later, as it will inevitably sell out and become expensive or hard to find on the secondary market. I certainly look forward to a third and fourth volume over the next decades. I think of Bischof as a rather unique voice in contemporary art, an artist who understands and has grown through the analog world of magazines, newspapers and television as it used to be, and has since, like myself, been asked to wade through the onslaught of cultural garbage associated in one part wonder, two parts disdain, the ignorance of the Internet age and is coping, as I am, with the cacophony of voices seeking our attention. In reference, I am reminded of Richard Prince’s playfulness, particularly his use of film stills and lobby cards in his work, as well as his appropriation of existing material for repurposing, such as pulp covers. There is a reminder of Hans-Peter Feldman’s use of everday advertising in his collages, particularly weekly grocery catalogs, and a general function of Roth and Kippenberger’s uses of common objects and materials repurposed from byproducts to high art. There are others, but these are the cohort that spring to mind with Bischof, though I suspect there is a deeper well of strange Swiss art from which his lineage derives.
