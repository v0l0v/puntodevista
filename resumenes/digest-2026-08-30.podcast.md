**Between Life and Art, Between Photography and Love: Nur Aishah Kenton's Analogue Photography**
Fotógrafos: kopanskarchive

Nur Aishah Kenton has a gift for translating intimate connections directly onto film. By weaving her photographs into series, she creates an inner dynamic and poetry that elevates the personal into the universal. In this interview, she shares her conceptual and practical process while reflecting on the role of analogue photography today. © Nur Aishah Kenton ,  From the series To Whom It May Concern Hi, Aishah, and welcome to Lomography Magazine. Could you please introduce yourself to our Community and say a few words about your relationship with analogue photography? Hello! My name is Nur Aishah Kenton. I am an artist and photographer based in Naarm/Melbourne, Australia. My practice explores how photography, particularly analogue photography, can be used to investigate the world around me and the relationships that shape it. I often work collaboratively, using photography as a means of connection, dialogue, and shared authorship. In your portfolio, you describe your practice as ‘between life and art, between photography and love’. Those intimate, close moments captured on film are especially visible in your series, Orchid and To Whom It May Concern. Please say a few words about these projects and your work with film photography in this context. Photography has always come from a deeply personal place for me. I don't see a distinction between my life and my art, my practice is shaped by my relationships and lived experiences, and much of what motivates me comes directly from the world around me. To Whom It May Concern was my first major body of work, and my graduating series from university. Through a series of photographs and three artist books, it explored my relationship with my partner. Looking back, I can see how pivotal the project was in helping me find my voice as an artist. It introduced ideas that continue to shape my practice today, particularly an interest in bringing intimate, private experiences into the public realm through photography. © Nur Aishah Kenton ,  From the series Orchid Nearly eight years later, Orchid revisits many of those same questions, but through a different relationship. Instead of focusing on my partner, the work centers on my mother. Photography becomes both a therapeutic and collaborative process, one that allows us to reconnect, reflect on our shared history, and navigate the complexities of family, memory, and identity together. While the subject has shifted, the underlying motivations remain the same: using photography to better understand the people closest to me and the relationships that have shaped who I am. Why do you consistently choose analogue over digital, and how does it influence your storytelling? I began my artistic practice working with analogue photography, and I'm incredibly grateful that it became the foundation of how I make images. It continues to shape not only my process, but also the way I think about photography and collaboration. “I think there's something about the materiality and slower pace of analogue photography that continues to resonate, especially in a time when we're constantly surrounded by digital images.” Returning to analogue photography time and again encourages me to be fully present with the people I photograph. Without the ability to immediately review an image on the back of a camera, my attention remains on the person in front of me rather than the photograph itself. That slower, more intentional process fosters trust, conversation, and genuine connection. Many of the topics you work with seem to stem from your close environment and personal experiences. How do you find your inspiration, and what does your photographic process look like? A lot of my inspiration comes from photography itself, but just as much of it comes from being in the world. Outside of my practice, I work as an arts educator and arts worker, and I feel incredibly fortunate that these roles allow me to spend time with people from many different backgrounds and communities. Those conversations, encounters, and relationships continually shape the way I think about photography and often become the starting point for new work. © Nur Aishah Kenton ,  From the series Orchid I'm also an avid reader, particularly of non-fiction and memoir. I'm drawn to the ways people tell stories about their lives, and I often find myself inspired by the intimacy, vulnerability, and complexity that memoirs can hold. My photographic process almost always begins with listening and with a sense of enquiry. Often it emerges from something I've experienced personally or a question that I can't quite let go of. Rather than beginning with a fully formed concept or carefully planning a project, I usually start by photographing. Making photographs is how I think through an idea. It is only afterwards when I'm editing, sequencing, and spending time with the images that I begin to understand what I have been drawn to. In many ways, the photographs lead me to the work, rather than the other way around. On the technical side, what methods and tools do you usually use in film photography, and are they accessible to beginners? I have dyscalculia, which affects the way I engage with numbers and mathematics, so I've naturally developed a photographic process that isn't overly technical. Instead, I try to keep my workflow as simple and intuitive as possible, allowing me to focus on the relationships I'm building and the photographs I'm making rather than the equipment itself. “I don't see a distinction between my life and my art...and much of what motivates me comes directly from the world around me.” When I first started photographing, I was eager to experiment with as many film cameras as I could. While that period of exploration was valuable, I eventually realized that constantly changing cameras wasn't necessarily helping my practice. These days, I work primarily with just two film cameras, and I know them well. Having a simple, familiar kit allows me to concentrate on the people I'm photographing rather than the mechanics of the camera. © Nur Aishah Kenton ,  From the series Orchid I also develop a large part of my process after making the photographs. I scan my own negatives, which has become a therapeutic part of my practice. I enjoy the slower pace of sitting with each image as it emerges, and scanning my own film also gives me greater control over the final look of my photographs. For me, film photography isn't about having the most technical setup or the newest equipment. It's about finding a process that feels sustainable, intentional, and allows you to stay connected to the photographs you're making. What advice would you give to someone who wants to start shooting film and use it as a means of artistic expression? My biggest piece of advice is simply to photograph. If you have an idea, don't wait until it feels fully formed, start by making photographs. Beginning is often the hardest part, but once you've taken that first image, the process has a way of unfolding naturally. I also can't stress enough how important it is to surround yourself with people who inspire you and who you trust to look at your work. Photography can sometimes feel like an isolating medium, but it doesn't have to be. Some of the most meaningful moments in my practice have come through conversations with friends, mentors, and collaborators who have challenged and encouraged me. Don't be afraid to reach out to artists whose work you admire, share your photographs with others, and collaborate with friends. As a practicing analogue photographer from the younger generation, what have you observed about the way film photography is currently used and celebrated? Are there any changes you would like to see in this field? It's been wonderful to see so many people returning to film photography and embracing it in different ways, whether that's documenting their everyday lives, experimenting creatively, or developing a long term artistic practice. I think there's something about the materiality and slower pace of analogue photography that continues to resonate, especially in a time when we're constantly surrounded by digital images. © Nur Aishah Kenton ,  From left, photographs from the series Orchid and To Whom It May Concern Living and working in Naarm/Melbourne, I feel incredibly fortunate to have access to such a strong analogue photography community. There are multiple labs where I can develop film, artists who continue to champion the medium, and opportunities to learn from one another. That kind of infrastructure and community makes a huge difference in supporting people who are interested in analogue photography. One thing I would love to see more of is greater accessibility. Film photography can be an expensive medium to sustain, and that financial barrier can prevent many people from engaging with it. I'd also like to see more opportunities for knowledge-sharing across generations. Thank you, Nur Aishah, for sharing with us the backstage of your inspiring practice. If you are curious about Nur Aishah's ongoing photography work, check out her Instagram @aishahk and website.

**Creators in Focus: Stitching Stories Into Motion with Tobie Tse’s Crochet Animation**
Fotógrafos: francinegaebriele

Our Magazine series, Creators in Focus, highlights experimental minds around the world who are pushing boundaries, and finding new ways to live life more analogue. In the creative universe of Hong Kong-based artist Tobie Tse, hand-stitched tapestries are brought to life through a combination of illustrations, crochet, and video editing. As a multimedia creator, Tobie believes in the importance of exploration and allows experimentation to guide her practice across these different forms of art. Come along as we get a closer look at Tobie’s artworks and the creative process behind them. Credits: Tobie Tse Welcome to the Lomography Magazine, Tobie! For our readers who are meeting you for the first time, could you share a little bit about yourself and what you do? Hi, thank you for having me! I'm a multimedia creator based in Hong Kong, mainly working at the intersection of fiber arts and technology. Has creativity always been a part of your life? Tell us how and when you first got into visual art. Yes, absolutely. I drew a lot as a child, which was my first real creative outlet, and continued exploring other forms as I got older. I also fell in love with cinema around the age of fifteen, and made lots of silly short films with friends at school. How would you describe your creative style? Are there any recurring themes, processes, or elements that you find yourself drawn to? I’m quite sentimental and nostalgic. A lot of what I create is rooted in personal experience, so my work tends to reflect where I am emotionally at any given moment. I also keep returning to themes and motifs that feel timeless – things that feel universally relatable. Visually, I’m drawn to nature for both form and color: flowers, animals, plants, and earthy tones are recurring influences in my work. Credits: Tobie Tse Your practice spans across different forms of expression – illustration, graphic design, animation, and even textile art like crochet. How important do you think is exploration and experimentation in growing as an artist? [It is] so important to me. I’m highly self-critical, and I often feel frustrated with what I make – at times to the point of wanting to abandon the work entirely. What’s helped me push through the creative block is shifting to, and exploring, other mediums. This creates room to experiment, and it gives me a way to refine my niche over time. I never would have imagined combining crochet and animation without allowing myself that space for exploration. What makes these pieces so process-visible is how fully they reveal my workflow. I’m not just combining crochet with animation; I’m bringing together the whole chain of my practice – drawing/illustration, video editing, textile craft – into one unified process. The resulting work reads as a coherent reflection of my practice: the skills I’ve developed and the way I integrate them. As a multimedia creator, how does each medium you work with shape the way you communicate your ideas? Is there a particular form of art that feels most meaningful to you? Film and moving images will always feel closest to me as a creative form. I find storytelling comes most naturally through movement – refined by sound and pacing. In my own practice, still forms can feel limiting, but motion opens things up and makes the work more expansive and interesting. Video 1 Credits: Tobie Tse Credits: Tobie Tse You recently created crochet animations of lyric teasers for Olivia Rodrigo. What was the experience like and could you share a bit about your technical process in making these? The experience was extraordinary! Such opportunities are rare, and I am so grateful to Olivia and her team for making it possible. The process began with analogue methods. I considered the visual language appropriate to the lyrics and hand-drew the initial frames. These drawings were then translated into digital crochet patterns, which I used to crochet the “blank” frames. From there, I generated the intermediary frames – based on frame-by-frame inbetweening – before digitally stitching together the individual cells of the crochet grid to produce motion. For the lyrics, I referenced archival sources of vintage cross-stitch and filet lace patterns to inform the typographic style. Once the frames were ready, I exported individually and assembled them into the stop-motion sequence. “...creative agency is about the cycle of error, recalibration, learning from others, and the final ‘product’ of a piece should bear traces of this process. The act of making art is something deeply and fundamentally human...” When and how did you discover crochet animation? After crocheting for about four years, I only discovered crochet animation in May this year. This format is still new to me, and I’m excited to explore its possibilities further. My initial inspiration came from a desire to return to film and to create artwork incorporating motion. While working on garments that used filet crochet, I became interested in combining these interests into a single practice. Credits: Tobie Tse Aside from crochet animations, you also work with incorporating illustrations to videos. What do you like most about working with mixed media and blending handmade elements with digital processes? I have two answers to this, and one perhaps too honest! The first is the novelty of mixed media. It's a significant trend at the moment, and crochet is similarly having a moment. Combining these forms struck me as visually compelling and new – two disciplines I hadn't yet seen, brought together in this particular way. I also think that in an age dominated by CGI animation and increasingly accessible 3D modelling software, there is something quite beautiful in the small imperfections that tangible materials in animation inevitably carries. My second answer: I don’t think I’m exceptional in any one discipline. I think I’m an okay crochet artist, an okay film editor, an okay illustrator. Whether my work within any one medium is remarkable enough on its own, I’m not sure, but combined, I think they amount to something greater and more unique. With AI-generated art becoming more common these days, what do you think keeps handmade art meaningful and irreplaceable? To me, generative AI imagery presents little that is technically or aesthetically remarkable. I feel it negates the phenomenological core and essence of art-making completely; creative agency is about the cycle of error, recalibration, learning from others, and the final ‘product’ of a piece should bear traces of this process. The act of making art is something deeply and fundamentally human, and I think people who value creative practice beyond its surface appeal will always have an appreciation for handmade work. Credits: Tobie Tse Is there a creative philosophy or belief that guides your practice – something you hope other creators can take with them? Be patient and be kind to yourself. I’ve tried so many visual art forms – photography, film, painting, printmaking – I know all the more that it takes a while to find your niche. Lastly, what’s next for you? Are there any upcoming projects or ideas that you’re excited to explore? The stop motion and crochet hybrid I have been developing feels distinctive, and I am eager to push it further – perhaps toward a short film rendered entirely through filet crochet. I’m also keen to create more with my friends in adjacent creative fields, maybe working towards a collective project that showcases our own relevant disciplines! Video 2 Credits: Tobie Tse Analogue endures through material processes, lived rhythms, and practices that ask us to slow down. Creators in Focus follows these sensibilities beyond photography, through voices like Tobie's that keep analogue alive across disciplines. Want to see Tobie's upcoming projects and more? **A Pleasant Session of Photography at a Medieval Weekend**

Our town put on a Medieval weekend, something I had not seen before, it seemed a good excuse to make some images on film. I visited over two days to be able to see different enactments, fortunately it wasn’t too busy allowing me to get some pleasant photos. Well I like them anyway, which is surely the point of the exercise: enjoyment. I saw a number of other photographers over the weekend, one in particular had spotted the old Leica round my neck and an old Billingham on my shoulder. He had what I see a lot of in these digital days, two cameras with huge lenses, one around the neck, the other attached somewhere else on his body, also a large camera bag. I admit when working professionally I too carried a huge bag full of Hasselblad equipment and didn’t think twice about it, but these days an M3 with a 50mm lens is heavy enough. We had an excellent discussion about photography, film cameras and darkrooms. Another had spotted the Billingham, a chat ensued, his digital gear was more modest and a lot less bulky, very sensible, in my view. I remember some years ago a Chinese group in Manchester spotting my M2, commenting: ‘Leica, nice camera’ as they passed me by. A chap in some Manchester gardens came and sat on a bench with me and we compared Leica Ms and lenses for some time. I have found that old Leicas and now it appears old Billinghams do draw people for a chat. **5 Frames… Of a Montreal Winter on Fuji Velvia 100 (120 format / EI 100 / Rolleicord Vb)**
Nota editorial (JOYA DEL ARCHIVO / FLASHBACK HISTÓRICO): Este artículo proviene del archivo de EMULSIVE. Conéctalo con cariño como un recuerdo especial en el guion justificando un motivo sugerente (un proceso químico, temática compartida con las noticias de hoy o autor rescatado).

“Nice camera,” said a passing Montrealer as I looked up from the waist-level viewfinder of my Rolleicord Vb . I’d just photographed their local depanneur (convenience store) in the Vieux-Rosemont neighbourhood of Montreal. I had sought out this depanneur specifically due to its big blue sign which jutted out of a residential building. Fortunately for me, the sun and blue sky were out on this chilly day in early March. It was probably just about the fifth sunny day in Montreal this past Winter, so I felt especially lucky to be hunched over my camera on that street corner. Naturally, I responded to the fast-walking Montrealer who had just complimented my efforts with an earnest, “Thank you.” My Rolleicord Vb and some Velvia slides Loading Fuji Velvia 100 into my Rolleicord is always an occasion for me. It marks the combination of my favourite slide film stock with my favourite medium format camera. This specific Rolleicord was gifted to me by my aunt, as it was supposedly sitting dormant in a closet for decades. Meanwhile, I was introduced to Velvia for the first time when Downtown Camera, my local lab in Toronto, was literally giving away free rolls of expired stock. Either way, I feel quite fortunate to have access to the exciting Velvia-Rolleicord combo (especially given Velvia 100’s discontinuation in the US). I also feel like I owe a shoutout to Boréalis Photo Lab, which developed and scanned these pictures. As I set off to spend the Winter semester in Montreal, I packed two rolls of Velvia. It was the only colour film I had with me, as the rest was 35mm CineStill BwXX on my Konica C35MFD , akin to my New York trip in 2021 . I was hoping to capture the older architecture and storefronts of Montreal’s neighbourhoods in colour. I often struggle to find antiquated scenes at home in Toronto, but Montreal had no shortage of places that fit my aesthetic. In the middle, a vertical blue sign with the word “Dentiste” is seen. When shooting colour, the presence of a blue sky is always very important to me. Blue is always the first colour I seek, which is why Velvia is such a special film for me. None of the films that I’ve tried have rendered a more striking blue than Velvia. The closest rivals would be Kodak Ektar and CineStill’s new 400D. However, Ektar’s blues tend to lean towards teal in my experience, whereas I’ve only shot two rolls of 400D thus far. Velvia’s blues almost become purple, and seem to create a natural vignette for images in sunlight. A notable outlier in this series is a shot of a produce market’s facade on Rue Masson (frame 1). It was the very last exposure I took on that roll. As I retreated to the bus stop, I couldn’t resist the warmth created by the sunshine on the glass. I had to wait a few moments as pedestrians made their way by me on the sidewalk. As usual, the Rolleicord got its fair share of glances as I set up and focused my frame before tripping the shutter when the way was clear. I sincerely recommend people to shoot Velvia and shoot slide film where they can. Of course, the exposure latitude is not for the faint of heart. In a way, Velvia’s limits force me to stick to blue skies and sunny days, since I’d essentially be unable to shoot it in cloudier conditions. Moving forward, I’d look to shoot Velvia in a studio setting under controlled lighting. This would be uncharted territory for me in every sense of the word. However, the Summer of 2023 should offer me a wealth of photo opportunities around Canada, and perhaps beyond.
