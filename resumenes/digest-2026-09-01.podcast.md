**In Memoriam : Jean-Francois Leroy (1956-2026)**

Yesterday morning brought sad news: Jean-François Leroy left us, the day after the 38th edition of Visa pour l’Image opened for the first time without him. Since then, homages and tributes have been pouring in, the world of photojournalism is mourning. I met Jean-François in the corridors of Paris Match, in 1987 or ’88. Roger Thérond was there, of course, and so was Jean-Jacques Naudet. We lived in different countries that happened to share a border. His was made of war and famine and the photographer who goes where the story is and doesn’t always come home. Mine was lighter, celebrities, fashion, the glossy end of a trade built on make-believe, one he had very little patience for and never pretended to. We crossed paths over the years. Cordial. Respectful. Joyful. Never in the same room for long. I watched him from afar. Thirty-seven years, and he never once let up. He fought for photographers the way other people fight for family; loudly, stubbornly, without asking permission. Even sick, he worked this year’s program start to finish, with Delphine Lelu and his team. The headline exhibition is a Depardon retrospective, the reporter, not the filmmaker. He gave photojournalism one more gift before leaving. For many years now, this has always been the week we put out a Special Visa pour l’Image. This one carries a darker tone. Below this editorial, you will find the statement from AFP and the one from the Office of the President of the French Republic. Then this edition’s program, followed by a text from his friends Eliane and Jean-Pierre Laffont, whose book, Rebels With a Cause, is being presented at the Festival this year. The rest of the edition is drawn from the many gems we found in our archives, going back to our first report on the Festival in 2012. Visa pour l’Image will live on, with Delphine Lelu at the helm and the rest of his team carrying it forward, faithful, I have no doubt, to the eye and the fight he built into it. We never worked in the same world. But I always knew which one kept the other honest. Gilles Decamps A defender of photojournalism and founder of Visa pour l’Image, Jean-François Leroy has died (AFP) With his tall frame, his outspoken outbursts, and his passion for images, Jean-François Leroy, founder of the Visa pour l’Image photojournalism festival, who died on Monday, was a fierce defender of freedom of information and the soul of a professional gathering crucial to press photography. Jean-François Leroy, 69, died on Monday from cancer in the midst of the 38th edition of this world gathering held every year in Perpignan. “The entire Visa pour l’Image – Perpignan team and all of Jean-François Leroy’s family are sad to announce the death, this Monday morning, August 31, of the founder and longtime director of the international photojournalism festival,” the festival wrote on its Instagram account. “He supported and showcased photojournalists from around the world,” it added on X. For the first time in nearly 40 years, this former Sipa Press photographer was absent from Saturday’s opening of the world’s only festival dedicated to press photography, which showcases the world’s greatest photojournalists in Perpignan every year. After more than a year fighting the illness, he died surrounded by loved ones at his home in Paris, according to the festival’s team. – The festival’s “soul” – Although ill, he took part “from A to Z in preparing” the 38th edition of the festival, Delphine Lelu, Visa pour l’Image’s managing director, told AFP. He had been made a Knight of the Order of Arts and Letters. “He was the soul of this festival, he carried it on his own shoulders from the very first edition, in 1989. He leaves us a great legacy. He always wanted Perpignan to be a beacon for photojournalists, and we will make sure the festival keeps shining,” Delphine Lelu added. In the late 1980s, the festival, sponsored by Paris Match and Roger Thérond, was created on the initiative of the city of Perpignan, which wanted to establish a cultural event to extend the summer season. Every year since, photojournalists from around the world have paraded through and exhibited at this gathering, where press executives, reporters, and visitors mingled. – Springboard and consecration – “The mantra of Visa pour l’Image for thirty-eight years remains: discovering young photographers, confirming and highlighting recognized photographers, and rediscovering historic photographers of the past,” he wrote in his book Le devoir de regarder, due out in October. For young photographers, Visa could represent a springboard or a consecration. “This festival is close to my heart,” said Ed Jones, an AFP photographer who has exhibited there several times for his photo coverage of North Korea. Tributes poured in on Monday to honor the host of “this meeting place for the photojournalist tribe,” in the words of French-Iranian photographer Reza, speaking to AFP. “He had a huge impact on the profession of photojournalism” and “brought many international talents to light. His impact will endure,” added the photographer, known for his iconic portrait of Afghan commander Ahmed Massoud, assassinated in 2001. “AFP mourns the loss of Jean-François Leroy, a tireless defender of photojournalism. Over the course of Visa pour l’Image’s editions, Jean-François left us with the memory of a passionate professional with a rare instinct for spotting and revealing new talent,” said Claudia Rahola, AFP’s director of photography. A testament to his demanding standards regarding the role of images in the news, Jean-François Leroy issued a warning, for the 38th edition of Visa, about visual manipulation generated by AI. “Reality strikes back” was this year’s festival theme, with “an explicit message: in the face of AI-generated images, defend fieldwork, its slowness, and its cost.” “Doctored photos now circulate at every news event, and the festival asserts the opposite role: every image exhibited is documented, dated, and signed.” Statement from the Office of the President of the French Republic A fierce defender of photojournalism and a tireless advocate for reality, Jean-François Leroy has passed away after a life spent making images a way of seeing the world and bearing witness to its wounds. As a child, he was passionate about magic tricks. He later discovered another darkroom, that of the photo lab at La Vie catholique, where his aunt worked. After briefly studying medicine, he chose journalism, working at Photo-Reporter, Photo-Revue, and Photo Magazine, where he became editor-in-chief, and shot his first assignments for Sipa Press. Judging his own photographs with no indulgence, he soon decided to devote his life to those of others. In 1989, with the support of Roger Thérond, the legendary editor of Paris Match, he launched in Perpignan a wager that few still believed in: devoting an entire festival to news photography. Visa pour l’Image was born. From a first edition bringing together a few dozen professionals, he built it, over nearly forty years, into the world’s great gathering for photojournalism. But Visa was more than a festival. Jean-François Leroy had made it a home, a public square, at times a refuge for a profession he watched grow more fragile. He tirelessly denounced anything that threatened the very possibility of bearing witness. In Perpignan, generations of photographers found a publisher, an assignment, recognition, and often a champion. Jean-François Leroy had his tempers, his enthusiasms, his loyalties, and his biases. Uncompromising, resistant to both trends and caution, he stood by his choices in the name of a self-avowed humanism. He took the side of those whom war, dictatorship, poverty, or exile too often condemn to disappear twice: first from the world, then from our sight. He knew that a photograph has never stopped a war. He also knew that it can prevent people from claiming they didn’t know. That is why, year after year, Visa showed conflicts, revolutions, and disasters, but also solidarity and ordinary lives; and it paid tribute to those who risked, and sometimes lost, their lives bearing witness on the theatres of war. Behind his outspokenness, his outbursts, and his unmistakable look, Jean-François Leroy held a deeply democratic conviction: a free society needs journalists who go and see on its behalf. This fight had become even more urgent in the age of social media and artificial intelligence. For this thirty-eighth edition of Visa, he was warning against fabricated images and the “gravediggers of reality.” Against them, he tirelessly defended verified facts, professional journalists, and a presence on the ground. His last book bore a title that summed up his life: Le devoir de regarder. Jean-François Leroy leaves us just as the professional week of the festival he called his “baby” was opening in Perpignan. Too ill, he had not been able to join it one last time. But his fight lives on — on its walls, in its screenings, and in the eyes of all those he taught never to look away. He was a great defender of reality. The President of the Republic and his wife pay tribute to the memory of a passionate journalist, a tireless defender of photojournalism, and extend their heartfelt condolences to his family, his loved ones, the teams of Visa pour l’Image, and all the photographers whose friend and advocate he was.

**38th Visa pour l’Image – Perpignan 2026**

Respectable media outlets and journalists who have grown up using social media – which they have come to regard as a reliable source of information – were taken in by the photograph. But paradoxically, the AI revolution and its consequences may help to save journalism. Because, to be effective, these systems depend on recent, verified, reliable information. **How I Meter Slide Film**

Slide film is beautiful! Online though, it feels like there’s a lot of trepidation regarding metering for slide film. The common advice I’ve heard is to bracket your shots, so that you can feel confident that at least one of the 3 or so is properly exposed. I think this is good advice if you’re photographing something valuable, but it’s also drastically increasing the cost per shot – and at least in the States, most slide film is expensive and hard to find. And in the case of an out-of-production film stock like MS 100/1000, it’s throwing away frames that can never be replaced. Lately, I’ve been digging through my fridge and using a lot of slide film for color, and as much as I love it, I’ve felt this pressure to get each frame right too. For this reason, I wanted to share some advice and my personal approaches to metering slide film, without needing to rely on bracketing. Spot Metering In my personal opinion, using a spot meter, either TTL or a handheld one, is the best way to get results on slide film. Metering this way gives you the most creative control over how a scene is exposed, regardless of whether you’re using slide or negative film. Spot meters may seem intimidating at first, but believe me, they’re actually straightforward once you start using them. I use spot metering mode on my Contax SLRs more now than center-weighted for all films! Fuji Velvia 50, 85/1.4 Planar T, Contax RX Why? Here, using my TTL spot meter, I preserved the dark window reflections just enough to be visible, without blowing out the sunset-lit façade of the boathouse. Spot metering allows you to pick out specific slices of your scene, and gives you finer control over what details to preserve. So, how do we meter for slide film with a spot meter? If you’ve used the zone system for B&W before then this will probably be familiar to you: the goal here is to use the spot meter to figure out the exposure, for the highlights and the shadows. Specifically, what highlight and what shadow details do we want to preserve in the scene? Those will become our image’s dynamic range. Then, we will set our exposure to fall somewhere in between this range. For example, if you’re shooting manually (let’s say, aperture of f/8), and the highlights are 1/500 while the shadows are 1/125, then pick 1/250. Keep in mind, slide film has very limited exposure latitude. My rule of thumb is +/- 1-1.5 stops from either end of the exposure range, though with Ektachrome, you might be able to squeeze 1.5-2 stops. If the dynamic range is tight, then exposing at the middle will resolve everything; otherwise, you will need to pick either the shadows or the highlights to preserve. Generally, crushing shadows and preserving highlights look better on slide film to me, so in such a situation, I would shoot at +1 stop over the highlight exposure. So, if the highlights are 1/250 and the shadows are 1/30, then I’d shoot at a shutter speed of 1/125. Fuji Velvia 100, Helios-103, Contax IIa On this photo, I actually just used a phone light metering app to find the range. I wanted to preserve the dock details and the city skyline, so I set those as my shadow and my highlight areas and metered them. The sky wasn’t particularly interesting, so I was fine sacrificing it here. Thankfully, the range between highlight and shadow was only about 3 stops, and Velvia 100 has slightly better exposure latitude than the more popular Velvia 50, so exposing dead center caught everything. Fuji Velvia 100, Helios-103, Contax IIa In contrast, I took this only a few minutes afterwards, but since the dynamic range of this scene is way larger, neither shadows nor highlights exposed the best. Don’t do this! It’s a hard scene and I’m not sure I could have gotten much better, but exposing more for the highlights here might have gotten a better result while still preserving the shape of the cyclist. As I was shooting on a fully mechanical Contax IIa in both cases, I just set the shutter dial to the closest speed to what I calculated, and snapped. The method may seem a little time consuming at first, but once you get the hang of it, I find it easy enough even when in a hurry. Fuji Provia 100F, 50/1.4 Planar T, Contax RX With electronic stepless shutters & TTL it’s even easier to follow this method. Just quickly sweep the scene and lock the appropriate exposure; you’ll be better equipped for more complex lighting situations and dynamic scenes. Like this shot of delicious Uyghur kebabs being grilled at Harvard’s Spring Festival. Despite the challenging lighting, I think I was still able to pull enough shadow detail to complete the image. Fuji Provia 100F, 180/2.8 Sonnar T, Contax RX Of course, you don’t always have to try to maximize your exposure latitude. Once you know the exposure of the highlights and shadows, you can play with the lighting however you like. As a simple example, in the above photo, I chose to crush the shadows in order to emphasize the person’s silhouette. Though this method is fast enough in most cases for my style of photography, there is another way to use the spot meter to be quicker. The Fast Way Sometimes, we’re in a rush and need to snap immediately, such as in a highly dynamic street scene…or even to catch falling cherry blossoms at just the right time, such as the featured image of this article. The quickest, most straightforward way of using a spot meter, is to just meter your subject and rely on it being properly exposed relative to the scene. Using Ektachrome or Provia 100F, which have better exposure latitude than Velvia 50 and Velvia 100, also helps make it easier. Kodak Ektachrome E100, 85/1.4 Planar T, Contax 167MT This in turn makes selecting the right subject the most critical aspect of composing and metering a scene. Here, I used my Contax 167MT’s TTL meter in spot mode to meter the lighthouse house, while I tried to catch the waves washing up upon the rocks. The deepest shadows came out a bit muddy, but everything important is properly exposed and full of detail, even the lovely peach & lilac tones of the sky. Fuji Velvia 50, 85/1.4 Planar T, Contax RX For this sunset/dusk shot, I was able to get away with simple center-weighted TTL metering on my Contax RX. In even, diffused light, such as on overcast days, blue hour, or subjects fully illuminated by sunlight, the dynamic range will become limited enough to where center-weighted will be sufficient. Worth noting too, Velvia 50 particularly performs well in such lighting conditions. Sunny 16-ish If you have no meter, it is possible to use the Sunny 16 rule for slide film. It’s not as scary as it sounds! This is in fact how people used to shoot their vacation photos in the past, without a meter. In general, the guidelines I’ve seen from vintage film are Sunny 16 exposure for a highlighted subject, +1 stop overexposure if the subject is partially in the shadows/complicated lighting, and +2 stops overexposure if the subject is entirely in the shadows. Fuji Velvia 100, Helios-103, Contax IIa Here, again using my Contax IIa, I followed Sunny 11 – in Boston our sunlight even in the summer doesn’t always go to f/16, most of the year it’s f/11. Actually, it seems the value that day was probably more like f/13, so this may be a little too much light, but thankfully there is no serious loss of detail. Films for Practice Now let’s go shoot! As with anything practice makes perfect, and while the first roll may be a learning experience, I think slide film is much easier to get used to than is commonly suggested. My recommendation for one’s first slide film is Ektachrome E100. I find it to have the most forgiving latitude of any of the currently available slide films, and it is also typically the cheapest to find. Even if your exposure is slightly off, you can still get a usable image. Like here: Kodak Ektachrome E100, 100/3.5 Sonnar T, Contax 167MT In this case, I overcompensated for the snow and the whites are pretty blown out. Nevertheless, some information in the highlights is still preserved; the cloud, seaspray, and snow textures still show through. Ektachrome is also good at capturing subtle, pastel tones in a scene, which can help with seeing how accurate your exposure is. Provia 100F is an alternative to Ektachrome for similar reasons, but it’s much harder to find in the States and is more expensive, plus it feels a tad less forgiving. If Provia is easy to find where you are though, it’s a fine alternative, especially if you prefer the more saturated, magenta look of Fuji over Kodak’s colder, more natural rendering. Fuji Provia 100F, 28/2.8 Distagon T, Contax RX Despite TTL metering being less accurate with wide-angles, Provia was able to give me a very well exposed view of the crowd in this street concert, without any muddied shadows. The sky is blown out, but given that it was a grey, overcast day, it wasn’t a loss to me. Another option in theory, is expired Fuji Sensia 100, if you can find some that was properly freezer stored. However I am generally against training on expired film because it introduces too many variables into the equation. The final Sensia rolls are at least 16 years old at this point, and chemical degradation will have affected their exposure latitude and color rendition. Still, if you can find some for a good price, it may be worth trying for fun. Ultimately, slide film is a ton of fun to use, and it rewards a careful eye, regardless of whichever stock you pick. By improving my own metering approach, I’ve both increased the quality and number of successfully exposed images whenever I shoot something like Velvia, and I hope that these tips will be helpful for you too. **Beacons Through Time: East Coast US lighthouses captured on solargraphs**
Nota editorial (JOYA DEL ARCHIVO / FLASHBACK HISTÓRICO): Este artículo proviene del archivo de EMULSIVE. Conéctalo con cariño como un recuerdo especial en el guion justificando un motivo sugerente (un proceso químico, temática compartida con las noticias de hoy o autor rescatado).

“Beacons Through Time” explores the themes of patience, constancy, and rhythm by capturing US East Coast lighthouses using extreme long exposure solargraphs . Between 2022 and 2023, I visited roughly 25 lighthouses along the coast and rivers, spanning Midcoast Maine to northern Georgia, to place and retrieve nearly 200 cameras. In this article, I outline the project execution and logistics, construction and placement of the solargraphs, and share some lessons learned and results. For those who are unfamiliar, solargraphs are cameras with the exposure triangle tilted in the extreme towards long exposure. As pinhole cameras, their aperture hovers between f/150 and f/300, barely sipping any light. Solargraphs typically use darkroom paper instead of film, which has an ISO in the single digits. In combination, these two elements allow exposure times in the months or even years; when placed in situ, solargraphs patiently record the sun’s transit across the sky in long arcs, as well as slowly exposing landscape and foreground elements. Early solargraphs that helped inspire me to pursue lighthouses as a subject: Morristown NJ Green Civil War Memorial Statue My interest in lighthouse solargraphy started out, oddly enough, as a purely aesthetic interest. I had completed an earlier local solargraph project in the depths of Covid, and my favorite images all had a common element – a tall building or structure juxtaposed against the sun’s leaping arcs. For my next project, I thought, what better subject than lighthouses?! But the more I thought about it, the more I saw a deep connection between the format and subject. Solargraphs are long-standing and capture a steady, constant daily cycle. In them, you can see cloudy days, storms, even times when the cameras are covered in snow and ice. Early solargraphs that helped inspire me to pursue lighthouses as a subject: Presbyterian Church in Morristown Parish House. Nevertheless, the sun maintains its drumbeat rhythm, a skyward sentinel that provides us with light and life. There’s much in common there with the beacons that line our shores and rivers, many dating back centuries and still proudly standing watch. The name of the project — Beacons Through Time — comes from a standing exhibit at the National Lighthouse Museum in Staten Island , which I visited as part of the research for this project and from which I left with a strong sense of vision and theme. This project initially started out as a small idea that rapidly grew in scope. I had first envisioned a calendar project in my home state of New Jersey, with the core idea to capture 12 lighthouses and bundle them together in a format that naturally expressed the theme of time. After a few phone calls for permission, I realized I might have to look outside the state to get 12 representative examples, and I expanded into New York state and down to Virginia. The map! The next thing I knew, I had secured permission or was underway with permitting up and down most of the East Coast. Planning & Permission I’m a stickler for getting permission for my solargraphs, a tendency this project has vindicated. On the one hand, securing permits from state and federal parks, seeking formal approval from local governments, managing insurance, and cold-calling countless private owners might seem daunting — and it is a lot of time and work — but the upsides to this are tremendous. Apart from ensuring compliance, it’s been a joy connecting with dozens of stewards of these wonderful historic places, and gaining access to a network of enthusiastic supporters who helped me contact further lighthouses up and down the coast. Working formally with sites also helps keep the solargraphs safe and untampered due to buy-in from the host sites and ensuring maintenance activities don’t remove them. When in doubt, make a phone call. Designing & Constructing the solargraphs I built roughly 200 solargraphs to employ in Beacons Through Time, and placed several at each site to ensure at least one successful exposure. Most solargraphs are homemade, although commercial examples are available on the market. In this project, I used a blend of homemade 3D printed cameras, with a sprinkling of solargraphs made from tall drinks cans and mint tins. In my opinion, the 3D printed designs reign supreme for a few reasons. The optically optimal shape is a half-circular cylinder with a curved film plane and a pinhole placed in the center. This ensures the focal length is horizontally consistent, as it’s basically the radius, reducing distortion at the edges. 3D printing allows for custom shapes and construction at scale. 3D printing also allows easy custom iterations to shape and design. I worked with my friend Preston, a CAD expert, to dial in my design, including a tailor-made cap, grooves to hold the paper steady, and a small receptacle for desiccant packets as a failsafe against moisture. After trial and error, we opted for PETG plastic, which is easy to use, heat resistant, and waterproof. For anyone interested in experimenting, the STL file is available here . 3D Designs allow for fast iteration and programmatic design Solargraph pinholes themselves are also typically homemade. Most practitioners poke or drill holes in thin metal sheets, then sand them down to ensure a smooth and circular hole. I take a different direction, using “single slot aperture grids” designed for laboratory use, which are laser drilled, incredibly precise, and consistent. They are available in practical pinhole sizes (0.2mm, 0.3mm, 0.4mm…). However, they are quite small, roughly the size of a sequin. To improve their practicality, I sandwich the aperture grids between two small pieces of sticker vinyl and then attach them to the solargraph bodies. After retrieving a few copper-pinhole solargraphs that made their way into this project and seeing the corrosion, I splurged on gold foil pinholes to increase resistance to saline coastal air. It was a wise choice. I specifically sized my solargraphs to match the lab standard aperture sizes, landing on 15mm, 32mm, and 65mm focal length bodies as the workhorses for this project. Paper choice Paper choice also matters and is interesting; sepia-toned papers become vibrant blue on reversal, and cooler papers become earthy. Modern available papers include those from ILFORD, Agfa, Foma, and Oriental. That said, my favorite are vintage Kodak darkroom papers dating from the 1980s, Kodak Polycontrast RC, Kodak Kodabrome RC, and Kodak Panalure RC, along with Agfa MCP 310 at the top of my list. If you have old paper around, don’t throw it out! It can absolutely still steal the show in solargraph photography. Agfa MCP 310; Concord Point Light, MD Kodak Panalure II RC; Nauset Light, MA Foma 312; Bug Light, ME As a final touch for my 3D-printed pinhole cameras, I did some extra weatherproofing to help them stand up to the wind and waves along the coast. On top of the PETG plastic, gold pinholes, and built-in caps, I coated each 3D-printed body with Flexseal to help improve resistance and wrapped each in heavy-duty duct tape (sometimes camouflaging units to help avoid public tampering). I also glued desiccant packets into the well of some solargraphs to soak up any wayward moisture inside. Placement & Retrieval I’m a hobbyist photographer, not a professional, but I think for many of us, our nonartistic professional experiences likely help our artistic pursuits in unexpected ways. I may not have an arts degree, but I’ve managed projects and delivered work product over the years, and managing the logistics of Beacons Through Time felt like a natural extension of those experiences. After securing permission from my host sites in early 2022, this project had two main phases: one to place and one to retrieve the solargraphs. Each involved a handful of long and wonderful road trips up and down the East Coast. In late 2022 and early 2023, I dedicated most weekends and a week off to travel north to Maine and south to Georgia to place my cameras, crashing on friends’ couches and camping along the way. Placement is the tricky part of solargraphy — one has to balance location and composition, susceptibility to vandalism, the sun’s Southern arc, and suitable rigid placement structures to secure a good shot. I typically use silicone glue to attach solargraphs, which holds them perfectly steady and is non-toxic, waterproof, and relatively easy to remove when complete. Placements near Assateague Light. Placing solargraphs in uncomfortable locations – like these in the middle of a swamp – helps keep tampering to a minimum. Placing solargraphs up high, like this one on a telephone pole at Sandy Hook Light, is another way to keep them out of sight and mind. The positioning of solargraphs, whether its perfectly vertical, canted upwards, or otherwise, have different effects and impacts on the final image. Timing also matters for placement since the sun’s arc is low in the winter and high in the summer. I knew I wanted to catch a wide range of the year, but the Southeast’s punishing hurricane season further complicated this, limiting my realistic window to a winter-to-summer cycle, and pushing me to place in winter 2022. To keep tabs, I gave each solargraph a unique ID, complete with an incremental identifying number and shorthand information about the container type & size, the pinhole type, and the paper used in each. Each was tracked in terms of placement location and date, and retrieval date. Solargraphy is one of those styles where the artist has to be comfortable relinquishing some control of the work. We have a lot of say over the design and placement of the cameras, but beyond that, it’s a months-long wait and collaboration with mother nature, time, and luck. Some host sites provided me with regular updates, but for the most part, I spent Spring 2023 patiently hoping for success. By summer 2023, it was time to retrieve. I made two trips to New England and one long weeklong journey to Georgia and back, scanner in tow. Solargraph retrieval is easy — cover the pinhole with a piece of tape, wiggle the solargraph off its mount, clean up any adhesive or attachment devices, and finally, log the retrieval date. The real fun begins with scanning! Solargraphs are typically undeveloped and unfixed, and directly scanned, inverted, and edited. Years of planning, construction, traveling, and waiting culminate in seeing the finished product come to life, with time itself manifesting in a single image and telling stories about what happened during the months of exposure. The solargraph paper directly out of the container. The solargraph after a high quality scan and orientation correction. The final solargraph image after inverting from negative to positive. A few notable lighthouses exemplified this concept really well in Beacons Through Time. At Portsmouth Harbor Light in New Hampshire, the large storm right before Christmas 2022 destroyed the wooden bridge over the sea right in the middle of my exposure, causing the bridge to be transparent in the final image. At LV-118 Lightship Overfalls — a lightship anchored on a bed of silt in Lewes, DE — the subtle tidal movements under the ship caused some of the sun’s arcs to wiggle and blur at regular intervals throughout the five months of exposure. Lightship Overfalls, Lewes, DE. Placed on the deck of a moored lightship, the squiggles and undulations in the sun’s arcs occur due to changing tides shifting the boat on and off the silt bed it normally rests upon. Portsmouth Harbor Light, Portsmouth, NH. The destruction of the footbridge to the light in December 2022 lends a ghostly element to this image, and tells the story of crashing waves, freezing winds, and a destructive sea. For me, this is the magic and essence of solargraph art. With a bit of planning and patience, we can be rewarded with unique and brilliant images that not only look beautiful but tell quiet and subtle stories about what happened during the exposure, whether it’s dramatic events like a Noreaster dashing a bridge on the rocks, or the gentle rhythm of the tides shifting and wriggling the sun’s arcs every few weeks. Bald Head Light, Bald Head Island NC The entire project can be viewed at the Beacons Through Time project site . Over the coming months, I’ll be working on prints, a calendar, and hopefully a book, as well as at least one artist talk, so stay tuned! I also owe a great deal to my friends and family who joined me on long excursions up and down the coast to place and retrieve these, and a special thank you to Preston Ruff and Matt Lopez, both of whom provided extensive material support on the design & construction of these cameras. **Making Chris Killip’s North**

Seacoal beach, Lynemouth, Northumberland, 1983 © Thames &amp; Hudson / Chris Killip Photography Trust / Magnum Photos “To be given the keys to the archive is a privilege,” says Mark Holborn who, nearly four decades after editing Chris Killip’s seminal In Flagrante, was trusted by Killip’s son Matthew to make a new book of his work, North When Mark Holborn sat down with a finished copy of North, the book he edited from Chris Killip’s archive, he was surprised. “I got an enormous charge off the book yesterday when I looked at it,” he says. “It’s almost visceral for me.” He had seen proofs and PDFs throughout its production, but this was the first time he had turned the pages of the physical book before. “It’s like playing an album for the first time. You sit and go from beginning to end.” There is more at stake here than the completion of another photobook. Holborn first worked with Killip almost four decades ago on In Flagrante, which was published by Secker &amp; Warburg in 1988 and is now regarded as one of the defining photobooks of Britain’s post-industrial era. Killip died in 2020, so it was his son, Matthew, who gave Holborn full access to the archive – including photographs the editor had never seen, alongside those he has known for most of his adult life. “For this one, I have a sort of duty,” Holborn tells me in a video call. “Killip is not here. To be given the keys to the archive is a privilege, which I have to qualify. I want to do him justice. This is not about the projection of my own fantasy of the North. It’s about my loyalty to him.” --> Wallsend, Tyneside, 1976 © Thames &amp; Hudson / Chris Killip Photography Trust / Magnum Photos Raby Street store, Byker, Tyneside, 1975 © Thames &amp; Hudson / Chris Killip Photography Trust / Magnum Photos “The first thing you see in the photographs is that they’re remarkably elegant. Their elegance is almost in contrast to their subject matter.” Mark Holborn North, published by Thames &amp; Hudson, is neither a conventional retrospective nor a collection of Killip’s greatest hits. Comprising 224 photographs, many previously unpublished, it follows a single extended sequence. It also comes less than four years after the publisher’s Chris Killip 1946–2020, which was conceived in conjunction with an international touring exhibition and organised around the breadth of Killip’s individual projects. Holborn says that book, which provides a definitive account of the photographer’s life and work, “will serve historians well in the future”. But his own editorial principle is almost its inverse. “I was trying to find a structure that could contain a single flowing narrative,” he says, “because that is what interests me and that is the way I work.” Project boundaries dissolve in consequence. Seacoal, Skinningrove, Huddersfield and photographs made across the North East meet within the same flow, while the main body of work is framed unexpectedly by early photographs from Killip’s native Isle of Man, and later images of pilgrimages at Croagh Patrick and Máméan in Ireland – what Holborn calls “stepping stones or border crossings” into and out of the sequence. Bad editing jars, often imperceptibly, whereas good editing goes almost unnoticed. North takes us back in time to view not just what Killip saw, but what he discerned. The effect is a kind of magical sense-making. Holborn’s astute grasp of Killip’s work, together with the magic he brings to placing pictures together – pairs working in harmony, setting each other off, building a sequence to achieve a poetic coherence – allows us to become fully absorbed within the mind’s eye of the photographer. Many darlings are killed along the way. Individual images give way to the whole. And while North never betters the concise, angry dignity of In Flagrante, it uses its 300 pages to convey Killip’s slow determination to map the margins – between city and country, the sea and land, industrial past and uncertain future – and his connection to the people caught in between. The sequence also brings terrain to the fore. Killip is celebrated for the intensity of his encounters with people – children and teenagers, shipyard workers, fishing communities, families gathering sea coal from the beach at Lynemouth – but North makes clearer the continuity of the landscape around them. Streets and housing estates, beaches, shipyards and industrial structures cease to be backgrounds; people and place become part of the same historical description. Holborn deliberately avoided many of Killip’s best-known pictures. “I was chasing something else, something altogether different,” he says. “I didn’t want this book to look like something you had seen before.” Only three photographs from In Flagrante appear, towards the end. “The inclusion of those three pictures was a huge concession on my part to what had come before.” One, Killip’s celebrated image of a boy on a wall in Jarrow, becomes “a coda of sorts”. There is, as Holborn writes in North, “both continuity and departure” Whippet and Richard, Skinningrove, North Yorkshire, 1982 © Thames &amp; Hudson / Chris Killip Photography Trust / Magnum Photos Byker, Tyneside, 1976 © Thames &amp; Hudson / Chris Killip Photography Trust / Magnum Photos Such decisions place unusual emphasis on the editor, a role Holborn has occupied for more than four decades but which, when In Flagrante was made, was yet to be clearly defined. He began at Creative Camera before moving to New York in the early 1980s to work at Aperture, an experience which immersed him in a photographic culture operating on a different scale to Britain. His subsequent career has encompassed long collaborations with Don McCullin and Daido Moriyama; major projects involving the archives of Larry Burrows, Lennart Nilsson and NASA; and books with artists such as Lucian Freud. In America, he worked with figures including William Eggleston, Lee Friedlander, Richard Avedon and Irving Penn. Most significantly for the Killip story, at Aperture he helped bring Nan Goldin’s The Ballad of Sexual Dependency into publication. “Nan was raw, authentic, and so it seemed to me totally obvious to publish The Ballad of Sexual Dependency,” he says. He adds that working with Killip seemed equally obvious. The feeling was mutual. When David Godwin at Secker &amp; Warburg offered Killip the opportunity to make a book – following his 1985 exhibition with Graham Smith, Another Country at the Serpentine – Killip specifically requested Holborn as editor. He later recalled that Holborn’s experience at Aperture, and his exposure to the best contemporary photography being published internationally, offered something he felt removed from, living in Newcastle. Aperture “was a very good place to serve an apprenticeship”, Hoborn says. “I could bring that back, in a sense, which is one of the reasons I started to work with Killip. I think he was attracted to some sense of internationalism that I might have brought with me.” Holborn had already tested some of the ideas that would shape In Flagrante. In 1986 he published Killip’s Seacoal photographs in Aperture magazine, running them across the gutter and giving them maximum height. “I loved the impact of the scale,” he recalls. For In Flagrante, Killip arrived at Holborn’s London home with work prints and a preliminary dummy. They abandoned the dummy and began again, laying the pictures across the floor; Holborn subsequently constructed another dummy on his kitchen table using prints, card and tape. His description of their respective roles is disarmingly simple: “He was an artist. He made pictures. I absorbed them and arranged them.” “Arranged” understates the intervention. The vertical format was Holborn’s decision; he resisted a large horizontal book because he feared it would resemble a “refined portfolio” (as Steidl would do nearly 30 years later with In Flagrante Two), in abrupt contrast to the photographs themselves. Instead, images frequently ran across the gutter. “A portfolio of good pictures doesn’t have the right voltage,” Holborn says. “This is an angry book. It’s savage.” A contemporary Time Out review complained about the gutter running through Killip’s photographs. Holborn remains unapologetic: “Well, of course we did. That was very purposeful. It benefits from the scale, and the gutter serves it. It’s an attack; an assault on the images themselves. Without it, the book would be docile.” For Holborn, editing a photobook is closer to editing film than arranging a portfolio. A sequence should become “cumulatively emotional”, controlling the reader’s experience of time, he says. “You move fast-forward, and you pause, and you slow down, and you accelerate The looking at the book has its own time.” Four decades on, what Holborn sees in Killip’s individual pictures is a similar tension between form and content. “The first thing you see in the photographs is that they’re remarkably elegant,” he says. “Their elegance is almost in contrast to their subject matter.” Bus shelter, North Shields, Tyneside, 1975 © Thames &amp; Hudson / Chris Killip Photography Trust / Magnum Photos Nini, Clare and Rocker, coming home from school, Seacoal camp, Lynemouth, Northumberland, 1984 © Thames &amp; Hudson / Chris Killip Photography Trust / Magnum Photos Killip’s slowly measured, formally controlled photographs possess what Holborn describes in North as a “pictorial grace”, sometimes so pronounced that it almost contradicts the stark reality being depicted. Yet what strikes Holborn equally is their tenderness. “It’s a very brutal world, and within this brutal world there are remarkable scenes of tenderness,” he points out. Children are held and embraced; dogs and puppies are handled affectionately; horses on the sea-coal beach are more than beasts of burden. “There is great picture-making, but within those pictures there is enormous humanity.” That humanity complicates the familiar shorthand of Killip as a photographer of Thatcher’s Britain. The central photographs in North begin in 1975 and were made under Labour and Conservative governments alike; to reduce them to opposition to Conservative power, Holborn writes, is “far too simplistic”. What Killip was witnessing was a larger, more fundamental historical transformation. “He was photographing a world that was passing as he photographed it,” Holborn says. “It was a world in transition, and the old world was decaying and collapsing all around.” The difference is that Killip and the original audience for In Flagrante were still living inside that transition. “At the time Killip was making it, he sensed it, but it was still there,” Holborn says. “Now it’s gone.” Time has therefore altered the images as much as it has the places they describe. Holborn realised while working on North that the distance separating a young reader today from Killip’s North East is roughly equivalent to that separating the 1980s from the Second World War. Photographs that once described an unfolding present have become historical evidence, and some have moved beyond photographic history into collective memory; “When someone wants to find imagery of the north of England in the late 20th century, they will turn to Killip.” North is an attempt to defamiliarise that familiar body of work – to return to the archive, remove many of the pictures through which Killip has come to be known, and construct another route through the landscape he photographed. Holborn describes the relationship between photographer and editor as a process of “osmosis”, in which each gradually absorbs the sensibility of the other. With Killip no longer present to approve or reject his choices, that relationship now depends on something more elusive. “I’ve made a living with my eyes,” Holborn says. “How to look is how I’ve made my living for the last 40 or 50 years. **Spotlight on Takuma Nakahira**

Takuma Nakahira in the coffee shop at the Nagisa Hotel in Zushi, Japan, February 1972, from At the Limits of the Gaze: Selected Writings by Takuma Nakahira (Aperture, 2025) © The Estate of Isao Sekiguchii Takuma Nakahira was a prolific theorist as well as a cutting-edge image-maker and co-founder of the radical Provoke magazine. Now, for the first time, his key 1970s essays have been translated into English Takuma Nakahira is not famous in the West, and in Japan is perhaps best-known as a photographer. But the co-founder of the legendary Provoke magazine was also a prolific critic and essayist, exploring the medium of photography in articles in art magazines and literary journals which were eventually collected into books. Now a selection of his essays has been published in English for the first time, edited and translated by Japanese photography experts Daniel Abbe and Franz Prichard. At the Limits of the Gaze collects 10 texts published between 1970–75, ranging from jazz to Eugène Atget to mass media, and showing Nakahira to be radical, cosmopolitan and still keenly relevant. “Nakahira considered what it means to be ‘contemporary’, the idea that folks such as Walter Benjamin had iterated certain ideas much earlier in modernity’s history,” says Prichard. “Being a leftist, Nakahira was adamant that the material conditions hadn’t really changed between his time and that earlier moment; he drew attention to this ongoingness. And the political potency of his questioning and interrogation of power vis-à-vis visual media has only amplified over time. The conditions have not diminished, they’ve only gotten more intensive – and the intensification of the integration of the visual media that surrounds us impels us to return, again and again, to these questions.” --> Untitled by Takuma Nakahira. From the photobook For a Language to Come, 1970 “The political potency of Nakahira’s questioning and interrogation of power vis-à-vis visual media has only amplified over time” – Franz Prichard As Prichard points out, Nakahira’s approach to photography was inseparable from his politics. Provoke was founded in 1968 amid protests in Japan, France, Czechoslovakia and beyond, and questioned images as part of a wider interrogation of how we relate to the world; Nakahira published a photobook in 1970 titled For a Language to Come. The magazine and book both reject easy paths from photography to knowledge, and abandon straight documentary in favour of images further from legibility, but closer to lived experience. Both also question the power structure embedded in the camera’s one-way gaze. As Nakahira wrote in his 1972 essay The Illusion Called Document: From Document to Monument: “Against our immediate association of the word document with an objective, impersonal viewpoint, I wanted to rethink this word through an emphasis only on the endless encounter between the world and the self.” In his 1975 essay The Will Toward History: Surrealism’s Potential Power, Nakahira writes that surrealism was an attempt at the total liberation of the human, meanwhile, and argues, “this is why it was the first shot fired against the logic of positivism, which supports the modern system of capital”. His 1973 essay Why an Illustrated Botanical Guide? argues along similar lines, emphasising subjectivity and dialogue over insensitive, hubristic authority. “Looking cannot happen apart from the body,” he writes, adding, “As much as I may look at the world, it will always throw the gaze of things back to me at the same time.” But Nakahira’s position also shifted over time, Abbe and Prichard point out; he became disillusioned with the new photographic language he and Provoke had tried to build after it was co-opted for advertising, and by the mid-70s was writing less politically. He also varied his subjects according to magazine commission, from avant-garde small press journals to the more established Asahi Camera. At the Limits of the Gaze includes essays from his two collections – Why an Illustrated Botanical Guide? Collected Writings on Images (1973) and Duel on Photography (1977, co-authored with Kishin Shinoyama) – and therefore “two discursive moments where he’s collecting his thoughts in very different ways, in very different contexts”, Prichard explains. He and Abbe emphasise that their book translates a fraction of Nakahira’s total output too – they knew it will often serve as an introduction, says Abbe, though they left “as many hints as possible to what else is there”. Untitled by Takuma Nakahira. From the photobook For a Language to Come, 1970 Untitled by Takuma Nakahira. From the photobook For a Language to Come, 1970 --> Originally interested in poetry, Nakahira also experimented with his writing, sometimes adopting the impersonal stance of a conventional essayist, at other times a first-person, more diaristic approach. His 1976 text Interlude passionately narrates burning his notebooks and negatives, and drinking with a passing stranger, referencing the burn of the harsh liquor and his own “clouded consciousness”. “I had no idea what I would do after this. But I will never take a photograph again, I thought. I cannot become a collector of memories, I thought,” he writes, later adding, “There was no boundary between the sea and the sky, just the jet-black celestial sphere.” Nakahira’s writing here is lyrical, and this essay points to overlaps between his writing and photography; elsewhere he highlights the differences between text and images, however, contrasting a visual document of an individual tree with the generalised – or universal, or even objective – ‘tree’ summoned by the word, in his 1970 essay Has Photography Been Able to Provoke Language?. At times Nakahira’s writing informs the photography and vice versa, Abbe notes, but in general direct links are rare. “They aren’t on separate tracks as if they never intersect, but they are only loosely touching,” he smiles. Even so, Nakahira is a key read for those interested in images and visual culture – or in mapping anti-imperial perspectives. A critic of America’s postwar role in Japan, Nakahira also questioned Japanese history, and in particular the nation’s approach to the Pacific Okinawa Islands. “Old people sell their things without uttering a word,” he writes in My Naked-Eye Reflex: 1974, Okinawa, Summer. “What does the silence of those men and women say?” At the Limits of the Gaze: Selected Writings by Takuma Nakahira, edited by Daniel Abbe and Franz Prichard, is published by Aperture, priced $30.
