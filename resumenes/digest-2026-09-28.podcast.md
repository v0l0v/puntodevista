They were perfect for the informal settings where photography was meant to record life, not interfere with it. With plastic set-focus lenses, fixed shutter speeds, and preloaded film stock, not much seemed to be left for technical experimentation. At more than 4,600 meters above sea level, the Salkantay Trek to Machu Picchu is a challenge to behold, just as beautiful as it is arduous. By the end of June, we booked tickets to Machu Picchu for September. The others decided to travel ahead, while Beatriz and I arrived in Peru on September 16, 2025. We would start the trek on September 19, 2025, and our flight back to Brazil would be on September 23, 2025, the same day as our visit to Machu Picchu, we were running on a very tight schedule. When we got to the airport at Cuzco, there wasn’t that much time to do anything else beyond getting an Uber to the hostel and doing a quick visit around downtown Cuzco (the Historic Center of the city). **The Questionnaire : Ovlac by Carole Schmitz**

OVLAC : PHOTOGRAPHING THE PERFORMANCE For Ovlac, spectacle is not the subject. It is the raw material. Costumes, bodies, temporary structures, improbable machines, desert landscapes: everything appears heightened, almost excessive. Yet beneath the visual intensity, his photographs are concerned with something more elusive—a world inventing itself in real time, where reality and performance constantly exchange places. Burning Man is the perfect paradox. Nothing there is entirely accidental, yet nothing is entirely controlled. Participants construct characters, environments, rituals, even private mythologies. Reality arrives pre-staged. Ovlac photographs what happens within that fiction: the moment when performance slips into something less certain, when a person becomes an image. This is where his approach to portraiture becomes particularly revealing. Faces are present, but identity is rarely the point. Painted bodies, masked figures and isolated silhouettes become visual signs, suspended somewhere between person and apparition. The portrait shifts from who someone is to what they become in front of the camera. Ovlac’s background in styling, communication and fashion is part of this equation. He understands how images are constructed, but he also knows when to let them escape control. Light, chance, crowds and accidents become collaborators. His photographs occupy the space between documentary and staging, between what is found and what is composed. The desert provides the tension. Its vastness turns bodies into sculptures and gestures into graphic marks. Figures hover against an almost abstract expanse, somewhere between reality and fiction. The landscape is not a backdrop; it is an active force in the image, stripping away context and amplifying presence. What distinguishes Ovlac is his ability to photograph a society willingly performing itself without reducing it to spectacle. Beneath the costumes and excess lies a more fundamental question: what happens to identity when ordinary rules are suspended? What selves do we invent when we are briefly allowed to become someone else? This is also where photography becomes more than documentation. Ovlac is interested in the moment when an improbable scene becomes believable because the camera has framed it. His images do not ask us to decide whether what we are seeing is real or constructed. They exploit the uncertainty between the two. The result is a body of work in which photography becomes a space for transformation: a place where bodies become characters, environments become stages, and reality acquires the strange authority of fiction. Instagram : @ovlacRepresented by Ellia Art Gallery : www.ellia-artgallery.com Your first photographic trigger?Ovlac: My uncle, Richard Melloul, a great photographer. When I was twelve, he was shooting Plastic Bertrand, who was one of my idols at the time. He invited me to the shoot so I could meet him. And, to my surprise, what has stayed with me all these years is not really Plastic Bertrand, but the astonishment of watching my uncle work. I watched him build a story, direct a shoot, with an entire crew around him listening to his every instruction. Even Plastic Bertrand—the biggest star in my eyes back then—followed my uncle’s directions to the letter. In the end, I was far more fascinated by everything happening around the shoot than by Plastic Bertrand himself. There were so many cameras, flashes, lights, so many people orbiting around my uncle… That whole world left a deep impression on me. A photographic memory from your childhood?Ovlac: A photograph of my parents dancing. They were people I didn’t recognize. From another era, another image, another story. Their youth was something I had never really been able to picture, even after hearing them talk about it. That photograph made me see my parents differently—as people who had had a life, a youth, a history before me. I still keep the photograph on my bedside table. The camera of your childhood?Ovlac: A Polaroid. Being able to capture a moment and show it immediately felt like magic back then. What I remember most is waiting for the image to develop and shaking the freshly ejected photograph, convinced it would somehow make the process faster or improve the result. The camera you use today?Ovlac: A Canon EOS R3 and a Canon EOS-1D X Mark III. I’ve stayed loyal to Canon because I know the system inside out. That familiarity allows me to focus on everything except the camera: the image, the moment, what is happening in front of me. The person in an image who inspired you?Ovlac: I’d say Richard Melloul. Two things fascinated me about him. First, discovering that you could tell an entire story in a single image. And it’s crazy how much of a team you sometimes need around you for that one click, that precise moment when you press the shutter. The second was his freedom. He could take off for a weekend on a Tuesday. His life was so different from that of my other uncles or my parents’ friends. His freedom fascinated me as much as his photographs. The image you wish you had taken?Ovlac: John Lennon and Yoko Ono, photographed by Annie Leibovitz on December 8, 1980, in their apartment at the Dakota in New York, just hours before John Lennon was assassinated. It takes an extraordinary amount of trust in a photographer to accept such a powerful image, to make yourself vulnerable in every sense of the word, and to allow the photographer’s vision to create a unique moment that will become iconic. Knowing that the photograph was taken on the very day of his death inevitably gives it an even more devastating dimension. The photograph that moved you most?Ovlac: A photograph of my father and me on Christmas Eve. I’m very young, holding a little guitar, and my father is leaning down to kiss me. My mother is standing just behind us, watching. It’s certainly not a great photograph in the artistic sense. Just an ordinary moment in life, captured almost without significance at the time. But over the years, the image has become far more important to me than the photograph itself. Perhaps that is also the magic of photography: freezing a moment without yet knowing, when you press the shutter, just how precious it will one day become. And the one that made you angry?Ovlac: The photograph of Britney Spears shaving her head. For me, that is the moment the photographer steps completely outside his role. There comes a point when you have to know when to put the camera down. Which photograph changed the world?Ovlac: Neil Armstrong on the Moon. **Japanese Photographs from the BnF, 1945-2025 – Scenes, Signs, Sensations**

The Bibliothèque nationale de France is among the first Western institutions to have taken a genuine interest in modern and contemporary Japanese photography. The collection devoted to this field today brings together nearly 4,000 prints, by some 200 artists and photojournalists, along with about a hundred emblematic magazines and books, tracing the transformations of the medium in Japan since 1945. It is from this catalogue that the exhibition marking the bicentennial celebrations of the birth of photography draws its material: 300 works, including 250 prints, mostly in black and white, and around fifty rare books from the BnF’s collections. Across this holdings — and despite some notable absences, such as Nobuyoshi Araki, Tomoko Yoneda, Kikuji Kawada or Hiroshi Sugimoto — a photography emerges that is as attached to the sheer description of reality as it is to its formal qualities. The apparent sobriety of the compositions is often accompanied by extremely elaborate printing work, in which the photographic material itself becomes a site of experimentation. Yet this tension between economy of means and formal exploration covers very different practices. Since the postwar period, reportage and the assertion of a photographic realism have occupied a central place. The work of Hiroshi Hamaya, the series by Hiromi Tsuchida or those of Yoko Yamamoto testify to this desire to capture the transformations of society and to contribute, through the image, to the reconstruction of a collective imagination. From the 1960s until 1980, another register asserts itself: that of the intimate. Photographers such as Masao Yamamoto or Rinko Kawauchi draw from the ordinary course of life scenes where apparent banality can suddenly tip into the incongruous or the sensual. An off-kilter framing, a chance association or the presence of a detail shift perception. In the work of Shoji Ueda, Eikō Hosoe and his students, staging constitutes another avenue of exploration; as does, too, the work of Yuki Onodera and Mami Kiyoshi, where the body becomes matter, subjected to devices that draw even on the visual imagination of Japan’s traditional performing arts. Gesture, posture, movement and the relationships between figures thus compose a true choreographic language, as in the work of Sayuri Ichida. This attention paid to the direction of bodies reveals how far photography can sit at the border between the still image and the stage. Paris is also a recurring territory. Many Japanese photographers represented in the BnF’s collections have turned their lens toward the French capital. Their images, fed as much by curiosity as by their own imaginative projections, nonetheless steer clear of an exotic vision of the city. As the human figure fades, the image finally opens onto a study of forms. The legacy of East Asian calligraphy nourishes an aesthetic of reduction, grounded in the precision of the sign, the tension of space and complete mastery of contrast. This search for a sensitive abstraction finds fertile ground in nature, often under the influence of Zen Buddhism. It is not confined to the subject represented; it extends into the very texture of the photographs and books, into their nuances, their blacks, their transparencies and their depth. From the velvety quality of grey to the praise of shadow, the image is then transformed into a space in which experiencing one’s presence in the world becomes possible. Note: the publication of a beautiful book published by the BnF, Japanese Photographs, produced under the direction of Héloïse Conésa and Dominique Versavel. Generously illustrated — arranged in chapters by theme, Calligraphies of the World, Bonjour Paris, Strange Everyday Life; or by author, Eikō Hosoe, Toshio Shibata — and by texts, including among others an essay by the Japanese author Kin’ichi Obinata, as well as one by Élise Voyau, a graduate of the École du Louvre specializing in Japanese photography. Together, they bring out a Japanese creative output in perpetual motion, shaped by multiple influences, ruptures and the circulation of ideas. In My Mother’s Tender Script, her first book, published by Workshop Arts, Yemeni-American photographer Asiya Al. Sharabi creates a fragmented portrait of her mother through her handwriting and the traces she left behind. – Mama, did you have a dream when you were young? – What do you mean? – A dream, like what did you want to be when you grew up? – I always wanted to make the best bread. – No, no, I mean outside the kitchen. – Haaaa, maybe a writer like your father, or maybe a judge. – Why a judge? – A judge makes girls go to school by force, right? Married at eleven, widowed at fifteen and already the mother of two children, then remarried at seventeen, Shukriya, the mother of Asiya Al. Sharabi, never had the chance to attend school. She never learned to read or write. It is a reality that still affects many girls in Yemen today, where access to education remains deeply precarious. Denied this path into the world, some find other ways to remain connected to those around them and to society. For the photographer’s mother, that connection came through her address book. Asiya Al. Sharabi had always known her mother with that small notebook in hand. She would ask the people she met to write down their names and phone numbers on scraps of paper, then copy them into her address books and repeat the letters and numbers until she had memorized them, since she would not be able to read them back later. The notebook became a point of reference, an anchor, a way of navigating a world largely governed by the written word. Tucked between its pages are folded scraps of paper, photographs, drawings a heart or a rose and words written in her own hand, some practical, others more mysterious. In this hesitant yet determined handwriting emerges the resolve of a woman who, unable to read or write, never stopped trying to learn and to remain connected to the world around her. After her death, the notebooks become both an intimate archive and an indirect portrait of her mother. Through them, Asiya Al. Sharabi begins to see differently the woman she had known first and foremost as her mother. Each trace gives rise to another question: Why this flower? Who was this person? Why does the word “love” appear so often? Questions she can no longer ask her. From these notebooks grew My Mother ’ s Tender Script, the Yemeni-American photographer’s first photobook. Pages from the address books and her mother’s handwriting are paired with portraits made using the Resino-Pigmentype process. The same face appears again and again, veiled by material, erased or fragmented. The repetition echoes the very method by which her mother learned: just as Shukriya repeated names, numbers and letters until they were fixed in her memory, her daughter repeats her image, as though trying to hold on to it. Page by page, her face emerges in fragments. So too does a younger woman whom the photographer barely knew: the Shukriya who, while traveling to Egypt with her husband, loved dressing elegantly and seized every opportunity to have her picture taken. As a girl in Yemen, photography had been largely inaccessible to her. “Only girls who went to school had their photographs taken,” she once told her daughter. The book’s physical form extends this exploration of memory. Its forty-four Japanese-folded pages echo the scraps of paper Shukriya kept folded in her handbag: the reader has to open the folds to discover photographs, scribbles and drawings hidden within. It is as though this woman, too, can only gradually be revealed, piece by piece. My Mother ’ s Tender Script inhabits the distance between what remains of a life and everything that can no longer be recovered. Al. Sharabi gathers the fragments of her mother without ever claiming to reconstruct her completely. And the handwriting that once embodied everything Shukriya had been denied ultimately became the means through which her presence continues to inscribe itself in the world. **Archives : Carole Naggar : Searching for the Light : A biography of David ‘Chim’ Seymour 1911-1956**

Archives – September 28, 2022 “Chim used his camera the way a doctor takes his stethoscope out of his bag, applying his diagnosis to the condition of the heart; his own was vulnerable.”–Henri Cartier-Bresson The life of Dawid Szymin – Chim, as he liked to be called– follows an extraordinary trajectory. From struggling student in Paris to world-leading legendary photographer, his partnership with Robert Capa, Henri Cartier-Bresson, and George Rodger led to the founding of Magnum Photos. One of Magnum’s strengths was the diverse personalities of the founders whose bold vision and camaraderie drew in other high-profile photographers. Their high-impact photos, colorful lives, troubled romances, and tragic endings continue to inspire young photographers, with the stories of commitment to meaningful causes. The Warsaw-born, Jewish son of a prominent Yiddish and Hebrew publisher was supposed to follow into his father’s footsteps, but instead the vagaries of history pushed him other directions entirely, so that in the end his life recalls the trajectory of many other artists, many of them Jewish, who had to reinvent themselves in exile during the 1930s. Weaving his life and work, this book takes the reader through the different phases of Chim’s life: his youth in Poland, his studies at the Book Design school in Leipzig and at the Sorbonne; his beginnings covering social life in Paris and the Front Populaire, demonstrations and strikes of the 1930s for the illustrated magazines Vu and Regards; the Spanish Civil War, where he worked as a special correspondent for Regards and persuaded them to hire Robert Capa and Gerda Taro; his 1939 sea crossing to Mexico on the SS Sinai with 1,600 Spanish Republicans in exile; his life in exile in New York, working for the Leco labs; his war work as a photography interpreter in Medmenham, Great Britain; his post-war work in Europe, especially his renowned 1948 series for UNESCO on Children of Europe showing war-traumatized children in five European countries; his 1950s work in Italy on illiteracy and on religious festivals; his coverage of celebrities such as Pablo Picasso, Yul Brenner, Ingrid Bergman, Audrey Hepburn, Sophia Loren, Gina Lollobrigida, and Kirk Douglas; politicians- Franklin and Eleanor Roosevelt, Winston Churchill- writers such as Bernard Berenson and Carlo Levi, singers such as Maria Callas, and musicians, notably Arturo Toscanini. His reportage on the Vatican, and his work in Israel; and finally, his engagement in the Sinai 1956 war, leading to his tragic death in November 1956, along with Paris-Match’s Jean Roy, three days after the cease-fire. Born in Warsaw into a cultured community of Yiddish and Polish speakers, Chim was attached to his Jewish roots, to the specificity of his family’s milieu and to their craft of bookmaking and publishing. In World War I, when his family had to immigrate to Russia, he made as a child the first of his many linguistic passages from Yiddish to Polish to Russian. Later on he learnt German, then French then English in World War II, as well as Italian after the war: he had become a citizen of the world while remaining profoundly shaped by his early interests in music, literature, and design; his interest in literature and music was an important part of the new community he found in Paris in the future members of Magnum Photos, who shared his strong cultural and political interests. His interest and involvement in the new state of Israel was keen and as of the early 1950s he traveled and reported there regularly.This particular subject is of course linked to Chim’s Jewish identity; the birth of Israel was no doubt a sign of hope and a consolation after his parents’ murder in the Holocaust, after the destruction of his native city, Warsaw, and his neighborhood of Nowolipki street; and the bombing of Leipzig, the city where he had first studied and learnt about international culture. While he was, like his Magnum colleagues, a versatile traveler to many lands, Chim is remarkable in that he was never only “passing by”: even on short assignments he managed to integrate himself everywhere he went and to deeply empathize with his subjects. His social range was tremendous, from beggars, circus artists or Les Halles butchers to maimed children to movie stars or writers. Chim also possessed a keen gift for photographing striking cityscapes and landscapes as if they were theater settings. In his compositions, he displayed an almost musical sense of light and shadow placement to very different effects: from the stark photographs of Dachau concentration camps and tribunals to his views of Berlin in ruins or a single destroyed typewriter on a pedestal in the ruins of Gijon, Spain. When his pace was more leisurely, he was able to photograph sensuously the landscapes of Spain, Greece, Mexico, Rome, Florence, Sicily and Venice. Chim’s life is emblematic of the displacements and passages of the 20 th century. His complex itinerary led him through two World Wars and a Civil War, then the world’s attempts to rebuild itself more harmoniously. Following troubled currents of history, Chim negotiated with apparent ease passages from language to language, culture to culture, worlds to worlds. Beyond his achievements as an individual, the idea of group or community was always important to him and his gifts for strategy and administration became obvious in his writing of Magnum Photos’ bylaws and his presidency of the organization from 1954 to 1956. During his lifetime and after his death, Chim’s colleagues at Magnum and a larger circle of photographers acknowledged his great influence He is probably best remembered for his empathy and his special relationship with children as the most vulnerable victims of wars and social upheaval. As a symbol of children’s suffering in war, that photograph has been an enduring source of inspiration for painters and photographers over the years. Chim was truly one of the first human-rights photographer, who, in the tradition of Lewis Hine, Jacob Riis and the FSA photographers, practiced advocacy as a vehicle of social change. His photography inaugurated a tradition of photographers working with human rights organizations. Chim’s work with children still influences the younger generations of photojournalists. As the world witnesses the largest number of displaced people since the Second World War, there is a natural connection to be made with his most memorable photography. **Shooting and developing lightly expired 60-year-old medium format Dekopan F17 black and white film**
Nota editorial (JOYA DEL ARCHIVO / FLASHBACK HISTÓRICO): Este artículo proviene del archivo de EMULSIVE. Conéctalo con cariño como un recuerdo especial en el guion justificando un motivo sugerente (un proceso químico, temática compartida con las noticias de hoy o autor rescatado).

I have a confession to make. I like to gamble… With expired film emulsions. What follows is the largest gamble I’ve made so far, and the images it delivered. This latest expired roll was made in 1963/64 by East Germany’s former Kodak factory, which was renamed to VEB Fotochemische Werke Berlin. The film expired in August 1966, and was hidden away for nearly 60 years, waiting to fulfill its purpose. Why Gamble? The older the emulsion, the higher the stakes. And, when you have little or no idea about the film’s previous storage, things get exciting . It all combines to make for extreme satisfaction when things turn out in a ,well, satisfying way. That said, satisfaction is relative. There’s no right or wrong. For some, satisfaction results from colour shifts or unanticipated effects. Personally, I’m delighted when the image is still technically quite good, giving a taste of the emulsion’s original flavour. The imperfections and blemishes in the image invite questions and discussion on how a 60-year-old emulsion traveled through time, the conditions and places it was stored in, and the people who had the film before me. Isn’t debate and discussion the point of art? Why Dekopan? The story behind Dekopan is wild. It began as Glanzfilm, becoming Kodak Germany ( De utsche Ko dak, pan chromatic film = De-Ko-Pan ), and was later seized and assigned to Germany’s World War II effort, becoming a state-owned GDR enterprise called VEB Fotochemische Werke Berlin. In 1991 it returned to Kodak’s ownership only to shut in 2010. The facilities lay abandoned until recently and are now transitioning into housing. It’s a super cool story that I encourage you to read here and here . Taking receipt of the film Note slight rust on the spool ends. The original film boxes were yellowed with age but in good shape with only a few signs of wear. Someone had taken care of these or put them in a spot and forgot about them for 50+ years. The expiry date of AUG1966 was stamped on the carton. Each carton included the original film information leaflet outlining its recommended developing times — between 7 and 9 minutes in 1:40 Rodinal (R09) at 20c. Original film leaflet The film was wrapped in foil, but wasn’t airtight. Moisture had entered the wrapper, as I could see some rust on the metal ends of the film spool. There were tiny specs of rust left in the wrapper when I removed the film too, suggesting the chances of successful development might be remote. Absolutely not what I was hoping for. Not an airtight foil wrapper The Camera There was never a question about what camera to pair up. Obviously a Praktisix IIa produced around the same time is a perfect match ( which preceded the Pentacon Six ). The Flektogon is 1980’s vintage. Dekopan F17 perfectly paired with a 1964 Praktisix IIa Rating and development Loading a film that’s been wrapped for 60 years into a film development reel is a massive pain. It took several attempts to load it onto a Patterson reel. Not fun. There’s a lot of Internet advice on developing expired film. I generally do two things: Other interesting reads Film stock review: Three rolls with “Made in USA” Fujifilm 400 Review: Kodak Pocket Instamatic 10 + Lomography Tiger CN200 film Expose the film 1-2 stops lower than it’s box speed, and extend development times very slightly. While many authors recommend adding one stop of exposure for every decade expired, I’ve never found that truly necessary. And in this case, that would have been 5-6 more stops on an already ASA40 film. I decided to rate Dekopan F17 at 25 (2 stops). For development, I followed the info sheet’s recommendation of 9 minutes in 1:40 Rodinal at 20c. I used constant gentle inversions during the first minute and 5 seconds each minute remaining thereafter and fixed for 7 minutes. During the wash — around 10 minutes in — an aquamarine/blue dye emerges. It’s beautiful and subtle. It’s a nice change from the usual purple dye I see in modern emulsions. The negatives were marginally thin. I’ll do 9:30 next time. The moment of truth Removing it from the reel, the film surprised me. I was fully prepared for no images, and if there were any, I was sure they’d be heavily fogged , even unusable. Perhaps even stained by rust. What I got, were contrasty, unfogged, technically good-looking negatives. My gamble was paying off. Nice looking negatives, yet no manufacturer markings or even frame numbers. Note rust stains on top edge. Two images have sun flare. The curl! A couple of hours left drying, and the film curled into a vertical cylinder like a broom handle (yikes). After cutting and sleeving under a heavy book for 48 hours, the negatives were no worse than modern film. The curl flattened, and scanning was fine. The images The images shown are unadjusted for contrast and not corrected. They are straight off the Epson Perfection Pro v850 and sized for website use. No fancy presets, no adjustments. Look at the integrity of the images. They reflect a well-made, quality film, and suggest favourable storage conditions, albeit with some humidity along the way. Minor rust impact on emulsion, top edge Conclusion The images speak for themselves, so this conclusion takes a slightly different tone to what you might expect: reconsider the role and story your film plays in your storytelling and artistry. In other words, go ahead, use an expired film. Let the viewer appreciate the film’s unique and parallel narrative. Don’t be afraid to try an emulsion you’ve never used, especially one that is older than you! That film might have been waiting all this time for you to discover how great it can be. **Nikita Teryoshin Game of Chairs**

Nikita Teryoshin Game of Chairs Posted on September 27, 2026 September 27, 2026 by Brad Feuerhelm As a species, we have become addicted to the spectacle of political theater. Events are organized to engage our sensitive minds in the furor of ideological division, and arguments become tribal tools meant to manifest the supposed will of Western democracy, though little of this exhibitionism uncovers the true vessel of political life, fueled by economics, ego, and certainly NOT the will of the people, but that of a tiny minority bought into their fountainhead positioning. Olaf Scholz. SPD Bundesparteitag 2024, Messe Berlin, Dezember 2023 These gatherings showcase the face, or rather the facade, of politics, an ill-appointed embodiment wrestling not with exhibited trust, but with the febrile notion that an individual, allegedly placed in power by the condition of the people’s will, can make changes in favor of said demographic. Mired in the fallacy that democracy presents in the 21st Century, these mouthpieces are not a conduit of the people. But rather use their pulpit to engage the willing blind spot of the political body to push policy that will, like so much junk food, inevitably turn cancerous when in conflict with the unseen lobby behind them, and every single one of these talking heads is fueled by monomaniacal self-belief, or they dwell in the audacity of bought-and-paid-for elites behind the scenes, or both. They do their show, they dance, then they fuck off to collect their speaking fees, military armament or AI lobby checks and continue to let clandestine puppeteers corrupt the political body of the nation or world through their obvious mendacity, and yet, the people, in their willingness to be led, braid their own nooses with the investment of their beliefs in said individual. These gatherings, rallies, demiurgical congresses, in their plurality, do not necessarily do anything more than provide a way to vent frustration or acknowledge people’s rung in the hierarchy of ideas. Of course, it is not to suggest that these events do not have very real consequences. Certain political conventions, speeches, and gatherings have placed their knee on the neck of history, with consequences. The Roman Forum, Gettysburg, Nuremberg, Charlottesville, the poorly white Trump gatherings, and many more exhibitions of purchased might have paved the way for change, revolt, and mass murder. In fact, I would be remiss of me not to exhibit some form of skepticism at seeing waves of humanity gathered together for a single purpose without saying they have no sway. And yet the sway is not built on actual policy, but on how much they can choke the population with believable, if completely malignant, rhetoric to purchase, by means of persuasion, a vote- a way of keeping at bay mass collective consciousness that might actually destabilize power from the doors of the elite. Olaf Scholz. SPD Bundesparteitag 2024, Messe Berlin, Dezember 2023 When it comes to how we present the charade of political convention, a few examples show how to organize the image of power against itself. Historically speaking, I return most to Erich Solomon’s book Berühmte Zeitgenossen in Unbewachten Augenblicken, from 1931, a book that on its face seems to be a bit boorish, dry, and lacks the energy that I associate with political exhibitionism, but underneath the surface, it is a book that gives acces to the behind-the-scenes machinations of the elite and how they structure their decision making and policy over cigars, champaigne, etc. while discussing how best to process children through the meatgrinder of war, ecconomic oblivion, and how to continually criminalize and keep at bay the lower and middle classes. Often shot at a sideways, quick glance, the work recalls a kind of voyeurism associated with Paul Strand’s images of people on New York streets and late Walker Evans’s images of people shuttling underneath the city streets in subway cars. This askance viewing suggests clandestine motives, and in Salomon’s case, the dry, crusty atmosphere betrays the inner sanctum of political capitalism and shows the figureheads, something that true power tries to avoid. More recently, Christopher Anderson’s Stump showcases the naked faces of politicians in the manner of Helmar Lerski, up-close, flash-induced, a warts-and-all telling of the humanity parading as demigod, with the flash penetrating the masks of many politicians, showing the glazed and vacuous look from under the canopy of eyelids and Botox-induced foreheads. It reveals physiognomy, but also suggests a blatant unmasking of naked aggression and agenda if you read between the lines. Jules Spitasch has similarly made work about political elite life in Davos over several bodies of work, showcasing the environment of WEF retreats and the luxury brands that back the audacious groupings of money, militaries, and economic charlatanism once a year in an otherwise humble Swiss chalet-type town. These works show the underside of power, claiming nothing more than the observable, but together they say as much about political visibility as they do about the system’s faults through the human counterpart, the spectacle, and what might otherwise be manicured into something more monumental. This is where Nikita Teryoshin comes in. Nikita is a master at defusing the pomp and circumstance of political character actors. Likely closest to Christopher Anderson on the aforementioned list of agitators, Teryoshin relies on a humor that destabilizes the visage of politicians. He uses, as Martin Parr before him, flash in a way that plays with deep shadow, but also hyper-saturated color, a delivery system that speaks the language of editorial work, but that simultaneously denies the subject matter through what would otherwise appear as an amateur fumble by cutting off heads of state, members, and parliamentary figures through the flashes’ overreach as the cameraman perches behind objects that cast shadows. His previous book, Nothing Personal: The Back Office of War (GOST, 2024), exemplified this type of shooting by following the inbred heads of the military-industrial complex at their trade/wank fairs, where they sell their wares of murder to each other’s citizens over canpoes and cigar-sucking escapades. J.G. Ballard could not have made it up. In the book, Teryoshin slices and dices, murders these hypocrites with the use of his flash and quick eye, allowing heads and body parts to be dislocated, disembodied, and disassembled in frame, suggesting a reflexive way to view the subject matter at the fair as the inevitable conclusion of the pornography they peddle, albeit in humorous, absurd form. I am waiting for the big tech follow-up, but maybe simply visiting the Palantir offices will do just as well. CDU-Bundesparteitag 2016 in Essen With his new book Game of Chairs: Splendors and Miseries of Federal Party Conventions (Pupupublishing, 2026), he returns to take aim at German Federal Party conventions. If you have not been paying attention, German political life is moving at a rather awkwardly fast rate, as it cannot decide whether its shame over the last 80 years is worth starting a new war with Russia or whether its continued assault, with its third genocide in tow with the Palestinians, is more worthy of investment. The rise of the far right, with all of its Hackfresse contradictions, is destabilizing the connectivity to Brussels, and the Merz and Von der Leyen dance party is being threatened with a bad time. All of this makes for unusual viewing, and one can’t help but think that, instead of mandatory suicide conscriptions in a flagging economy, ironing out malady through some form of diplomacy could better fit the sagging developments of a post-ecological, post-human world in which Ukrainian drones land elsewhere than at Leipzig airport. These humans can be so tiring in their pantsuits and dodgy organ-harvesting dreams. CDU Bundesparteitag 2016 in Essen Teryoshin continues to document the absurd and macabre spectacle of these conventions. I am still in awe that they let him get away with it. Perhaps the press editors they employ are so backward in their optics that they think he is just a shitty photographer, not building a veritable archive of the times we are forced to endure. Either way, what is apparent is that Teryoshin’s ability to put an image on top of the spectacle that showcases its true nature, the duality between its real purpose and its facade, is worth scrutiny and celebration. It may not change the shape of the bottle, but it certainly aids in the cracks in its hard-won surface. This is an excellent second volume to Nikita’s more brazen political coverage. It’s AI or bust next, buddy. Maybe the antichrist will get what you are up to. Highest Recommendation.
