Tintype photography was once a popular medium for capturing formal and casual portraits. Across America, and later the United Kingdom, travelling and studio photographers immortalized countless faces of ordinary people. This repository, much of it hidden from the public eye, waits for archivists and collectors avidly browsing auctions, flea market stalls, and second-hand shops. In this interview, Nigel Shephard of London-based archival project The Family Museum (@the-family-museum) speaks about the history behind the tintypes recently shared on Lomography. Please introduce us to this collection of historical tintypes and tell us how you acquired it. The Family Museum’s tintype collection dates from the 1860s to the 1960s when the medium disappeared from view. By the 1960s it is fair to assume that every family had at least one camera of their own, so the desire for commercial portraiture diminished. Like most of the archive, I started collecting through car boot sales and second-hand stores, but latterly on Ebay and other online sources. Ebay is not only a marketplace but a de facto archive itself, where one can study many examples that one does not have to go to the expense of owning. “While it is true to say that many tintypes are of low fidelity, they can nonetheless hold interest and great charm.” For those not familiar with this historical photography technique, please say a few words about this process and its usage in the past. In America, where tintype photography first took off in the 1860s, tintypes represent an important national record and cultural bond. Their rise in popularity coincided with the desire for durable keepsakes prompted by the upheavals of the Civil War, which began in 1861. The hold they have on the American imagination is evidenced by the fact that more than 10 craft tintype portrait studios have opened up across the country since 2010; in 2019, the director Greta Gerwig had the entire cast of her film, Little Women, photographed in tintype. Yet in the UK, this form of photography has been almost entirely forgotten. [](https://www.lomography.com/homes/the-family-museum/photos/29698804) Credits: the-family-museum American Scott Archer announced his wet-collodion positive technique in 1852. By 1856, the first US patent had been granted and, by the end of the following year, ferrotype plates – the technical name for tintypes – were being commercially manufactured. The tintype trade in America was pursued with great vigor and acumen, but in Britain, to begin with, the attitude towards it was wary. The most influential British Victorian photographers at the time regarded themselves on a noble quest for the advancement of the arts and sciences, and therefore felt that any commercialization in their field diminished not only their profession but also their social standing. As a result, the tintype trade in the UK did not get going until the 1870s, and even then, this was only as a result of American agents travelling across the Atlantic to set up shop in Regent Street, London. [](https://www.lomography.com/homes/the-family-museum/photos/29708246) [](https://www.lomography.com/homes/the-family-museum/photos/29708248) [](https://www.lomography.com/homes/the-family-museum/photos/29717053) Credits: the-family-museum Although the trade survived for 100 years, and fine examples still exist, tintypes were always regarded in the UK as the cheapest and lowest form of photography. The photographers themselves were seen as no more than hucksters and drunken ne’er-do-wells peddling cheap tat to undiscerning punters; like silhouette cutters before them, they thought nothing of setting up shop alongside a pub. Tintypes could be produced ‘while-u-wait’, with the rapidity of a Polaroid, which was all part and parcel of the quick thrills available at fairgrounds, wakes, village fetes and bustling beauty spots. While it is true to say that many tintypes are of low fidelity, they can nonetheless hold interest and great charm. Tintypes typically ranged in size from full plates measuring 6½in by 8½in, descending down in fractions to the smallest size available, the ‘American Gem’. American Gem plates measured 1in by ¾in. As their name suggests, this was a style of tintype imported from the US. Their diminutive size and lower fidelity seems to have been compensated for by the sheer variety and attractiveness of their decorative mounts. At 36 for 2s 6d [2 shillings 6 pence] – less than a ‘penny a pop’ – they represented the cheapest portraiture on the high street. [](https://www.lomography.com/homes/the-family-museum/photos/29708256) Example of American Gem format made in England from the archives of The Family Museum Could you elaborate on some of the photographs in this collection, which, unlike tintypes, are preserved on paper? As regards the term ‘faux’ tintype, they are a discovery of my own. You will notice that all of the faux tintypes date from the 1940s. Because of their size, which is referred to as Bon Ton, and the decorative paper mâché surrounds, they are clearly made by tintype street venders. Although the wet collodion process used to produce tintypes is also used here, the actual substrate is paper/cardboard, not metal. This is because all of the ferris metal was commandeered by the government for the production of munitions during the second world war, so unavailable to tintype photographers, who were called on to improvise to keep their businesses going. [](https://www.lomography.com/homes/the-family-museum/photos/29702719) [](https://www.lomography.com/homes/the-family-museum/photos/29705711) [](https://www.lomography.com/homes/the-family-museum/photos/29702727) Examples of faux tintypes from the archives of The Family Museum Many of these tintypes are framed in colorful paper sleeves. What was the purpose of these frames, and have you noticed any patterns in how specific colors or shapes were used? The first form of photographs collected by the public in albums were known as carte-de-visite, which blew up into ‘cartemania’ in the 1860s. These photos would be slid into precut apertures inside grand, purpose designed leather-bound albums. As I have already mentioned, the most popular form of tintype was the American Gem, measuring less than an inch square. The purpose of the frames was to make them the same size as carte-de-visite so that people could easily collect them in the same way. It was a clever marketing tool to exploit existing practices. As regards their graphic aesthetic, I can observe no pattern other than following the tastes of the times; Victorian neo-classical decorative grandeur, art nouveau, art deco, machine aesthetic, etc. [](https://www.lomography.com/homes/the-family-museum/photos/29717122) [](https://www.lomography.com/homes/the-family-museum/photos/29708255) Credits: the-family-museum What are the challenges and specifics of working with this collection of historical materials? Is it important for you to gain information about the historical relevance of particular photographs and techniques? Although things have slightly changed in recent years, this area of visual culture is woefully under-researched, so it is greatly dependent on primary resources, which is why The Family Museum archive is so important. It is a vast collection, and I am learning every day. **The Questionnaire : Élie Chouraqui by Carole Schmitz**

Élie Chouraqui : The memory of images Élie Chouraqui has never really changed territory: he has changed his way of looking. From cinema to photography, from fiction to history, from movement to the still image, his career traces the same underlying concern: understanding what images say about us, about our time, and about what we choose to preserve. From Mon premier amour, his first feature film in 1978, to Harrison’s Flowers and Ô Jérusalem, his cinema has developed at the intersection of the intimate and the collective, individual stories and the great upheavals of History. In his work, personal destinies are never entirely separate from the world in which they unfold. This attention to reality, faces and memory extends into his photographic practice. For a filmmaker, photography might seem like a way of reducing time: removing movement, editing and narrative to preserve a single instant. But for Élie Chouraqui, photography is not cinema brought to a standstill. Rather, it is another space of freedom, another way of entering reality. The frame becomes more frontal, time more silent, and the viewer must accept what will remain untold. His photographs, particularly those taken in Kenya, reveal this particular sensitivity to light, landscapes, bodies and presence. They suggest rather than narrate, leaving the viewer free to imagine what came before and what comes after the image. Perhaps it is this relationship with time that most deeply connects his two practices. Cinema creates memory through a succession of images; photography concentrates it into one. In both cases, however, the act is one of choosing: one face rather than another, a gesture, a light, an instant, a detail that suddenly becomes essential. Every image is therefore a form of selection—and thus already an interpretation of reality. This question of memory takes on particular resonance in his forthcoming film, Les Héros du Louvre. Revisiting the story of those who, during the Second World War, helped save the works of the Louvre Museum, Élie Chouraqui once again turns his attention to what human beings choose to preserve when everything around them threatens to disappear. Behind the story of the artworks lies that of the people who protected, transported, concealed and passed them on. The museum thus becomes far more than a setting: it becomes the physical site of a collective memory, but also a symbol of the profoundly human desire to save the traces of the past. There is, in this story as in photography, the same question: what remains when time has done its work? An image, an artwork, a face, a memory. Perhaps this is ultimately where the filmmaker and the photographer meet: in the desire to hold on to what might disappear, while knowing that no image can ever fully restore what it claims to preserve. Élie Chouraqui is also a man whose career cannot be confined to a single discipline. Sports journalist, international volleyball player, filmmaker, screenwriter, producer, stage director, photographer—he has always seemed to move from one language to another without truly abandoning the previous one. Each experience feeds the next. Sport taught him movement and observation; journalism, reality and the necessity of looking; cinema, storytelling and duration; photography, silence and immediacy. It is therefore less the story of a filmmaker who also became a photographer than that of a particular way of seeing which, over the years, has found several forms through which to express itself. And if his films tell stories, his photographs sometimes seem to search for what comes before every story: a presence, a light, a fleeting emotion, an instant dense enough to contain an entire narrative within itself. In this questionnaire, Élie Chouraqui looks back at the images that have accompanied his life, from those of his childhood to those that shaped his artistic vision, as well as the photographers, artworks and memories that have helped forge his relationship with the image. An exchange guided by questions of looking and memory, and by those images which sometimes say more through what they leave unsaid than through what they reveal. Instagram: @eliechouraqui Current project: Les Héros du Louvre, in cinemas September 9. Your first photographic revelation?Élie Chouraqui: My hand covering the sun while using my father’s camera. A six- or seven-year-old child fascinated by the discovery of shadow and light. My father scolded me. According to him, I had wasted a roll of film. A photographic memory from your childhood?Élie Chouraqui: A black-and-white family photograph. It was probably taken by my older brother. My mother is in it, with a solemn expression, and my two sisters are smiling. My father is not there, and I have always attributed my mother’s solemnity to his absence. The camera of your childhood?Élie Chouraqui: I wasn’t lucky enough to have one. The one you use today?Élie Chouraqui: My smartphone. The man or woman of images who inspired you?Élie Chouraqui: Sven Nykvist, Bergman’s cinematographer. Curiously enough, because his photography would be unsuited to my own work. What impresses me is its coldness and its lack of humanity. I see in it everything I admire about Bergman and everything I dislike about his cinema. And I admire Nykvist for having respected and understood his director’s vision. The image you wish you had taken?Élie Chouraqui: A war photograph. Saigon Execution, taken by Eddie Adams in Vietnam in 1968. A man shoots another man in the temple. This terrible photograph shows the limits of photography. Adams himself said: “Pictures don’t tell the whole story.” I met Eddie Adams before shooting my film Harrison’s Flowers. We talked about the why and the how, about what came before and after that photograph. We also talked about the way it had been interpreted. An interpretation he believed was wrong. Because in his photograph, the villain is not the man you might think he is. That conversation stayed with me and undoubtedly changed the way I viewed war photographers and war photography. The one that moved you the most?Élie Chouraqui: A photograph of my first dog, Hoppy, whom I happened to find again one day by chance. It brought tears to my eyes. The photograph was imperfect, crooked. Which proves that the emotion of a photograph is connected to its content and to what it evokes. And definitely not to the way it is framed. Nor to its aesthetics. And the one that made you angry?Élie Chouraqui:Tank Man. Tiananmen Square, this young man standing alone in front of a column of Chinese tanks during the student uprising in 1989—the photograph was taken on June 5. It is an image everyone knows. It was taken with a telephoto lens from different angles by different photographers: Franklin of Magnum, Cole of Newsweek, Tsang of Reuters. They took it from the roof of a hotel where the entire press corps had been confined. This photograph says everything about the strength of one man. A kid ready to die to regain his freedom. And all the horror of totalitarian regimes. It echoes what young Iranians—the real ones, not the mullahs—have been experiencing since 1979. A thirst for freedom. A cry. And often death at the end of it. All against the general indifference of what we call our democracies. A murderous silence. Which photograph, in your opinion, changed the world?Élie Chouraqui: None. But if you know of one, I would be happy to frame it and put it above my bed, distribute it everywhere, paste it on walls and shout: “Photograph! Photograph that changes the world!” Just as Éluard wrote Liberté in 1942, and probably changed the world a little, I would gladly write photograph that changed the world in his style: “On the photograph of a schoolchild, on my desk and the trees, on the sand, on the snow,look at my photograph that changed the world.” (I’m smiling, of course.) My mother is in it, with a solemn expression, and my two sisters are smiling. My father is not there, and I have always attributed my mother’s solemnity to his absence. (You have already read these two lines above in response to another question.) As a filmmaker, what interests you most in an image?Élie Chouraqui: That it tells something, of course. I don’t much like images in a film that add neither laughter, suspense, anxiety, release, reflection, emotion or any other profound feeling. Images that are put there simply to look pretty. (Although beauty, of course, is an essential element of an image.) They clutter the narrative. For example, in Les Héros du Louvre, my new film, I made a point of being as demanding as possible with myself so that every shot would have meaning. Can technique sometimes take precedence over emotion in photography or cinema?Élie Chouraqui: NO. Never. Absolutely not. Is beauty in photography, for you, purely aesthetic?Élie Chouraqui: No. An ugly image can convey an idea with tremendous power. The strength lies not in aesthetics but in the creativity of the person who took the photograph and created a new aesthetic for that particular moment, intentionally or otherwise. A photograph is the miracle of an instant, even if that miracle—I’m thinking of fashion photography, posed photographs, and so on—has been worked on beforehand by the make-up artists, hairdressers, designer or advertiser, and of course by the model and the photographer who presses the shutter at precisely the right moment. In cinema, everything seems to have been invented, thought through, pondered, revisited, worked on and reworked long before the word “action.” And yet, after that “magic word” (as my youngest son calls it), nothing may happen—or something completely unsuspected may happen. Something that surpasses the wildest dream a filmmaker could ever have dreamed in his most extravagant dreams. That’s cinema. **Gitterman Gallery : Jean-Pierre Sudre**

Gitterman Gallery opens tomorrow an exhibition of photographs by Jean-Pierre Sudre (1921–1997). A masterful technician in the darkroom, he employed and created innovative techniques that amplified the abstract and suggested both spiritual and metaphysical concerns. Enamored by the wonders of nature and the possibilities of photography, his investigation began in the early 1950s photographing nature. His work evolved to evoke nature’s infinite textures and patterns. In the 1960s, he took his explorations of nature toward the micro, creating crystals on glass plates that he would use as “negatives.” Sudre employed the Mordançage technique on many of these prints, which he invented based on a late nineteenth century process known as etch-bleach. Mordançage solution includes hydrogen peroxide, acetic acid and copper chloride. Once a fully processed gelatin silver print was immersed in the solution, the print became bleached, and the areas with the most silver (the most exposed and thus darkest areas of the image) would swell and lift off the paper base. Sudre would then wipe them away, leaving an outline of the latent image. Next, he would thoroughly wash the print and redevelop it. He used a variety of developers in differing ratios, often making his own photographic chemistry. He was therefore able to achieve a range of colors from the various toners he used and by letting the developer oxidize. His dynamic and expressive use of color became an integral part of his work. Sudre emphasized the symbolism in his work with his titles and by the 1970s he had begun to combine cliché verre images with photograms and even found illustrations. Jean-Pierre Sudre was born in Paris in 1921. He studied at l’École Nationale de Photographie et de Cinématographie and at l’Institut des Hautes Études Cinématographiques. Due to the limited opportunities in cinema, Sudre decided to become a professional photographer. Growing up, his family owned a property surrounded by woods; it was there that he became entranced by nature. In addition to his commitment to his own work, Sudre was an influential teacher in both traditional and experimental photography. He created the photographic department at the School of Graphic Art in Paris. In 1974 Sudre and his wife Claudine, a fine photographer and printer in her own right, moved to Lacoste and opened a research center, later named the Association for Professional Training and Research in Photography, where photographers would spend nine months immersed in photography. During his lifetime Sudre’s work was exhibited throughout Europe, including the Musée d’Art Moderne in Paris and the Palais des Beaux-Arts in Brussels. He was featured in the Museum of Modern Art’s exhibition A European Experiment in 1967 along with Denis Brihat and Pierre Cordier, an exhibition, curated by John Szarkowski, emphasizing color and texture and the physicality of photo-based work. Sudre’s work is represented in international institutional collections, including the Center for Creative Photography, Tucson; Cincinnati Art Museum; Centre Pompidou, Paris; The Gernsheim Collection, University of Texas, Austin; Maison Européenne de la Photographie, Paris; The Morgan Library & Museum, New York; Musée Nicéphore-Niépce, Chalon-sur-Saône; Museum of Fine Arts, Boston; Museum of Fine Arts, Houston; Museum of Modern Art, New York; Princeton University Art Museum; Saint Louis Art Museum; and the Victoria & Albert Museum, London. **Thames & Hudson : Chris Killip : North**

It was on a page of Paris Match that a Cartier-Bresson image– the famous one of a small boy carrying two large wine bottles on Rue Mouffetard in the French capital – caught the attention of Chris Killip, enrolled at the time on a hotel-management course on the Isle of Man. His life took a new direction when he abandoned the management course and dedicated himself to becoming a photographer. The veracity of the street scene on Rue Mouffetard and the attention given to a human face must surely have impressed him because the tangible reality of working life – the boy with the wine bottles, after all, has been charged with a task and he is fulfilling the job – and a concern for portraiture grounded in the unrehearsed are two enduring characteristics of Killip’s photography. Later in life, he would come to be influenced by the work of Walker Evans, evident in what became Killip’s documentary instinct, and in the two photographers’ shared regard for the functional and vernacular. Material entities – bus stops and brickwork, clothes and chimneys – are significant elements in his compositions and they matter because they are not commoditised (indeed, did he ever photograph a commodity?). An inconspicuously ordinary object, like a lamppost, comes to stand out as a part of a built environment that shares living space with a ship’s hulk being constructed in a South Shields shipyard or a boat being attended to in a Yorkshire fishing village. Chris Killip North encompasses the photographer’s output in England’s north-east, north west and Yorkshire, bookended by work on the Isle of Man and Mayo and Galway in Ireland, between 1975 and 1986. The editor in his introduction claims that the photographs transcend the political polarities of Left and Right but this is challengeable when looking at the images at the level of their empirical content and the inequitable material deprivation they so starkly depict. Killip’s work from this period amounts to photography’s equivalent of Vasily Grossman’s dispatches from the Eastern Front during World War II. In a way that is comparable to the Soviet’s astonishing reportage, Killip compiles visual testimony, impersonal in nature, to a social context that insists on being seen: a contextual frame announcing the advent of a prescriptive ideology damaging working-class communities. He photographs a combat zone where the enemy is powerful but unable to defeat the spirit of those on the receiving end of an attritional assault. The vital presence of children in many of his scenes – imbued with tenderness but shorn of romanticism – comes to embody this spirit of resistance and resilience. At the same time, vitally complementing the need for a political point of view, Grossman and Killip both had the ability to attend to individual voices and allow people to present themselves. In their different forms, each of the two artists cast particular people, in a place and time not of their own choosing, whose concretely visible being compels respect. Individuals are pictured in a way that serves as a reminder that the etymology of the word portrait, the Latin protrahere, comes from a verb meaning to draw forth/reveal. But the subjects of Killip’s portraits are showing aspects of themselves that remain outside the photographer’s intentions and the viewer must abide with this. In this sense, his photos do transcend political polarities and cannot be reduced to being epitaphs of the past. They may be photographed in their place of work but their employment does not define them. At an early stage, Killip switched from 35mm to a large format camera on a tripod and this allowed for precision within a pictorial structure constructed around more than one focus of concern. Such a nuancing of what might otherwise seem too masculine is hugely impressive. It shows how the past that Killip’s images preserve is not a realm of simple pieties but a mode of living and a measure of things that is not only different but possibly more worthy of being cherished than many of our own. **5 frames of Night Photography at Mount Tabor with a Pentax IQZoom/Espio 120Mi**

We’ve been here before. For many, night photography conjures up images of big tripods, cable releases, and an external meter with a “serious” instrument like an SLR, medium format camera, or even large format. I sometimes take that approach. But I often find myself out at night with a compact camera. This is not considered the ideal tool for the job. But I’ve gotten great results with my Olympus XA and XA2, so it can be done. But it’s really bucking the trend of “professional” night photography with an all-singing, all-dancing compact zoom from the turn of the millennium. Sometimes I like to be an iconoclast. But sometimes I just use what I got. And what I got in this case is a Pentax IQZoom 120Mi, known outside of the States as the Espio 120Mi. Pentax’s IQZoom/Espio lineup had a staggering number of models during the last couple decades of The Film Era. Many of them are just variations on the same theme of a smallish body, a big zoom, and automatic-everything features. While there’s a few duds and a lack of influencer-favored machines, most of them are good. By this time in photography history the big camera companies knew what they were doing. Released in 1998, The Pentax IQZoom/Espio 120Mi is a great example of a Millennium Compact. The zoom ranges from focal lengths between 38 to 120mm. A lens of 7 elements in 6 groups ensures sharp results, though the middling maximum aperture of f/4.8 (at 38mm) will not inspire confidence in “available light” shooters. That’s why I’ve learned to embrace the flash. The other thing worth embracing is its diminutive size: 105mm x 62.5mm x 42 mm (4.1” x 2.5” x 1.7”) and just 205g (7.2 oz) sans batteries and film. And while not technically a “premium” compact in the vein of a Minolta TC-1, the brushed aluminum exterior is a big improvement over an all-plastic body. The Indiglo-style LCD backlight comes in handy during low-light situations But where most of the Pentax IQZoom/Espio cameras sing besides their excellent lenses is in their feature set. Yes, if you turn it on you are in full auto mode. But these cameras give a little more control than that for when you want to get creative. No, you will not be able to manually set exposure and focus, but that’s okay. (When I want that in a compact, I reach for my Olympus XA.) My most used setting is landscape mode, where the camera focuses at 10 feet (3.05m) to infinity and the flash is turned off. It’s such a useful feature, yet not all compacts of this era had it. Then there are the features that make the Pentax IQZoom/Espio 120Mi useful for low light photography. You can put it in “Slow Shutter Speed” mode, where the flash turns off and the shutter can stay open for 2 seconds. Slow-Speed-Sync Shooting will turn on the flash at the beginning and then the shutter will stay open. To get really creative, the 120Mi has a bulb mode, which can keep the shutter open for 1 minute. (Bulb-Sync will flash first and then keep the shutter open.) Bulb mode on a camera like this is not that scientific, as I’m not really sure what aperture is set. (I’d guess f/4.8 but the manual does not shed any light.) Leave stressing out about reciprocity failure for a different camera. And since there’s not a cable release, the best way to use Bulb is with the Pentax remote shutter release, which are usually pretty cheap on eBay. Or, use the self-timer which is what I generally do for non-bulb shots. Couple this with the smallest Goby Gorillapod tripod that always lives in my bag, and I’m set for some night photography adventures! For this bike shot I used the Bulb-Sync mode, moving the camera after the flash to get that background blur My partner Emee and I went up to Mount Tabor for sunset on a lovely summer evening. Sitting at 636 feet (194m), this former volcano is the highest hill contained entirely in the east side of Portland, Oregon. It’s also a great place to watch day turn into night over the City of Roses. And I had my Pentax IQZoom/Espio 120Mi loaded with Kodak Ultramax 400 in my Bantam bicycle’s front bag, so I decided to have some fun and take some night photographs. While a “real” photographer may point out some technical inadequacies with some of these photos, I think they came out well. Sometimes photography is more about feeling and having fun than technicalities. And a camera like the Pentax IQZoom/Espio 120Mi is about fun. These cameras are still pretty cheap for now, and will not last forever, so grab one while you can. **Cada rostro es un paisaje**

Hay fotógrafos que persiguen montañas. Otros persiguen tormentas. August Sander persiguió personas. Mientras otros buscaban imágenes espectaculares, él entendió algo mucho más interesante. Cada rostro cuenta una historia y una buena fotografía debe ser capaz de hacerla visible. Sus retratos siguen fascinando hoy porque no hablan solo de quienes aparecen en la imagen. Hablan de quiénes eran, cómo vivían, qué soñaban y cuál era su lugar en el mundo. Y eso es precisamente lo que convierte al retrato en una de las disciplinas más complejas y apasionantes de la fotografía. Porque fotografiar a una persona es mucho más que colocar una luz y apretar un botón. Es observar. Es decidir. Es construir. Es conectar.
