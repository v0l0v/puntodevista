**The Beauty of Impermanent Existence: Graeme Williams' Collection of Found Photography**
Fotógrafos: kopanskarchive

Graeme Williams is a professional photographer and avid collector from South Africa whose repository of found photographs has been growing for the past 25 years. Among his collection of apartheid-era amateur photography, Graeme found a body of work that captures the spirit of the time like no other record of the past. In this interview, we speak about the unintended beauty of ‘happy accidents’ and the analogue magic carried by these anonymous photographs. This interview is part of our Archival series, where we invite inspiring voices to share the many ways old and found photography can connect with the present and inspire a new generation of analogue photographers. From the collection of Graeme Williams Hi, Graeme, and a warm welcome to Lomography Magazine. Could you please briefly introduce yourself and your relationship to analogue photography? I grew up in the time of analogue photography. Like so many others, I was drawn to the full experience of making a picture; the beautiful, shiny SLR and the excitement of breaking open a box of film, stretching it through to the take-up reel and starting to shoot. The darkroom was a magical space, with its own chemical smells and gadgets that only wizard photographers seemed to know how to operate. Knowing how to turn a captured moment into a print was a secret superpower and made you part of an exclusive club. We invited you to our Archival series to speak about your unique collection of found photographs. Please say a few words about your archive and how you began it. Being a professional photographer just means that you make a living from taking pictures, but it doesn’t necessarily make you a good or interesting photographer. Now and again, I would come across a picture in someone’s photo album, and it would stop me in my tracks. The image somehow captured something unique and startling about human lives, even if the person who took the picture was unaware of what they had achieved. From the collection of Graeme Williams I started looking for discarded photographs and transparencies in second-hand and charity shops. At first, it was very easy to find them because, when digital photography took over, many people didn’t really know what to do with their old analogue pictures and simply dumped them. Now, after a quarter of a century of digital, I have to spread my net wider in order to source these images. What does your work on sourcing and working with found photographs look like? I would buy up almost any set of photographs that I could find: old albums, boxes of prints, and carousels of slides. It is usually impossible to make a proper selection in a poorly lit shop, and the management generally just wants to get rid of everything. “‘Happy accidents’ or flaws can transform a photographic image from a mere document into something magical – we become aware of the frailty of human beings as well as the fallibility of the photographic process.” I would sometimes arrive home with huge boxes of prints and slides. That’s when the real work begins. I developed a clear idea of the type of feel I was looking for: pictures that capture a sense of the time in which they were taken. But they also have to contain something that places them in the ‘extraordinary’ category. This could involve an odd gesture, an unlikely juxtaposition or some form of imperfection caused by an error made by the photographer, or some interesting chemical abnormality. My hit rate is usually extremely low, and I would often leave out 99% of what I bought. From the collection of Graeme Williams You have previously said that the act of collecting and composing an archive presents a novel approach to the way history is seen and told. Could you expand on this thought? After collecting for close to 25 years, I have recognized that certain patterns have emerged. Some of these are due to my own particular selection process, while others reflect a particular way of seeing the world that could be described as naïve or even amateur. That might sound patronizing, but it isn’t. Most people use their camera to record some significant moment in their lives. During the days of film, they wouldn’t snap away freely because the process was costly. So they would often be very focused on the event they wanted to capture and wouldn’t pay much attention to secondary details. It’s this slightly unconscious approach to photography that has the potential to be extremely revealing. Your collector’s perspective is especially visible in your photography-based projects, such as The Europeans, a book dedicated to amateur photography in South Africa. Tell us some more about the narrative that you have composed in this book and what your work on it looked like. Most of the analogue images that I have collected were taken by white South Africans during the apartheid era. The project name, The Europeans, refers to the terms used during this period to separate white people from people of color – ‘Europeans’ and ‘non-Europeans’. Even park benches were demarcated; signs indicated who was allowed to sit on which benches. From the collection of Graeme Williams The photographs I have chosen offer an insight into how the average white South African lived their lives. Most of them purposely blocked out the reality, or remained blissfully unconscious of the impact this severe political system had on the majority of the population. Is it important for you to get to know the behind-the-scenes story of particular photographs, the history of their authors, and their surroundings? By creating new connections between the pictures, I am building a broad narrative of the lives of white South Africans during this period in South African history. So I have made no attempt to research the origins of individual pictures. On occasions, I have found more comprehensive bodies of work produced by a single photographer. These collections are interesting on a different level because they reveal something more specific about the way the photographer interacted with the world. Usually, though, there is very little information available to trace the photographer or learn more about them. From the collection of Graeme Williams In this collection, there are many images that were altered by the passing of time or feature eye-catching effects such as light leaks or multiple exposures. At Lomography, we like to think of such marks on photographs as “happy accidents”, the unintended effects of the analogue process. Though technically imperfect, these kinds of photos also get your attention and make it into your collection. Why? “Happy accidents” or flaws can transform a photographic image from a mere document into something magical – we become aware of the frailty of human beings as well as the fallibility of the photographic process. I think this is why there has been such a strong pushback against the slick art made by AI. The bland perfection is also soulless. The imperfections that I discover in my found pictures remind me of Kintsugi bowls. After Japanese artisans have repaired the pottery with gold, the item becomes both intriguing and beautiful. Another alluring aspect that I look out for is the ageing process. Seeing slides or prints that have deteriorated or been transformed over time reminds us of our own impermanent existence. From the collection of Graeme Williams What would be your advice to someone who is thinking of starting to collect photographs and work with their archive? Initially, I never set out to be a collector. When I began to discover unique and expressive found pictures, I felt that they contained a quiet truth. The response that they evoke in me is similar to being captivated by profound photographs made by well-known photographers. Perhaps it is the quality that all good art possesses – the ability to communicate meaning across time. So my main advice would be to pursue a collection of photographs that really connects with who you are. Discovering a picture to add to one’s collection should be like panning for gold and finding a nugget. From the collection of Graeme Williams Thank you, Graeme, for your insightful perspective on collecting found photographs and celebrating the photographic culture of the past. **Close Focus at 800mm – Macro on a Super Tele**

Lizards on my doorstep! I’ve tried for two years to get the little guys on camera, but every time I tried to get them into range of my 70mm macro they scurried into the wall. So more working distance was needed. To achieve a long working distance, you need long tele lenses. Sadly there are none for macro photography, it stops at a measly 200mm. So I tried my luck with my trusty 800mm f11 and a diopter. Coming from wildlife photography and wanting to explore the smaller world below without scaring away the tiny critters, the simple solution was the unholy amalgamation of a cine diopter and my 800mm tele lens. It’s just under 2kg and looks pretty sleek The Diopter attaches via a 95mm magnetic filter adapter Testing the Setup The 3-4x Nisi Diopter proved a challenge too great for me. I tried to get even the tiniest bits in focus, but to no avail. So the choice landed on the much more manageable 0.5x Simmod. This resulted in a working distance of 1.4m to 2m and a magnification of about 1.2. Hectic on-and-off screwing of the diopter was quickly replaced by a magnetic adapter ring, and ready we were. No flash or diffuser was used, as I really don’t like the thought of pestering birds with them. Also, it proved best to treat the autofocus as more of a suggestion and focus manually. Handling It’s great fun just plopping on the diopter and start delving into the macro world. Scaring away lizards or dragonflies is a thing of the past, and it’s perfect for a flower and bugs break mid birding sesh. The Good All images were shot on the Canon R8 with this lens. The pictures were edited with Darktable, and slightly cropped. Peekabo! There is something about getting your composition completely on the spot in your viewfinder that I enjoy immensely, no cropping, no zooming, getting to see the movements of the animal up close. That’s what drew me to the 800mm in the first place, I care little for bokeh quality or high ISO. Being there in the moment is what counts for me. It almost vanishes into the bark The harshness of a clean digital image is lost, and the subject softly emerges from its environment. Colour fringes can be mystic veils that give a dreamy quality to the image. The little guy fell asleep Getting a dragonfly this close, filling the viewfinder is magical The Bad Chromatic aberrations can be overwhelming. Direct sunlight is a clear no, and even on a cloudy day, sharpness has taken a hit compared to the original lens performance. In harsh sunlight the CA’s are quite strong The legs turn into orange and blue The low sharpness makes cropping risky Alternatives Extension tubes or a dedicated macro lens would yield much better quality pictures. But the former would have brought my very long (and made entirely out of plastic) lens to comical proportions while being unpractical in the field. And the latter would need to be hauled around in my bag that’s already heavier than I would like. The experience of just grabbing my camera with the one lens and going out not knowing where I will land is unrivaled, but truth be told, in the future, a light tele macro will probably find a way into my or my girlfriend’s kit. **Houston, Untitled by Rory Gardiner**

&nbsp; In Rory Gardiner’s photographs, Houston appears as a city designed for future events. Empty car parks, motorway flyovers, concrete corridors and facades devoid of signage outline a landscape in which the architecture remains, whilst people are temporarily absent. Houston, Untitled, produced as part of the Houston Archive Project at the Rice School of Architecture, originated from a commission focusing on two city landmarks, Rice Stadium and the Astrodome, but Gardiner soon broadened his observation to include the surrounding areas. The twenty-one photographs, taken in February 2026, are not organised by location or type. Even the titles avoid descriptive language, being reduced to geographical coordinates. This choice, almost administrative in nature, contrasts with the atmospheric tone of the images. In 29.749394, –95.358120, a road vanishes into the fog whilst a series of ‘Do Not Enter’ and ‘Wrong Way’ signs transforms the road signage into a system of directions devoid of any apparent logic. In 29.691110, –95.413818, the late-afternoon light casts sharp rectangles onto the nondescript wall of a commercial building. The city takes the form of a collection of surfaces, gaps and obstacles. One can see the influence of American photography by Stephen Shore and Lewis Baltz in the focus on ordinary elements: infrastructure, car parks, service buildings. Gardiner, however, refrains from both celebration and explicit criticism. His portrait of Houston is not spectacular, nor does it aspire to be. Even in the case of Rice Stadium, the monument loses its centrality. Beneath the stands, the repetition of the concrete pillars creates a structure in its own right; small maintenance vehicles, cables and pipes left on the floor indicate the human scale without showing it directly. It is a suggested presence. The same is true of the car park surrounded by skyscrapers, where a small building with an external staircase appears fragile compared to the surrounding towers. Gardiner describes Houston as a city that is difficult to interpret from ground level: flat, repetitive, governed by grids that do not lead to a definitive form. It is in this respect that Houston, Untitled transcends a purely architectural study. The archive does not merely preserve what a city considers significant. It also preserves its in-between spaces: what remains between one event and the next, when the stadium is empty, the vehicle is parked, and the city simply continues to exist. Houston, Untitled is on display at the Rice School of Architecture from 25 September to 5 December 2026.

**False Awakening: Objects Out of Place**

Bottles, boxes, hands and fragments of matter form the starting point for Teona Kokhodze’s photographic series False Awakening. The work explores the moment when a door opens onto a familiar room and a familiar object is grasped, yet something seems out of place. The series does not aim to depict the dream itself, but to capture the instant when reality temporarily loses its reliability. False awakening is a phenomenon that mimics everyday life without constructing fantastical worlds: it replicates what is familiar to the point of making one believe they are awake. Kokhodze takes this uncertain familiarity as her starting point. The objects, arranged in still lifes reminiscent of Giorgio Morandi but stripped of their stability, split in two, lose their substance and are pierced by their own shadows. Photography becomes a tool of distortion rather than of documentation. Overlays, negatives and double exposures produce images in which it is difficult to determine the order of the elements. In one photograph, two hands appear to meet whilst their outlines multiply, rendering the gesture ambiguous; elsewhere, a bottle appears both light and dark at the same time, almost a negative of itself. Even a simple geometric structure made of cardboard is duplicated and intersected by transparent planes, transforming into an impossible architectural form. The series bears affinities with Surrealism, though without its penchant for the spectacular. Closer to the experiments of Man Ray and László Moholy-Nagy, it uses the language of photography to challenge the certainty of the image. Shadows take on the same weight as the objects that cast them, becoming autonomous presences. Black and white accentuates this instability, eliminating temporal references and reducing the world to light, matter and trace. Even in the most abstract images, recognisable elements remain: a crumpled surface, the outline of a hand, the neck of a bottle. It is precisely this alternation between recognition and loss that generates the tension of the series. Kokhodze does not photograph what happens in dreams, but the brief moment when one is not yet certain of having woken up.
