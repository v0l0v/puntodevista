**MEP : PENN & FASHION**

Opening the MEP’s 30th anniversary season, PENN & FASHION, produced in close collaboration with The Irving Penn Foundation and the Nicola Erni Collection, celebrates one of the greatest masters of twentieth-century photography. For nearly six decades, Irving Penn’s fashion photographs— created primarily for Vogue—embodied an aesthetic of timeless modernity in which the restraint of his compositions, the precision of his gaze, and the intensity of his relationship with his subjects reveal an uncompromising artistic vision. From the shoot to the final print—which he always produced himself— Penn devoted extraordinary care to every stage of the creative process, making the platinum-palladium printing technique his signature. As a founding artist of the MEP collection, Irving Penn maintained a close relationship with the institution from the early 1990s onward. Today, the collection comprises more than one hundred prints spanning the full breadth of his career. It has been enriched by the exceptional gift of Tom Penn, featuring photographs that once belonged to Lisa Fonssagrives-Penn—pioneering model, artist, and collector. This exhibition celebrates an extraordinary photographic legacy and the enduring bond between the MEP and this essential body of work. Echoing this tribute, the MEP continues its commitment to contemporary creation by presenting a new body of work by Nanténé Traoré, a rising figure on the French art scene, alongside a selection of works by the American artist David Gilbert, whose practice, at the intersection of photography, installation, and sculpture, is still insufficiently known in Europe. ##### Julie Jones Director, MEP From 30 September 2026 to 17 January 2027, the MEP presents the exhibition PENN & FASHION, a tribute to Irving Penn, one of the most influential photographers of the twentieth century. With a body of work that was always ahead of its time, Irving Penn (1917, Plainfield – 2009, New York) left a profound mark on the history of fashion photography thanks to his collaboration with Vogue magazine spanning over sixty years. His refined aesthetic, like his exceptional mastery of composition, has influenced generations of photographers. The exhibition unfolds chronologically, retracing Irving Penn’s career from 1947 to 2008. It begins in the 1950s, with the presentation of his legendary photographs of Parisian haute couture collections such as those of Dior and Balenciaga, brought to life by the era’s greatest models, including Lisa Fonssagrives and Jean Patchett. In the 1970s, the creations of Ungaro and Courrèges and the extravagant outfits worn by Marisa Berenson or Lauren Hutton caught the photographer’s eye, followed by those of Yves Saint Laurent and Karl Lagerfeld in the following decade. The exhibition also presents Penn’s unique collaboration with the Japanese designer Issey Miyake, whose sculptural garments perfectly complemented his minimalist photographic style. Throughout the last three decades of his career, Penn maintained a consistent approach with an emphasis on the silhouette, volume, and texture. He captured in colour the sumptuous gowns designed by John Galliano and the opulent creations of Christian Lacroix, as well as Chanel’s spectacular feather headpieces, in breathtaking images. Finally, his striking images for the beauty pages of Vogue magazine in the 1990s and 2000s bear witness to the creativity and modernity of an artist whose work is yet to be explored in full. Always faithful to a demanding artistic vision, regardless of the context of the commission, Penn helped to bring art, fashion, and commerce together with unparalleled mastery. His work is characterised by an aesthetic of elegant simplicity. Each image is the result of a long process of reflection, culminating in timeless compositions created using sophisticated printing techniques, many of which have become icons in the history of photography. The exhibition is drawn primarily from the collections of the MEP and that of Nicola Erni. Assembled over the last twenty-five years, the latter is now one of the most extensive and remarkable collections dedicated to fashion photography, covering the period from the 1930s to the present day. The exhibition also benefits from numerous exceptional loans from The Irving Penn Foundation and the Miyake Issey Foundation. Irving Penn was an American photographer born in 1917 into a family of Russian origin. He studied at the Pennsylvania Museum School of Industrial Arts from 1934 to 1938, where he trained under Alexey Brodovitch in his Design Laboratory. Following a year of painting in Mexico, Penn returned to New York and was hired in 1943 by Vogue’s new art director, Alexander Liberman, to prepare layouts and suggest ideas for covers to the magazine’s photographers. Recognising in him “a mind, and an eye that knew what it wanted to see,” Liberman urged Penn to take the photographs himself. In 1948, as the aspiring photographer was building a reputation with his eye- catching still lifes and portraiture, Liberman sent Penn to Peru for a fashion assignment in Lima. Once his task in Lima was complete, Penn traveled to Cuzco where he produced an extensive series of portraits of the ancient city’s inhabitants. This formative experience confirmed Penn’s preference for the controlled environment of the studio, where he could eliminate anything that was not essential to his compositions and focus solely on his subject. Parallel to his obligations for Vogue, Penn embarked on a personal project in the summer of 1949 photographing corpulent nudes and experimenting with their printing through the use of bleach and redevelopment techniques. A year later, Penn traveled to Paris to photograph the haute couture collections and begin his Small Trades series. The resulting images were published as extensive portfolios in Vogue magazine. Many of the fashion photographs feature Lisa Fonssagrives, a remarkable model whom Penn married in London immediately after their work together in Paris was complete. From 1964 to 1971, Penn travelled extensively for Vogue, producing photographic essays on Japan, Crete, Spain, Dahomey, Nepal, Cameroon, New Guinea, and Morocco. In 1973, he closed his Manhattan studio and immersed himself in platinum-palladium printing in the darkroom he built on his family’s Long Island farm. This experience led him to create three of his most iconic series: Cigarettes(1972); Street Material(1975); and Archaeology(1979–80); respectively exhibited in New York at The Museum of Modern Art in 1975, The Metropolitan Museum of Art in 1977, and Marlborough Gallery in 1982. In 1983, Penn re-opened a studio in Manhattan and resumed a schedule of commercial work and magazine assignments. The following year, he was honored with a retrospective curated by John Szarkowski at The Museum of Modern Art, which toured internationally until 1989. After the retrospective, Penn resumed painting and drawing as a creative pursuit, He also found artistic freedom through his thirteen year collaboration with the fashion designer Issey Miyake. Penn’s creativity flourished during the last decades of his life. His innovative portraits, still life, fashion, and beauty photographs continued to appear regularly in Vogue. Determined to shape the body of work he left behind from such a prolific career, he donated his professional archives to the Art Institute of Chicago in 1995 and established The Irving Penn Foundation in 2005. Penn died in New York in 2009 at the age of 92. Curated by: Pascal Hoël, MEP in collaboration with Frédérique Dolivet, MEP. The exhibition is organised by the Maison Européenne de la Photographie, in collaboration with The Irving Penn Foundation and the Nicola Erni Collection. For this exhibition, the artist unveils in exclusivity a new work from the series: Rue de Rennes, Paris, adding to a body of some hundred compositions that has, for over fifteen years, been devoted to urban life in all its richness and diversity: the movements and individuals that make up cities. Internationally recognized for the singularity and mastery of his photographic language, the artist brings a fresh perspective to cities and their transformations. His Time Lapse works form true sociological portraits of the urban world, in which time, movement, and the flows of city life condense into a single image. Far from traditional documentary photography, each composition captures a city at a precise moment while also conveying the multiplicity of instants running through it. With Rue de Rennes, Paris, the artist turns his eye to one of the capital’s iconic thoroughfares. This new composition extends a series that questions our perception of the city, of movement, and of time. For this exhibition, Delamour Gallery is showing a selection of the artist’s most recent Time Lapse works. Other works from the series may also be made available to collectors on request, offering a fuller view of this body of photographic work. Galician photographer Xan Padrón (Ourense, Spain, 1969) received his first camera from photojournalist Enrique Reza, who sparked in him a passion for everyday photography — much as his father, journalist Luís Padrón, instilled in him the patience to listen to and tell stories. After a series of street photography projects in New York (Human City, Motion City, Visions of New York), he began in 2011 his immediately acclaimed “Time Lapse” project: a collection of portraits capturing cities through the people who inhabit them. His “Time Lapse” series has been the subject of solo exhibitions at United Nations headquarters in New York, at the Benrubi Gallery in New York, the Matria Gallery in Milan (Italy), Delamour Gallery in Paris (France), the Pfizer Building in New York, and Sala Valente in Ourense (Spain), among other venues. His “Time Lapse” works were also selected for New York City’s “Art on Link” program. Xan Padrón’s career as a photographer is deeply tied to his earlier profession as a professional musician. For more than ten years, he toured the roads with his bass and his camera, capturing the life around the musicians he worked with. As an artist and concert photographer, he worked officially for APAP (Association of Performing Arts Professionals, USA) and contributed to publications such as Inside Arts and The Writer Magazine (USA). Xan Padrón shares his life with musician, educator, and writer Cristina Pato. Since 2005, he has divided his time between Galicia and New York, and keeps his studio at Mana Contemporary (New Jersey). **Hamiltons : Daidō Moriyama : Ink**

In London,Hamiltonspresents Daidō Moriyama: Ink. From 1968 onwards, Daidō Moriyama became preoccupied with Andy Warhol and his silkscreens. This was furthered by a consequential visit to New York City in 1971, where he further came into contact with Warhol’s work and milieu. This marked the genesis of a process where Moriyama played with the essence of duplication, repetition and mass production in his practice. America’s postwar occupation of Japan and subsequent global cultural hegemony seriously inflected Moriyama’s subject matter. The artist started his freelance photographic career in 1964, the same year that Tokyo hosted the Olympics. In order to prepare for the event, old neighbourhoods were torn down and large concrete expressways and neon-lit commercial districts were erected, rapidly setting the city on a path hurtling toward hyper-capitalism and Western influence. In response, Moriyama’s work centres on the movement, immediacy, transience and frenetic energy of daily life in this evolving metropolis. Images of laundry drying, scenes through the windows of speeding cars and people walking down teeming streets heighten the impermanence of reality and the new brutal pace of life. In addition to the practice of silkscreen printing, Warhol’s preoccupation with ideas of celebrating the mundane and repetition can be traced in Moriyama’s work. As Warhol was recognised for his representation of consumer goods and logos, Moriyama represents advertising hoardings for North American mass-produced consumer goods such as Coca-Cola, Canada Dry and Pepsi and the intensity of their presence in the visual world he was shooting. The Americanisation of Japanese culture and the increased eroticism in high-street advertising became an iconic motif of Moriyama’s work, as did the act of moving through the world and the exchange between individuals and their environment. Another significant influence on Moriyama was Jack Kerouac’s 1957 novel On the Road. As described by Simon Baker, former senior curator of photography at Tate, this led to works where “the photographic image loses its indexical grip on the journey, giving itself over to a delirious revelling in the strangeness of automotive visions: speed, motion, night-time headlights, rain-swept windscreens and the world receding in the rearview mirror”. Moriyama opted not to pause to focus, but rather to travel at the same speed as the motion he was capturing. To capture the world as it is in flux. Made using deep black ink, Moriyama’s silkscreens are large format and either unique or in small editions – a marked departure from his usual open-edition gelatin silver prints. The works span multiple aspects of his oeuvre, from initial projects made in Tokyo in the ’60s to later influences which would push his work to contemporary, international acclaim. Born in Osaka in 1938, Daido Moriyama is one of the few living modern masters of photography from Japan. Many of his early photographs were influenced by the ‘Provoke’ group which produced magazines almost entirely composed of photography. Provoke was more than just a publication but defined the style of documentary photography that arose out of late 1960s Japan. Highly motivated by the opposition that artists felt towards bourgeois Japan. The group set out to create images that didn’t rely on words for interpretation and came to define a whole aesthetic for grainy, blurry and out of focus images. This signalled a turn towards experimentation against the technical precision favoured at the time. Moriyama’s oeuvre and aesthetic reflect his feelings about the destabilisation of social structures in Japan. He captures life during and following the American occupation of Japan after World War II; in particular the effects of industrialisation and the consequential shift in urban life in which some areas were left behind by the rapidly changing city. Beyond the social context, Moriyama was preoccupied and inspired by various international artists, Andy Warhol being the most influential of these. He first saw Warhol’s silkscreens in 1969 on a visit to New York and perceived in them the essence of duplication, repetition and mass production essential to photography. Moriyama went on to make silkscreens of his own, but also made reproduction and duplication a primary focal point of his work, as seen in his repeated attention and production of images of lips, tights, and street signs. Daido Moriyama’s work has been exhibited widely in Japan and internationally including New Japanese Photography (MoMA, 1974), Daido Moriyama: Stray Dog (SFMOMA, 1999–2000), Fracture: Daido Moriyama (LACMA, 2012), William Klein + Daido Moriyama (Tate Modern, London, 2012), and Daido Moriyama: A Retrospective (The Photographers’ Gallery, London, 2023–2024). His photographs can be found in the collections of the Museum of Modern Art, New York; Tate Modern, London; Centre Pompidou, Paris; J. Paul Getty Museum, Los Angeles and more. **Jayne H Baum – JHB Gallery : Scott Morgan**

Jayne H Baum – JHB Galleryprésente une sélection de photographies deScott Morgan. Humankind’s relationship to the natural world and the cosmos has always been at the heart of Scott Morgan’s innovative photographic work. Whether appropriating scientific imaging techniques or documenting actions created directly in the environment, Morgan has used photography and its metaphors as a means of probing the relationship between the material and the metaphysical. With his current project, 10,000 Years, Morgan harnesses the rays of the sun to etch directly onto an array of modern and vintage silver gelatin photographic papers. The interaction of the sun’s rays and the photographic emulsions coating the paper cause complex and unpredictable material reactions—burning holes, singeing the paper with deep gold, oxblood, blue, and orange hues, and drawing metallic silver particles to cluster at the surface in shimmering reflective finishes. The project title 10,000 Years refers to the age of the photons, birthed in the heart of the sun, that daily bombard the objects and surfaces around as light. The spiritual underpinnings of the artist’s methodology, meanwhile, are reflected in the term he has coined to describe his unique process: “Suryagrams”, named after the Vedic god of the Sun. Deftly manipulating light and chemistry, the artist reconfigures the traditional elements of analog photography and darkroom chemistry, drawing out the alchemical energies at the heart of image making and returning photography to its etymological root as “light drawing”. Scott Morgan was born in Patuxent River, Maryland in 1957, and currently resides in Toronto, Ontario and Vancouver, British Columbia. He studied painting, sculpture, photography and literature at the University of California, Santa Barbara on a scholarship for art and writing. After many successful years working in the commercial and fashion photography worlds, Morgan has since fully pursued his own art practice. The artist has had solo exhibitions at Janaki Larsen Studio, Vancouver, B.C.; Museum of the Southwest, Midland, TX; Ricco Maresca Gallery, New York, NY; Tatar Alexander Gallery, Toronto; Van De Griff Gallery, Santa Fe, NM; Foote Cone Belding Gallery, San Francisco, CA; Lithographix Gallery, Los Angeles, CA; and El Camino College, Los Angeles, CA. **16th Gwangju Biennale : Swiss Pavilion : Denise Bertschi : State Fictions. Beyond Blossoms and Borders**

The Swiss Pavilion at the 16th Gwangju Biennale opened to the public on this Saturday 5 September 2026. Curated by Claire Hoffmann and Tadeo Kohan (Centre culturel suisse, Paris), Denise Bertschi’s exhibition State Fictions. Beyond Blossoms and Borders transforms the ground floor ofGwangju’s Eunam Museum of Art into a living archive shaped by contemporary debate, participation and collective memory. Developed from more than a decade of research, Denise Bertschi’s project analyzes Switzerland’s presence on the Korean Peninsula through archival photographs and films. Produced by Swiss members of the Neutral Nations Supervisory Commission (NNSC), a mission established following the Korean War Armistice Agreement in 1953, this archival material is preserved today in Switzerland’s Library Am Guisanplatz in Bern. The exhibition now returns these images to Korea, inviting the public to consider not only what they depict, but also how they were produced, by whom, and in what context. On entering the pavilion, visitors walk through a suspended textile installation combining floral motifs photographed by Swiss soldiers in the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) with slogans found on billboards near the Swiss Camp and in the surrounding Joint Security Area. The unexpected juxtaposition in this installation, entitled STATE FICTION. In Front of Them All(2014/21), contrasting the beauty and innocence of the flower photographs with the commanding slogans meant to enforce security and order, establishes the tensions that run throughout the exhibition. Further inside the pavilion, photographs, films and historical documents are assembled through Denise Bertschi’s practice of montage. An important work shown here is the artist’s edited film STATE FICTION. Neutral Only on the Outside(2021). Rather than presenting the archive as a neutral repository of facts, the artist exposes its gaps and blind spots while investigating the photographic gaze: Who holds the camera? What remains (in)visible in the images? How should we read Switzerland’s efforts to forge a narrative that combines the national myth of neutrality with a desire for geopolitical relevance? These questions continue in STATE FICTION. Fluid Montage(2026), a new large-scale installation that brings together Super-8 film stills selected from the archive for their imperfections and glitches – as if the political, historical and aesthetic tensions inherent in these images had resurfaced onto the filmic material itself – mounted onto a panoramic photograph of the DMZ, a view towards the North. Moving between close-up and overview, the work reveals how seemingly ordinary images can become witnesses to larger histories of conflict, diplomacy and international influence. Magnum Gallery in Paris presents its latest exhibition, Traversées, a retrospective of René Burri’s photographs from his vast archive. Featuring 30 prints and spanning two decades, Traversées — meaning crossings — not only conveys the Swiss photographer’s geographical passages, but his attention to what permeates the human experience and the ever-evolving passage of time. Organized by country, the exhibition showcases a selection of black-and-white images from Burri’s travels in Europe, South America, Cuba, China, and the U.S. from 1950 to 1971. Highlighting both his acclaimed and lesser-known images, Traversées explores Burri’s perspective of cities and cultures during periods of rapid social transformation, and his careful interplay between architecture, form and street photography. Born in Zurich in 1933, Burri studied at the Zurich School of Arts and Crafts. While still a student at just 17 years old, Burri ventured to Paris, attuning his eye to people’s subtle gestures in their built environment. In 1957, two years after becoming an associate of Magnum, 24-year-old Burri began documenting postwar Germany, a divided country grappling with a new political upheaval after the Soviet creation of the DDR. Documenting life in both East and West Germany before and after the construction of the Berlin Wall, his images record a crucial period in the nation’s history; its shifting political landscape and deepening social ruptures. **Petri Color 35 D & Foma 100 – A twin test**

When I see a camera going cheaply in a charity shop, I tell myself that if it turns out to be good it will turn out to have been a good buy but if it doesn’t, then I will have made a donation to a good cause. This just happened when I came across an interesting camera I had never seen before – a Petri Color 35 D. It seemed potentially too good a bargain to be missed. There was a note saying that the shutter had been tested but the meter had not. Inside the case was a receipt for a repair, in 1987, saying that the viewfinder glass had been replaced but there was something that could not be fixed because the necessary part was no longer available. I decided that the price was well within what I would be happy to donate to the charity, so bought it. Naturally, I looked it up on the web, only to find Hamish has reviewed a very similar model (very similar, except mine has a fastest shutter speed of 1/300 instead of 1/250). I decided the battery was dead, but as the manual says clearly that the battery has to be 1.3 volts and my only spare was an SR44, I removed the battery and decided to test it immediately with a roll of Fomapan 100, a film that I had never tried. (A significant issue is that the battery can be replaced only when there is no film in the camera.) The test site was Blanchland, a village built on the site of a former abbey, presumably with stone taken from the abbey when it was demolished, though with a few of the abbey’s buildings still in use. It was a dull day, with intermittent drizzle, so fairly low contrast. Exposure was set using an OM4Ti, though it hardly changed at all. The village is spoiled by parked cars but it is easy to see why it is in demand as a film set. One of the old abbey buildings, repurposed at the pub Probably f2.8 or f4 to test both subject separation and my ability to guess the distance Village centre showing the original abbey gatehouse Close up of gatehouse Juxtaposition of Victorian postbox, with penalty for mailing coins, and the modern notice inside the window Building now repurposed as a cafe Everything Hamish said about the camera is correct (not that I would expect anything else) though like at least one other person on the forum , I ended up more positive about it. It is a camera that pushes you towards treating it as shutter priority, though as there is such a limited choice of shutter speeds, that hardly seems an issue. What I like most is the viewfinder display, showing both focus distance and exposure extremely clearly. The main frustration is having to turn the focus wheel to extend the lens, especially given that there is a bit of play in the mechanism (maybe that is what the repairer could not fix). There is also the need to remember to delay winding on until you are ready to take the picture, because winding on turns on the meter (if there is a battery installed). But the camera is small and I enjoyed using it. On getting home I installed a zinc-air battery and the meter seemed to tally with what a Weston meter said, which was a bonus. My reactions to the Fomapan are mixed. Not wanting to waste time or money on something that might not work at all, I cut off half the film and developed in Rodinal 1:50. Given the dull light and drizzle, I am impressed with the contrast and the tones, though the grain is a bit obstrusive for my taste, especially in plain areas. But perhaps that is the result of pixel-peeping 42 megapixel scans done with a Sony A7R3. It makes me look forward to seeing how similar shots taken on the same day with the OM4Ti on Pan F look. It is possible that results would be better with a tripod, as there could be a trace of camera shake, though the grain makes it hard to tell. I enjoyed using the camera and intend to use it again, next time using its own meter, though my rational self says it would be much better to use my OM4Ti, which is easier to focus, has better metering, and allows me to use a focal length other than 40mm, or even a rangefinder with interchangeable lenses (such as a Leica IIIf, despite its inferior viewfinder). **Saigon and the Delta: A Taste of Old Saigon**

&nbsp; In Saigon, the street unfolds according to its own dynamics, serving simultaneously as a corridor, a market, a living room, a playground and a waiting area. In Dino Mehle’s photographs, this interplay is neither organised nor interpreted, but simply documented. The result is a portrait of the city built upon minute coincidences, everyday gestures and compositions destined to last but a few seconds. Street photography has often sought this balance between observation and chance, from William Klein to Martin Parr’s ability to lend the ordinary a slightly unexpected dimension. Mehle operates within the same sphere without seeking dramatic effects. A man smiles whilst sitting on his moped with two dogs perched in front of him; four children turn a pavement into a football pitch; three men are concentrating on a makeshift chessboard. These are scenes in which little happens, yet the essence is captured. The city presents itself above all as a system of relationships. Bodies, signs, electric cables, scooters, plastic chairs and market stalls share the space without any obvious hierarchy. It is a defining feature of urban life in Ho Chi Minh City, where pavements and roadways continue to function as extensions of domestic and commercial spaces. Mehle captures this density by getting close enough to become part of it, without reducing it to a folkloric element. Colour, too, contributes to the sense of closeness. The deep blue of the chairs and shutters, the red of the signs, the green of the fruit, the almost harsh light of a butcher’s shop: the film captures the imperfections and gives the city a tangible texture. In one of the most direct images, a bare-chested man gives a thumbs-up in front of a row of roast ducks. The portrait arises from the encounter, not from the search for an ideal composition. Subsequently, the rhythm changes. Having left Saigon, the journey continues towards the Mekong Delta and the urban density gradually thins out. It is perhaps in this transition that the work finds its most significant aspect: not in defining a place, but in recording two different paces. On the one hand, a city that seems to constantly anticipate the next frame; on the other, a landscape that invites one to pause. In between, Mehle chooses to proceed with simplicity: walking, waiting and observing whatever comes into the frame.

**“Faux” Panoramic Camera Mods with the Ansco Pix Panorama**
Nota editorial (JOYA DEL ARCHIVO / FLASHBACK HISTÓRICO): Este artículo proviene del archivo de EMULSIVE. Conéctalo con cariño como un recuerdo especial en el guion justificando un motivo sugerente (un proceso químico, temática compartida con las noticias de hoy o autor rescatado).

Panoramas: a great format for (in my opinion) most photography. Dramatic landscape? Make it panoramic. Artsy architectural photo? Panoramic. Wanna make that softball game look a little more like Ben-Hur? Panoramic of course. Weddings? Well… never mind. So as a panoramic enthusiast, and someone who enjoys shooting film, I’ve had my eye on several panoramic film cameras. Unfortunately, a true, full panorama camera can be very costly, like the Hasselblad XPan (>$3,000) or the Fuji GX617 (>$2,000). There are also swing lens cameras, which use a mechanical method of capturing a panorama. The lens of the camera pivots or “swings” in a horizontal arc, exposing the film to the panorama as it moves. This mechanical motion can add distortion to photos which may be a desirable effect for some. A handful of companies produced these cameras, like the Widelux F7 (<$1000) used notably by Jeff Bridges behind the scenes of his films, or the more affordable Horizon Kompkat (<$200). Regardless, what makes these cameras all true panorama cameras is their ability to expose the entire panoramic photo onto film. For example, a standard 35mm film camera exposes an area of 24 x 35mm, meanwhile something like the Hasselblad XPan exposes 65 x 35mm. This uses more film, but results in a larger, more detailed image and a true panorama. However, there exist a type of camera which “fakes” the panorama. The “Faux” Panoramic Camera There are many film cameras that emulate the effect of a true panoramic camera by reducing the area of film exposed to the dimensions of a panoramic photo, cropping it, rather than expanding it. The Ansco Pix Panorama (<$10) is a camera that does just that, exposing a minuscule 13 x 35mm compared to a standard 35mm camera at 24x 35mm or the Hasselblad XPan at 65 x 35mm. The (unmodified) Pix Panorama Along with this, it is incredibly simple, having a static shutter speed of 1/125, a 28mm fixed focus lens, an aperture of f11 and no light meter to speak of. The camera is completely mechanical, the film is advanced, and the shutter is charged by winding a knurled plastic disk. So, with its tiny frame size, nonexistent settings, this little plastic box is truly a point and shoot; hardly a Hasselblad. A few simple modifications to the Pix Panorama would go a long way to improve it. Tripod Mount Yes, this camera even lacks a tripod mount. Something as simple as using a tripod alters how I take photos with any camera, as I spend more time framing and adjusting shots. Now, since the shutter speed of the Pix is 1/125, handheld daylit photos should be just fine but having the option of using a tripod is better than not having it, so I made a mount. The design for it is very simple, in Autodesk Inventor, I made a trapezoid with a hole in it. This hole is a little smaller in diameter than a ¼” brass nutsert. I 3D printed this mount on an FDM printer using PLA filament with 100% infill. When the mount was finished printing and removed from the print bed, I moved it to my bench and used some forceps to hold the nutsert over the hole. Pix Panorama – The tripod mount, a 1-4” nutset, and the tools used Pix Panorama – Heating the nutset Pix Panorama – Applying a bit of pressure Pix Panorama – Nutset is nearly flush Pix Panorama – Flush Pix Panorama – Bottom of the mount covered in double sided tape Pix Panorama – Mount adhered to the bottom corner of the Pix Panorama Pix Panorama – CAD model of the tripod mount I heated the nutsert using my soldering iron, applying a small amount of pressure. As the nutsert heated it melted the PLA and buried its way in, and when it was flush, I removed the soldering iron and let it cool. With the mount complete, I then adhered it to the bottom left side of the camera using double-sided tape. Glue or epoxy would work, too but I wanted something less permanent, as I may alter the design in the future. Although simple, this worked quite well. Filter Holder Filters may not be the first thing to come to mind when modifying such a basic camera. However, since this camera lacks any kind of control for exposure, the use of an ND filter would never be more appropriate. In addition, using color filters when shooting black and white film can enhance aspects of a photo. Whether it be a red filter to boost contrast or a yellow filter to bring out clouds, using filters would be a beneficial addition. Back in Autodesk Inventor, I designed the base of the filter holder to conform to the curved face of the Pix Panorama. I then designed the part responsible for holding 58mm filters. The filters are held in place by two small “fingers” 180° apart from one another, the distance between them is a little less than 58mm, meaning the filters fit snugly in place due to friction but can be easily changed. I made the choice to print this using a matte black filament, so it absorbs light, as it’s being placed around the camera’s lens. In my mind, this will mitigate the chances of light reflecting oddly into the lens if using white or another light-colored/colored filament. Once the models were printed, they were removed from the printer and brought to the bench where they were adhered together using WELD-ON 4, one of the best solvents I’ve found for adhering PLA together. Disaster Much like my life, I expected loading the Pix Panorama to be easy, however disaster struck almost immediately. When loading the camera with film, for reasons unknown, I didn’t believe the film was winding properly, so I hit the rewind release and rewound the film… completely. I wasn’t planning on doing this, I thought I could rewind it just enough to leave the film leader exposed and then try again. Nope. Normally saying goodbye to the film leader wouldn’t be so bad, as I would pull out another roll of film and save the process of retrieving the leader for home. Double nope. Not only was I an hour or so away from home, I also didn’t bring an extra roll of film. In a panic, I began trying everything to recover the leader, using my Leatherman to cut up bits of random plastic and cardboard found in my car to make a makeshift leader retriever but nothing was working. About to give up, I realized that I could turn my jacket into a makeshift darkroom bag by zipping it up, sealing both top and bottom openings by rubber-banding them closed and sticking my arms through the sleeves. Before doing this, I loaded the jacket darkroom bag with the roll of film, the modified Pix Panorama, and my Leatherman. A recreation of the jacket darkroom bag Once my arms were in, I was able to pop one cap of the film roll off using the Leatherman’s bottle opener tool, remove the spooled film just enough to feel the leader, and slot it back through the opening slit in the case. I popped the cap back on best I could and proceeded to load the camera with the roll. With a few prayers said, the camera was removed from the jacket-darkroom-bag, and was ready to shoot. The Final Product and Results The Final Product The Final Product Fortunately, it appears my Macgyvered methods to load the camera worked. I shot a roll of ILFORD HP5 PLUS, which I developed in old ID-11 developer. The age of the developer likely contributed to the grain seen in the photos. Regardless, I’m quite satisfied with the outcome, even if they aren’t “true” panoramic photos, the effect is still impactful. The Pix Panorama also has unique focus, with it being sharpest in the center and some blurring and vignetting in the corners. The use of various colored filters achieved what I wanted, and the tripod mount was incredibly helpful. So, in the end, is there really any reason to use, let alone modify a camera like this? Absolutely. I had a great time designing the parts for it, and I had an even better time shooting and developing the film, even with the initial mishap accounted for.
