**When Color Becomes Tool: Ishmael Claxton on His Methodical Use of LomoChrome Films**

Ishmael Claxton is an African-American photographer now based in Dublin, Ireland who has been utilizing the various tones and hues from LomoChrome films to express different ideas on race, identity and meaning. One of his series, Garden of Eden, uses LomoChrome Purple 35 mm ISO 100–400 film to document youth and moments of transition. Meanwhile, in a recent project, Dead Zoo in Blue, he photographed the Dead Zoo Lab at The National Museum of Ireland and shot the entire project on LomoChrome Turquoise 35 mm ISO 100–400 35 mm film. The work raises questions about decolonization within museum spaces, with a special focus on the dismantling of the Dead Zoo. We talked to Ishmael about his work, the messages he wants to create through his practice and and why he chooses to shoot with LomoChrome films. Photos by Ishmael Claxton ,  Shot with the LomoChrome Purple 35 mm ISO 100–400 Hi Ishmael, please tell us a bit about yourself? I am a visual artist and photographer based in Dublin. I grew up in New York City in the 1990s and early 2000s, in a neighborhood that was first hit by drugs and later by waves of gentrification. Watching a place change that quickly shaped how I think about images, memory, and who gets to remain visible as cities transform. My work moves between photography, installation, and curating with others. I photograph people, spaces, and objects that already carry histories. A museum display, a street corner, or a quiet portrait can all reveal traces of power, migration, and belonging. Photography became the way I sort through those layers. I often mix staged images with documentary moments because real life rarely sits neatly in one category. Photos by Ishmael Claxton ,  Shot with the LomoChrome Purple 35 mm ISO 100–400 Over the past few years my work has been recognized through several awards and exhibitions. In 2023, I was shortlisted for the LensCulture Editors’ Pick. In 2024, my work was shortlisted for both the Taylor Wessing Photographic Portrait Prize and the Royal Photographic Society International Photography Exhibition 166. In 2025, I was selected as one of the 100 Fine Art Photographers of Tomorrow for the Shutter Hub OPEN exhibition at the University of Cambridge. My work has been shown at places including the Museum of Literature Ireland, PhotoMuseum Ireland, the National Concert Hall, the RHA Annual Exhibition, the Hunt Museum, the Royal Ulster Academy, the Irish Georgian Society, Russborough House, and Gallery X. I have also taken part in residencies that helped shape my practice, including PhotoMuseum Ireland, the Center for Visual Storytelling in Santa Fe, Cow House Studios, 3 Walls Gallery, Culture Vulture Autumn AIR, and Queens Collective. I am also a co-founder of ÍOVA Collective, a photographer-led group in Ireland that supports image makers and creates space for new work. Photos by Ishmael Claxton How did you get into shooting film? I first touched film in an after school program when I was in junior high school. We had a small darkroom and I remember watching an image slowly appear in the developer tray. That moment stayed with me. Years later, I came back to film after two events happened close together. A digital hard drive crashed and I lost years of images. Not long after that, my family home burned down. The only photographs that survived both events were negatives and prints. “Color is one of my favorite tools in photography. Changing the film stock can completely shift how we see the world.” That experience changed the way I thought about photography. Film lasts. It sits quietly in a box and waits for you. When I returned to New York, I started taking classes at the Bushwick Community Darkroom, where I was mentored by Lucia Rollow. That time in the darkroom gave me a deeper respect for the craft and the patience film demands. Since then, I have trusted it in a different way. Photos by Ishmael Claxton ,  Shot with the LomoChrome Purple 35 mm ISO 100–400 Tell us about this project and how it came about? I often work with Lomography film stocks because their color shifts push the image somewhere unexpected. The film itself becomes part of the language of the work. For a series called Garden of Eden in Purple, I used purple-toned film to photograph youth culture and moments of transition. I also used LomoChrome Metropolis to photograph crumbling housing and empty buildings during the housing crisis. Color can carry meaning before a viewer even reads the photograph. For queer portraits, I leaned into purple again because it holds softness and tension at the same time. With turquoise and blue tones I began developing projects like Dead Zoo in Blue and Mood Indigo. These works move through museums, archives, and portraiture to question how race, identity, and bodies have been classified or displayed inside institutions. Garden of Eden Series by Ishmael Claxton ,  Shot with the LomoChrome Purple 35 mm ISO 100–400 What made you want to shoot the Dead Zoo in Blue project using only LomoChrome Turquoise film? Color is one of my favorite tools in photography. Changing the film stock can completely shift how we see the world. Turquoise film bends color just enough to make familiar scenes feel slightly strange. Skin tones, skies, and shadows all move away from what we expect. That shift is useful to me. It interrupts our habits of looking. When an entire body of work is made with the same palette, it builds its own atmosphere and the viewer steps into that world. Most of the Lomography film I use comes from John Gunn Camera Shop. The Gunn’s family have become an important part of the photography community in Dublin. A few years ago, I actually asked if they would consider stocking Lomography film. They brought it in, and since then more photographers here have started experimenting with it. It has been nice to see that small shift help grow a local film community. Photos by Ishmael Claxton ,  Shot with the LomoChrome Purple 35 mm ISO 100–400 If the LomoChrome Turquoise film you used for your project, Dead Zoo in Blue, had a soundtrack, what song would capture its mood best? When I look at those colors, I hear jazz. The first song that comes to mind is Mood Indigo by Duke Ellington. I also think of the quiet intensity of Nina Simone and the spiritual depth of Alice Coltrane. Those sounds carry blue in the same way the film does. Slow, reflective, and full of feeling. Garden of Eden Series by Ishmael Claxton ,  Shot with the LomoChrome Purple 35 mm ISO 100–400 What’s coming up for you in 2026? 2026 has already been an incredibly rewarding year and many of the projects I began developing over the last few years have finally taken shape. One of the biggest has been Mood Indigo, an ongoing portrait series inspired by the Irish phrase “duine gorm”, a historical term once used to describe Black people that literally translates as blue person”. Originally conceived as a series of 100 portraits, I decided to expand the project to 120 portraits, marking each year of Irish independence and allowing the work to continue growing beyond a fixed endpoint. Using specialized blue tinted film, the project explores identity, belonging, and contemporary Black life in Ireland. As of August 2026, I have photographed 118 people of color from across Ireland, ranging in age from 6 to 85 years old. The project has introduced me to extraordinary individuals from a wide range of backgrounds and experiences, and those conversations have become just as important as the photographs themselves. I am excited to see where the final portraits, and the relationships formed through the project, will lead in the years ahead. Earlier this year, I also released Dead Zoo in Blue, a project that began during my African Artist Residency at Photo Museum Ireland in collaboration with the National Museum of Ireland – Natural History. The work examines museum collections through the lens of colonial history, asking how bodies, objects, and narratives have been classified, preserved, and interpreted over time. The accompanying publication was launched at The Library Project in Dublin. Garden of Eden Series by Ishmael Claxton ,  Shot with the LomoChrome Purple 35 mm ISO 100–400 Another highlight of the year was curating Black Noise at Gallery X. The exhibition brought together 22 Black artists from across Ireland and became far more than an exhibition. We expanded the programme to include a roundtable discussion exploring how the arts can build stronger communities, a live music jam session, poetry readings, contemporary dance performances, a collaborative zine-making workshop, and concluded with a healing circle. Seeing artists, audiences, and community groups come together across different disciplines made it one of the most meaningful projects I have worked on. Looking ahead to the remainder of 2026, I will be co-curating the 7th annual ÍOVA Photography Collective Culture Night exhibition at REDS Gallery this September, continuing our commitment to showcasing emerging and established photographic practices from across Ireland. I am also developing a new collaborative exhibition, Alternative Methods, which will open at Tøn Gallery in November. Created alongside four female artists, the project explores alternative approaches to curatorial practice by integrating pedagogy into the exhibition-making process. Rather than presenting a finished exhibition assembled by a single curator, we are collectively undertaking studio visits, conversations, and shared decision making, allowing the exhibition itself to emerge through collaboration and dialogue. **In photos: The enduring resistance of Notting Hill Carnival**

Carnival of Resistance — A new photobook by Wayne Campbell and Wayne Crichlow reflects on the protest beginnings of Europe’s biggest street festival, along with the traditions keeping its original spirit alive today. Notting Hill Carnival always evokes sharp visions in the imagination. Intricate floats. The technicolour, feathered outfits of mas bands. Eardrum bursting reggae, dub, jungle and UK garage of the soundsystems. Jerk chicken, curry goat and rice and peas. Huge, pulsing crowds that file through the area’s streets. Or for its critics, it represents disorder, noise, and unpoliceable chaos.But lesser remembered is its place as a protest. In 1958, severe and violent race riots broke out in the West London neighbourhood, as mobs of white gangs armed with sticks, bottles, bricks and knives attacked homes and businesses of Black people. In response, a year later in 1959, journalist and activist Claudia Jones – who was born in Trinidad and Tobago and moved to the UK after being imprisoned in the US in the ‘Red Scare’ – organised a party at St Pancras Town Hall.There were steel pan bands, singers and dancers, as the cultural celebration protested violence and racism with music and dancing. The event was an early blueprint for resistance through joy and celebration of culture, which eventually inspired the first official Notting Hill Carnival in 1966. “There was the murder of [Antiguan migrant] Kelso Cochrane that was dividing communities,” explains photographer Wayne Campbell, co-author of a new photobook Carnival of Resistance, which is published as the festival celebrates 60 years of existence.Read next: Notting Hill Carnival 2023: an oral history of all-female sound systems “A lot of tension existed within the Afro-Caribbean community that had come over just after Windrush, and it felt like something needed to be done,” he continues. “Ironically, what happened came not with knives and sticks but with heritage and culture. And so was born the Notting Hill Carnival.”Ever since, the August Bank Holiday celebration of the Afro-Caribbean diaspora, and its diverse cultures in the UK, has bloomed to become Europe’s largest street festival, growing and iterating across the decades. Yet underneath the noise, outfits and party façade, the original protest spirit remains alive through rituals and traditions.These are the focus of Carnival of Resistance, which is made in conjunction with fellow photographer and Carnival regular Wayne Crichlow. Both photographers have been involved with Notting Hill Carnival since they were young. They’ve been photographing the event for years, with the pair also having performed there in their younger years. Campbell was part of a steel pan band, while Crichlow was in dance music group Closer Than Close.Read next: A look down the side streets at Notting Hill Carnival “It was the main stage, typical bank holiday weekend – it was raining, and I was playing the synth,” Crichlow remembers. “I played this bassline, and I remember playing my first note and the sound engineer on the other side of the field was freaking out, because they weren’t expecting the levels. The particular track was very bass heavy, but it was just absolutely brilliant.”Carnival of Resistance collates photographs that the pair have taken over the past 15 years. Yet it shines a different light on the weekend’s proceedings to the usual maximalist, colour-forward images of Carnival, subverting stereotypical imagery through Campbell’s black-and-white photos featuring just a strip of colour, along with Crichlow’s street shots that embrace grit and reality.“We wanted to remind people that Carnival was all about resistance,” Crichlow continues. “It was about the emancipation from slavery, pushing back against racism, and the community. Carnival is not just about getting drunk, eating your rice and peas and dancing very intimately with strangers – there’s an underlying seriousness.”Read next: On the frontlines of Britain’s ’80s protest movements There’s a chapter on J’ouvert – the Trinidadian originating tradition where people gather before dawn to ring in the carnival with a huge splash of oil, chocolate, paint and more, with the ritual being derived from slaves who would burn sugarcane fields to resist against their slave masters. Elsewhere, there are photos of Jab Jab, where people cover themselves in black oil and charcoal, while wearing horns and chains as direct symbolic expression of emancipation from slavery. One picture pays tribute to Grenfell Tower, which overlooks the celebrations and stands as a reminder of continued inequalities, while another photo shows a girl dancing on her own in the foreground while holding up a plastic bottle, with three Metropolitan Police officers on motorbikes stationary behind her. Despite existing now for 60 years, when Carnival rolls around each August it comes under scrutiny, often with op-ed attacks from the right wing press over its cost to the taxpayer, or complaints by the police about the strain the event puts on its officers. Occasionally, politicians raise the idea of moving the celebration to Hyde Park to make it easier to regulate.Campbell explains that its critics are missing the bottom line. “For the Notting Hill area and that part of the community, Carnival makes hundreds of millions of pounds,” he says. According to research commissioned by The Voice, Carnival’s net economic impact is close to £400 million each year. “Local businesses and the extended area make so much money in a very short period of time. People come from abroad, and it boosts London’s economy, but there will always be a small percentage of people who are sure that it shouldn’t exist, or use it as a political football to say X or Y.”Read next: Photos capturing the soul of Notting Hill in the ‘70s Ultimately, as long as Carnival is treated politically by mainstream society, its presence will always be a protest. “In the ’60s, a lot of those houses in West London were squats, so a lot of people in the [Afro-Caribbean] community settled there,” Crichlow says. “So Carnival is not only keeping the original spirit alive, but it’s actually reclaiming our streets. You may want to pile in a load of money, bring in faceless investors and price us out, but even if it’s only three days a year, we’re going to take our streets back.”Purchase Carnival of Resistance by Wayne Campbell and Wayne Crichlow here.Isaac Muk is Huck’s digital editor.
