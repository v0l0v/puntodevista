Neon signs, dai pai dongs (open-air food stalls), mahjong parlors, nightclubs, bamboo scaffolding, and old-school market stalls all found a place before my lens. Starting out with a single bowl of snake soup, it has not only kept classic Cantonese flavors alive but has also become an unforgettable landmark in Soho. Meanwhile, the historic “Tak Hing Dried Seafood” in Yau Ma Tei protects the warmth of Hong Kongers in a different way, building trust through meticulously prepared soups and an enduring reputation, quietly serving the neighborhood for over half a century with low-key yet steadfast dedication. I have thought about this question repeatedly. Finding a balance between taking a good photo and respecting boundaries is a challenge every street photographer must face. In my experience, when shooting indoor scenes, like the dynamics of people inside a cha chaan teng (Hong Kong-style café), I usually patronize the shop first and take photos on the premise so as not to disrupt their business. One of the keys to street photography is speed. A great piece of work is often born in the blink of an eye. Therefore, before pressing the shutter, observe first, compose the shot in your mind, and then shoot decisively when the timing is right, thereby minimizing interference with the subject. For beginners, you can start with a longer focal length lens to shoot from a comfortable “social distance”. With the rapid development of post-war industry, businesses of all kinds – restaurants, nightclubs, pawn shops – used neon signs as advertising tools, reaching a golden era in the 1980s and 90s. Some local neon signs even set Guinness World Records. In 1973, the National neon sign on the exterior wall of the Majestic Theatre in Jordan was made using over 4,000 neon tubes, becoming the largest in the world at the time. In 1982, the Citizen Watches neon sign in Causeway Bay broke the record with tubes stretching a total of three miles. By 1990, the Panasonic neon sign broke the record yet again, becoming the longest neon sign globally at the time before it was officially retired in 2021. Today, the number of neon signs in Hong Kong has plummeted to around 224, with about 139 in Kowloon and 85 on Hong Kong Island. Over the past five years, more than 16,000 signs (including neon ones) have been removed, turning what was once a brilliant city icon into a vanishing cultural heritage. It feels as though some landscapes are destined to become specimens of a bygone era. Online commentators often note that Hong Kong is no longer what it used to be, losing its uniqueness to homogenization. Some places have witnessed such beauty that people form deep attachments to them, making it difficult to let go even when they become unrecognizable. Since we cannot reverse the tides of time, we can use images as a form of preservation, reclaiming those familiar scenes through the lens. **Poet-Photographer Satellite June on the Intersection Between Poetry & Analogue Photography**
Fotógrafos: basiapodst

As it turns out, poetry and photography share a deeply common language. Netherlands-based creator Nikki, also known as Satellite June, proves exactly that in her new book, The Many Deaths of My Mother. Armed with a typewriter and a collection of film cameras, she set out to confront her grief. Whether in black and white or color, medium or 35 mm format, the reader gets to see images turned deeply personal. In this article, she opens up about the cathartic effects of making art. © @ satellitejune Hi, Nikki! It is so lovely to meet you. I'm really happy to have the chance to interview you on your recent book, The Many Deaths of My Mother. But first, could you briefly introduce yourself to the readers of Lomography Magazine? Hi! I'm Nikki, a film photographer based in the Netherlands, working under the artist name Satellite June. I love finding magic in everyday life – the quiet, often overlooked moments that ask you to slow down and look again. In my work, I build bridges between art and mental health, using photography as a way to explore vulnerability and inner landscapes that are not always easy to put into words. © @ satellitejune Your book is a beautiful homage to both poetry and photography. Tell us, when did the idea of combining these two media start to take shape? It all happened very naturally, actually; a regular day for me is starting the day writing my morning pages and going for a walk. During that walk, I always look around me to find magic in everyday life; shadows that are dancing, beautiful shapes, colors that spark my interest. I always have a little camera in my bag everywhere I go, so I can capture a moment that makes me feel something. My book is a combination of both these habits of mine. © @ satellitejune Looking back, I realized I photographed during the periods when I couldn't explain my feelings. The images helped me understand what I was going through. When life became a little lighter again, the words followed naturally. Writing poetry became another way of processing those experiences. Bringing photography and poetry together in the book felt inevitable – they complete one another. Throughout the book, you use solely analogue photos. Why did you decide to use film instead of, say, digital images? From the moment I started taking photographs, I've done everything with an analogue camera. I never even tried doing it digitally; I fell in love with film straight away. I loved the feeling of being in the moment and learned to trust myself and my vision a lot this way. I am a big fan of the grainy, nostalgic feel film gives my images; it matches the dreamy vibe I like to create. © @ satellitejune When browsing through your published work, we find many self-portraits. Why was it important for you to have them accompany your poems? Photography became an instinctive way of processing what I couldn't put into words. The self-portraits were never about showing myself to other people; they were about finding myself again. During periods where I felt like I was simply surviving, the camera helped me to ask, “How am I, really?” Those photographs became a quiet visual diary of my inner world. © @ satellitejune In the introduction to your book, you wrote that after finishing it, you “feel that you have learned how to hold living loss differently”. Can you tell us more about how completing this creative piece helped you navigate and process grief? The idea for the book came to life when I was browsing through my ten years' worth of photographs and noticed a common thread: I photographed to deal with overwhelming feelings, and in a lot of them, that was grief. When I couldn’t find the words, I turned to photography. Looking back now, I can see that grief has been a major part of my life, and I think most people experience forms of grief throughout their lives as well. “Bringing photography and poetry together in the book felt inevitable – they complete one another.” Sometimes it’s a big wave of grief, sometimes it’s a little splash. Grieving is way more common than we think; it’s just something that isn’t talked about a lot when it’s not the “obvious” kind of grief when somebody dies. There is also grief in ending a relationship, missing an old version of yourself, missing a time in your life, missing friendships that changed as you got older, etc. So, I guess, from making the book, I learned that taking photographs is a way for me to deal with heavy feelings. In my photography, I capture both the hard times, as well as the beautiful, mundane things, and it works really soothing for my mind. © @ satellitejune Loss and grief are deeply personal. What made you share these vulnerable feelings with the world? It took me a long time to find the courage to share this work. The photographs were made during a period when I was trying to survive rather than create. They were never intended as a project; they were a way of holding on to myself. Over time, I realized that the title, The Many Deaths of My Mother , could belong to anyone. You could replace the word mother with health, love, home, or the future you imagined. The details of our stories may differ, but the feelings they carry are often remarkably similar. Sharing this work is my way of creating space for those feelings. I hope the book offers recognition, comfort, and permission to grieve experiences that are rarely acknowledged. If my vulnerability allows someone else to recognize their own experience, to find language for feelings they haven't been able to name, or simply to feel a little less alone, then sharing it feels worthwhile. I believe that the more we allow ourselves to speak honestly about grief, the less isolated it becomes. © @ satellitejune Since we have a lot of photography geeks reading our magazine, we have to ask: what cameras did you choose to work with for this project? Was there any Lomography gear involved? Most of my work is shot on my main camera – the Canon AE-1. In addition to that, I used a Mamiya C3, a Nikon F3, a Polaroid Spirit 600 CL, Kodak Ektar H35, and the Lomo’Instant Square Glass Camera. The photographs in the book are a compilation of my work from the past 10 years, so I used a lot of different cameras along the way. © @ satellitejune ,  Shot on Lomo’Instant Square Glass Camera How about the film stocks? I love how it feels really warm, but also otherworldly, like you’re entering a dream world. Besides that, I used a lot of different films: Kodak Gold 200, Cinestill 800T, Kodak Portra 160/400/800, Fujifilm 400, Ilford HP5, and Polaroid. I love to keep on experimenting and finding the films that fit the mood I want to photograph. Switching between cooler/warmer tones, or more greenish or yellowish tones, adds more depth to my work. I like to think that I “edit” my photographs before I even press the shutter. Choosing the camera and film stock is already part of the creative process; they determine the atmosphere long before the image exists. © @ satellitejune ,  Shot on Lomography Redscale 35mm Poetry is obviously a massive part of the book as well. A while ago, I got a typewriter as a gift from a friend, and I love the feeling of writing on it. The sound is lovely as well, and it fits my analogue approach in my work. It helps you to be in the moment more, to just focus on writing. During the book-making process, something magical happened: on some days, I could just feel that there was a poem that needed to be written. I would sit down and start writing, and it would just flow easily. I couldn’t stop thinking about the book Big Magic by Elizabeth Gilbert when that happened. © @ satellitejune Last, but not least – can we expect any similar projects from you in the future? I'm interested in expanding beyond photography and exploring how grief, care, and connection can be experienced through different materials and rituals.
