**Fahey/Klein Gallery : The School of Los Angeles : Herb Ritts – Interview with Mark McKenna**

Mark McKenna: Memory as a gaze When a photograph becomes an icon, something is inevitably lost in the process: the conditions, choices, and ideas that made the image singular in the first place. Mark McKenna’s role is to reopen that image. A photographer, editor, and leading authority on the work of Herb Ritts, McKenna worked alongside the artist and is now closely involved in preserving, editing, and shaping the legacy of his work. His position is unusual: he approaches the photographs not only as historical documents, but as living constructions whose meaning continues to shift. That proximity matters. McKenna’s understanding of Ritts allows us to look beyond the familiar designation of “celebrity photographer” and reconsider the formal intelligence behind the images: the body treated as volume, light as a sculptural force, the reduced setting, the tension between eroticism and geometry. In this vocabulary, glamour is not simply about seduction. It is a way of constructing presence. That perspective finds a broader context in The School of Los Angeles, an exhibition bringing together Herb Ritts, Greg Gorman, and Matthew Rolston. The title does not suggest a formal school, nor a shared aesthetic. Rather, it points to a particular cultural ecology: a Los Angeles in which Hollywood, music, fashion, advertising, and popular culture converged to create an unprecedented machinery of image-making. All three photographers emerged within that landscape, but they approached it differently. Ritts reduced the body to its essential form; Gorman pursued presence, personality, and intimacy; Rolston pushed artifice toward theatricality, turning styling and fabrication into part of the subject itself. Their differences are as revealing as their affinities. Each, in his own way, understood that the photographic portrait does not simply record identity. It produces it. This is where McKenna’s role becomes particularly significant. His engagement with Ritts is not an exercise in preservation for preservation’s sake. It is an act of critical retrieval: looking beneath the finished image to understand how it was constructed and why it continues to exert its power. What emerges are not simply bodies and faces, but attitudes, desires, codes, and myths. Seen from this perspective, The School of Los Angeles is less a gathering of celebrated photographers than a study of a decisive shift in visual culture. In Los Angeles, photography became inseparable from the machinery of celebrity and, increasingly, from the manufacture of the celebrity itself. Herb Ritts is closely associated with sculpture, geometry and Californian light. Were these simply aesthetic markers, or a deeper way of thinking about the body and personality? Mark McKenna:Herb’s sense of form and keen eye for composition originates from his parents’ influence. His father Herbert Sr. was a furniture designer and ran a successful company designing and manufacturing rattan furniture in the 50s and then transitioned to working with acrylic in the 60s and 70s. Herb’s mother Shirley was an interior designer and was instrumental in the success of the family furniture business. Their design and visual aesthetics extended well outside of the business and were part of their daily lives and home décor.As a young man prior to becoming a professional full-time photographer Herb worked in the family business. The design influences he was surrounded by had a direct impact on his approach with composition and visual aesthetics. Trying to describe what the California light meant to Herb is equal to explaining why humans need water and air. Probably best if this is left to a poet. What I can say, Herb, like a sorcerer of the sun, understood where to place the light and the shadow be it high noon or the golden hour, summer or winter as no other. The light he grew up with was his ultimate tool. It gave him the ability to create an ingenious space for him and the subject to collaborate outside the constraints of the typical studio experience. What did Ritts bring to his subjects that other photographers could not? Why did a star need to be photographed by Herb Ritts? Mark McKenna:Because Herb was born and raised in Los Angeles, he brought an immediate level of comfort and trust with the subjects. Herb’s next-door neighbor was Steve McQueen. At that time Steve McQueen was one of the biggest Hollywood movie stars you can imagine but for Herb it was just his neighbor who would take him and his brothers motorcycle riding and the neighbor happen to have an acting career. With the entertainment and film business based in Los Angeles it is a company town and no different than a town or city with a single industry providing the livelihood for the community. Herb understood this early on and was part of that fabric of the industry.When he started photographing actors and actresses, he didn’t walk into a session idolizing them as movie stars, he understood this was just a part of their jobs, and he was there as an equal professional getting the work done. The subjects always knew he was going to capture them at their best. His images often achieve an almost timeless perfection. Did this perfection erase the individual behind the archetype- or, paradoxically, reveal something deeper? Mark McKenna:Herb did not approach the subjects with a preconceived projected vision of them and certainly was not intending to erase something from the subject’s personality or character. The core of Herb’s approach was highlighting and reveling something in the subject which most of us overlooked even if it became obvious once he captured it. Most of the time he was not focused on a physical attribute, but he was using his lens to capture the “It” of a subject’s persona and the moment they were sharing. Even when he photographed a subject many times over years, he would create a fresh approach in each sitting with an unfettered creative sensibility. Herb Ritts arrived in Los Angeles in the late 1970s, as Hollywood, music, fashion and popular culture began converging in new ways. How inseparable is his work from this cultural transformation of Los Angeles? Mark McKenna:The beauty of Los Angeles is that it is always transforming. Herb, Greg and Matthew arrived at time when the old studio system was no longer fully controlling all the actors and creatives careers and thus giving a fresh approach in how the business of Hollywood was getting done.The old rules no longer applied. Each of them was able to take advantage of this unbridled era to create their own unique path with their vision and image making. Herb’s choice was getting out of the studio and capturing these iconic subjects in common settings with natural light. What was the nature of Ritts’s relationship with his subjects? Beneath the formal control of his images, was there a genuine intimacy with the people he photographed? Mark McKenna:Herb was incredible at fostering a level of comfort with the subjects in his sessions that created an unguarded spontaneous environment with a dance of shared moments between Herb and the subject, there just happened to be a camera with a roll of film between them. This was truly Herb’s gift. Warhol understood early on that celebrity could become artistic material, while Interview helped circulate these new images. What role did Herb Ritts play in transforming celebrity into a visual language? Mark McKenna:With his lens Herb was instrumental in capturing the uninhibited essence and spirit of someone typically only seen on the silver screen at a theater and make them feel relatable to a viewer. Placing Herb Ritts alongside Greg Gorman and Matthew Rolston reveals three different ways of constructing an icon: idealizing, revealing, transforming. Is this a useful way to understand their differences, or does it oversimplify their practices? Mark McKenna:While I don’t think any of these three image makers would want their body of work reduced to a one-word summary, these single descriptives are not inaccurate. They can certainly become the foundation for a larger conversation of their work Can we really speak of a “School of Los Angeles”? Or does the term describe more a community, an era and a creative ecosystem than a true artistic movement? Mark McKenna:This is an interesting question. For me personally, these three photographers are my “School of Los Angeles”. I know I am in a small club of photography assistants who had the opportunity and honor to work with each of them in the 1980’s and eventually staying with Herb until his passing in 2002. That time can be described with all those terms but as someone who picked up a copy of Interview Magazine as a teenager far from Los Angeles, I found inspiration and dreams within the pages of their printed work, it is an era now long gone and not seen again. More than twenty years after his death, what remains radically contemporary about Herb Ritts’s vision? What do today’s photographers and image-makers still understand from what he invented-consciously or not? Mark McKenna:While Herb was a self-taught photographer, he was a passionate observer and collected the fine print work of those that came before him. The common thread in the work he was drawn too was the elegance of simplicity in composition and attention to the detail. These became the defining elements of his own work which he honed to perfection. Text & Interview Carole Schmitz The School of Los Angeles In Los Angeles, celebrity has never been simply a matter of fame. It is a visual material, a language, a construction. In a city whose primary industry is the production of images, identity has always been less a fixed condition than a form of spectacle. The School of Los Angeles, presented by Fahey/Klein Gallery from October 1 through November 28, 2026, brings together Herb Ritts, Greg Gorman, and Matthew Rolston around precisely this territory. The title does not describe a formal movement, nor does it imply a shared aesthetic or manifesto. It names something more elusive: a moment when photography on the West Coast began to understand celebrity not as a subject to be recorded, but as a medium in itself. From the late 1970s through the 1980s, the photographic portrait underwent a decisive shift. The classical portrait had traditionally operated under the promise of revelation: the photographer would somehow penetrate the public mask and disclose the person behind it. In Los Angeles, that premise became increasingly unstable. There was no longer necessarily a person behind the image waiting to be revealed. There was an image to be constructed, manipulated, intensified. This distinction is crucial. Ritts, Gorman, and Rolston did not simply photograph celebrities; they participated in the production of the very visual identities through which celebrity was experienced. Their studios became laboratories in which bodies, faces, light, fashion, gesture, attitude, and cultural references could be assembled into a new kind of photographic fiction. Yet their practices could hardly be more different. Herb Ritts approached the body as structure: he reduced celebrity to the essential architecture of flesh, gesture, geometry, and light, turning glamour into something almost sculptural. Greg Gorman moved in the opposite direction, toward psychological instability, using the face as a threshold between public persona and private presence. Matthew Rolston treated identity as theatre, constructing elaborate images in which the celebrity becomes character, quotation, disguise, and performance. What binds them is therefore not style but a critical position: the refusal to pretend that the icon is natural. Their work belongs to the moment when the photographic portrait became increasingly aware of its own mechanisms. Glamour ceased to be merely an aesthetic of perfection and became a technology of transformation. The studio was no longer simply a place where a photograph was taken; it became a place where identity could be rehearsed, edited and manufactured. This is also what makes Los Angeles different from a simple geographical backdrop. Hollywood had spent decades perfecting the manufacture of stars. These photographers inherited that machinery and turned the camera back toward it. They understood that the celebrity image was already a fiction and that photography could expose, complicate, or deliberately deepen that fiction. Seen together, Ritts, Gorman, and Rolston describe three distinct responses to the same historical rupture. One constructs the icon through the body. One searches for the unstable subject beneath the surface. One makes the surface itself the subject. The three texts that follow examine these practices not as variations on glamour, but as three different theories of the photographic portrait: Ritts and the body as sculpture; Gorman and the face as psychological territory; Rolston and identity as performance. What emerges is less a school than a constellation and, within that constellation, a fundamental change in the status of the photographic image. The question was no longer simply who is being photographed? It was becoming something more troubling and more contemporary: Who is being invented? Carole Schmitz Add to bookmarks Show bookmarks

**Fahey/Klein Gallery : The School of Los Angeles : Greg Gorman**

GREG GORMAN : THE PORTRAIT AS REVELATION What do you photograph when everyone already knows the face? For Greg Gorman, that is where the portrait begins. His subjects arrive before the camera already photographed, reproduced, circulated, and remembered. Their faces carry a history of images. The task, then, is not to identify them, but to disturb recognition to make something familiar momentarily uncertain. This places Gorman at the heart of a problem far older than celebrity photography: how can an artist transform a person into an image without reducing their singularity to a sign? Great portraitists have never simply recorded appearances. They have constructed presence through light, posture, framing, setting, and expression. Gorman inherits this tradition but relocates it within late-twentieth-century Los Angeles, where public image had become a form of continuous production. He works at the very center of that image-making machine, film, music, magazines, advertising, and knows its codes intimately. Yet he neither exposes them nor retreats from them. He subtly displaces them. His photographs are highly constructed: carefully considered light, precise framing, calculated distance. Nothing is strictly documentary. And yet something seems to escape the apparatus. It is within that tension that Gorman’s singularity lies. The subject arrives with a history, a set of physical habits, an awareness of their own face and of what it produces before a camera. The photographer arrives with his frame, his light, his expectations. Between the two, an unstable zone emerges. Gorman is not searching for a “true face” hidden behind the public persona. That would be too simple. He is interested in what happens when familiar habits meet a situation they can no longer entirely organize. Portraiture becomes less an act of capture than a negotiation. This is what helps explain his place within The School of Los Angeles. The term, coined by Charlie Churchward to describe this particular moment in Californian photography, does not refer to a unified aesthetic. Ritts, Gorman, and Rolston shared a place, a generation, a creative network, and the same fundamental challenge: how to give lasting form to personalities whose images were already largely manufactured by the media. Their proximity constitutes less a school than a community of practices around representation. Los Angeles was therefore more than a backdrop. From the late 1970s through the following decades, actors, musicians, stylists, designers, directors, advertisers, and photographers existed within an exceptional creative proximity. The three artists knew this culture from the inside. Their photography emerged from that immersion. Ritts, Gorman, and Rolston nevertheless responded to the same problem in radically different ways. Herb Ritts tended to remove the subject from context in pursuit of sculptural clarity: geometry, California light, simplified backgrounds, and controlled bodies produced almost archetypal presences. Matthew Rolston took the opposite route, multiplying the signs of fabrication—costume, makeup, historical and cinematic references, staging—to turn representation itself into spectacle. Greg Gorman occupies the space between them: he neither removes the persona nor entirely reinvents it; he observes what happens when that persona enters a relationship with the camera. With Ritts, construction tends toward synthesis. With Rolston, it becomes transformation. With Gorman, it becomes interaction. Proximity is crucial here. The close-up is not simply a way of bringing the viewer closer to the subject. It narrows the field of possibilities. The more the face occupies the frame, the less the subject can take shelter behind the surrounding environment. The setting disappears, social markers recede, accessories become secondary. What remains is the face—but a face that is never neutral. An eyelid, a tension in the mouth, a slightly displaced gaze, a shadow across the cheek: these details are not psychological evidence. They are photographic events. They do not tell us what the subject is thinking; they register the way an expression appears, holds, or disappears. Gorman does not photograph personality as one might record a character. He photographs the fleeting manifestation of a presence within a given situation. This is where the idea of “revelation” must be treated with caution. Gorman does not reveal a hidden truth. He makes a possibility visible. Photography does not grant access to an individual’s interior life; it produces an image onto which the viewer projects fragility, strength, solitude, or desire. The portrait therefore reveals as much about our desire to know someone through their face as it does about the person themselves. This ambiguity becomes particularly fertile with subjects who know exactly how to be photographed. Actors, musicians, and public figures know their angles, their expressions, the effects of light and framing. Their media-trained bodies have become second nature. Gorman does not necessarily seek to catch them off guard. Instead, he subtly shifts their established habits: a change of rhythm, an unusual proximity, a conversation, a pause can be enough to unsettle the automatic. What emerges is not some hidden “truth,” but something less prepared. The vulnerability that sometimes surfaces in his portraits is born from this gap. The subject may momentarily abandon the obligation to produce a coherent image of themselves. Gorman does not tear away the mask; he photographs the moment when it is no longer perfectly fitted. Time plays a decisive role here. A long session does not automatically produce a better photograph. It allows the subject to move through several states: the moment when they perform their role, the moment when they grow tired of performing it, and perhaps the moment when another register begins to emerge. Poses become less mechanical, prepared responses lose their efficiency, attention shifts. Time is therefore not simply a way of waiting for the “right moment”; it transforms the photographic relationship itself. This is not, however, an argument for spontaneity. Gorman’s photographs remain constructed. The face is lit, the body positioned, the moment selected. Even the most stripped-down image is a dispositif. Its strength lies in allowing that construction to remain perceptible without making it the subject. This is where his difference from Matthew Rolston becomes particularly clear. Rolston makes artifice his raw material: costumes, makeup, cinematic references, staging. Gorman adopts a more discreet strategy. He reduces visible artifice to allow the subtle variations produced by the encounter to surface. With Rolston, the persona is fabricated before our eyes. With Gorman, it shifts. Portraiture thus becomes a relational situation. The subject is never entirely available to the photographer’s gaze; a degree of opacity remains. The photographer directs but can never fully determine what will appear. A successful photograph does not eliminate the distance between two individuals; it makes that distance palpable. This is why some of Gorman’s images resist psychological interpretation. We are close enough to observe, never close enough to know. The portrait gives us access to a face while denying us the illusion of possessing it. That quality takes on a special resonance today. We live in a culture in which anyone can produce, retouch, and distribute their own image within seconds. Contemporary images are often designed to circulate immediately; Gorman’s seem designed to hold the gaze. The difference is less technical than temporal: what is disappearing is the time required for a behavior to cease being automatic. This tension between circulation and duration also contributes to the lasting life of his work. Gorman’s photographs circulated through magazines, advertising, music, and popular culture, participating in the visual economy that shaped the late twentieth century. Yet once detached from their original context—session, magazine, campaign, or album cover—some acquire a life of their own. The encounter becomes an image, and the image becomes a cultural object. This question indirectly connects to the world of Andy Warhol and Interview. Warhol understood that celebrity could become an artistic material: how does a person become an image, and how does that image in turn acquire a cultural reality of its own? The Screen Tests reduced the apparatus to almost nothing—a face, a camera, time—while Interview reconstructed celebrity through conversation, style, and photography. Gorman operates within the same landscape without adopting the same strategies. Where Warhol exposed the machinery of celebrity, Gorman looks at its interstices: that tiny space between a person and the image they produce. His camera does not dismantle the system; it observes what escapes it. Perhaps this is where his modernity lies. His work belongs to an era when portraiture could still be slow, constructed, and deeply relational, but it is not founded on nostalgia for that era. It simply reminds us that time is also a photographic tool. It allows the subject to move beyond what they already know how to do, and the photographer to relinquish the image they had initially imagined. At this point, Gorman fully converges with the other two figures of The School of Los Angeles, even as he remains distinct from them. Ritts transforms the body into form; Rolston transforms representation into spectacle; Gorman transforms the encounter into a method. Three responses to the same historical problem: how to give lasting form to individuals whose images are already being constantly produced, reproduced, and consumed. Gorman’s answer is neither to monumentalize the subject nor to reinvent them entirely. It is to preserve a zone of uncertainty between the person and the image. That uncertainty is what gives his portraits their density. Portraiture truly begins when the image no longer allows us to believe that we already know the face it shows. **Fahey-Klein Gallery : School of Los Angeles : Matthew Rolston**

MATTHEW ROLSTON : IDENTITY AS PERFORMANCE For photographer Matthew Rolston, portraiture is never a capture. It is a staging. The face does not appear as a truth to be revealed, but as a surface to be shifted, charged with signs, references, and fiction. He does not simply photograph celebrities. He photographs what happens when a person becomes an image. That distinction is essential to understanding his place within what Charles Churchward has called The School of Los Angeles. As design director of both American Vogue and Vanity Fair, Churchward commissioned and closely followed the work of Herb Ritts, Greg Gorman, Rolston and others who reshaped the visual language of 1980s Los Angeles. His perspective is that of both an insider and a historian: he is the author of Herb Ritts: The Golden Hour and contributed the defining essay, “Glamour Reborn: Out of the School of Los Angeles,” to Rolston’s Hollywood Royale retrospective. The connection between Ritts, Gorman and Rolston were not merely retrospective. In 1985, David Fahey –then gallery director for G. Ray Hawkins– brought the three photographers together on a gallery wall for Working in L.A. It was a formative moment, giving visual shape to a generation that was already redefining the relationship between photography, celebrity and popular culture. Alongside Ritts and Gorman, Rolston helped transform Hollywood glamour into a new economy of the image. The question was no longer simply how to photograph a star, but how a star is constructed: how a person becomes a character, how that character becomes an image, and how the image can ultimately eclipse the individual. Rolston grew up inside this mythology and learned how to make its machinery visible. As a child, in his grandfather’s private medical office in Beverly Hills, Rolston was surrounded by images of Hollywood stars. His grandfather was a physician to the stars, particularly MGM figures such as Joan Crawford and Jean Harlow, and the portraits that filled his office had been given to him as personal gifts signed affectionately by the stars themselves, displayed in elaborate frames and scattered across tables and desks. Among them were portraits by George Hurrell, the celebrated master of Hollywood glamour, and László Willinger, another leading studio-era portraitist. Long before he considered photography as a profession, Rolston was therefore absorbing its visual grammar almost by osmosis: glamour, sophistication, artifice, theatricality and, above all, Hollywood’s peculiar promise that a camera could transform identity. Later, he immersed himself in his mother’s old issues of Harper’s Bazaar, extending that education from the intimate mythology of Hollywood into the more constructed world of fashion imagery. His arrival at Andy Warhol’s Interview in 1977 gave this instinct a place to develop. The young photographer had not yet formulated a theory of the image, but he already understood its narrative potential. For his portrait of Steven Spielberg, he did not simply photograph the director; he constructed a science-fiction atmosphere inspired by Close Encounters of the Third Kind. A light hovers above Spielberg’s head as though a spacecraft were suspended just beyond the frame. The portrait ceases to be descriptive. It becomes a proposition. This is where Rolston truly begins: now when the portrait stops asking, “Who is this person?” and starts asking, “Who could this person become?” CELEBRITY AS RAW MATERIAL His work is built on a subtle shift: celebrity is no longer the subject of the photograph, but its raw material. Matthew Rolston encounters personalities whose images are already saturated with signs. Madonna is Madonna before she even enters the studio. Prince is Prince. Michael Jackson is already a media construction. Jodie Foster already possesses a public history. The photographer therefore does not have to manufacture an identity from scratch. He intervenes within an existing identity, fissuring it, shifting it, or amplifying it. This is what gives his images their almost cinematic dimension. They do not seek spontaneity; they embrace fabrication. In Madonna as Marlene, made in 1986, Rolston transforms Madonna into an imagined heir to Marlene Dietrich, the German-born star whose androgynous glamour and sexually ambiguous screen persona helped redefine the image of the Hollywood woman in the 1930s. The reference points specifically to Dietrich in Morocco (1930), Josef von Sternberg’s romantic drama set against the backdrop of the French Foreign Legion, in which she famously plays with masculine dress and performance. But Rolston is not simply paying tribute to a cinematic icon. He layers one image onto another: Dietrich, Morocco, the backstage, the makeup mirror, cross-dressing, classical Hollywood and Madonna, already emerging as the ultimate figure of reinvention. The result is a visual palindrome: Madonna looks back at Dietrich, while Rolston makes us look at Madonna through Dietrich. The same logic runs throughout Rolston’s work. He does not quote the past to illustrate it; he uses it as a latent code, allowing an earlier image to acquire a new charge when inhabited by a contemporary subject. Molly Ringwald, the defining young actress of 1980s Hollywood and a central figure of the so-called “Brat Pack,” is recast as Clara Bow, the original “It Girl,” whose nickname was born from the title of the 1927 silent film It. The quotation is therefore not merely visual: it connects two generations of female stardom, two moments when youth itself became a form of cultural currency. With Jodie Foster, the reference is more oblique. Rolston invokes Dorothy Arzner, the only woman to direct for the major Hollywood studios during the Golden Age, to mark Foster’s own emergence behind the camera. The choice also carries a discreet subtext: Arzner’s life and relationships existed within a largely unspoken queer history of Hollywood. The reference allows Rolston to acknowledge that history without spelling it out. With Cyndi Lauper, the strategy becomes almost mischievous. He places the outspoken 1980s pop star in the visual guise of Mae Murray, a celebrated star of the silent era. The tension between Lauper’s famously expressive persona and the mute, highly stylized language of silent cinema is precisely the point. The quotation works because the two identities do not quite fit. Prince, meanwhile, becomes deliberately unreadable, suspended between masculine and feminine, black and white. Michael Jackson appears crowned before popular culture has fully established him as the “King of Pop.” In each case, the reference is doing more than decorating the image. It creates a collision between identities, eras and cultural memories. For Matthew Rolston, the archive is never inert. It is a living vocabulary. He takes images we think we already know and places them on new bodies, where their meanings begin to shift. The photograph becomes less a portrait than a site of cultural memory in motion. A PHOTOGRAPHY THAT REMEMBERS Perhaps this is what makes Rolston’s work particularly compelling today: his photography is profoundly conscious of the history of images. He does not work within a present stripped of references. He works with the ghosts of cinema, fashion, and photography; images that have accumulated meaning over decades and continue to haunt the contemporary frame. That education took place not only through magazines and photographs, but through places. Larry Edmunds, the legendary Hollywood Boulevard bookstore and memorabilia emporium, has long been a landmark for anyone fascinated by the mythology of old Hollywood. Its shelves and archives offer a physical encounter with a vanished visual culture: vintage photographs, movie posters, books, and ephemera from the studio era. For Rolston, such material was not simply collectible. It was a working archive. The vintage prints found at Larry Edmunds, the portraits by Hurrell, his mother’s magazines from the 1930s, figures such as Dietrich and Clara Bow, all of these constitute less an iconographic repertoire than a kind of visual unconscious. They form the deep archive from which Rolston continues to draw, consciously or otherwise. His photographs are contemporary, but they are never innocent of what came before. Matthew Rolston therefore photographs with memory. This is a fundamental distinction. He is not necessarily looking for the new image; he is looking for the precise displacement of an old one. The practice belongs to a postmodern logic in which originality no longer resides in the absence of reference, but in the way, references are reassembled. The image becomes quotation, mental montage, a collision of temporalities. A Rolston photograph can therefore contain several eras at once. Hollywood of the 1930s meets the pop culture of the 1980s. The contemporary star meets his or her historical double. The present begins to perform its own past. GLAMOUR AS CONTRADICTION Yet it would be reductive to confine Matthew Rolston to the category of glamour photographer. His glamour of that period was never entirely innocent. There is an acute awareness of artifice in his work, sometimes even a form of visual perversity: the more sophisticated the image appears, the more insistently it seems to ask us to look at what lies behind that sophistication. He frequently cites George Hurrell as one of his major influences. But what interests him in Hurrell is not simply formal perfection. It is the tension contained within it. Hurrell once reportedly summed up glamour as a kind of “suffering look.” True beauty, then, is never entirely comfortable. It needs a flaw, an excess, a sense of unease. This contradiction becomes one of Rolston’s engines. Glamour is sumptuous yet artificial. Celebrity is public yet constructed. The character is fabricated yet may reveal something real. Disguise conceals and, paradoxically, reveals. For Rolston, artifice is not the enemy of truth. At times, it is its revealer. LOS ANGELES, OR THE FACTORY OF THE IMAGE This is where the notion of The School of Los Angeles takes on its full significance. In Matthew Rolston’s account, 1970s Los Angeles remained photographically marginal compared with New York. Major East Coast magazines did not yet regard California photographers as natural interlocutors. The situation gradually changed with Matthew Rolston, Herb Ritts, Greg Gorman, and a handful of others. Their work helped bring a sometimes-ironic take on Hollywood glamour back to the foreground and establish Los Angeles as a photographic center. But their shared subject went beyond glamour. It was the image as cultural industry. Hollywood produces films, stars, narratives, bodies, mythologies. Rolston’s photography does not merely record this industry: it reproduces its mechanisms within the frame itself. The studio becomes a set. The photographer becomes a director. The celebrity becomes an actor. The portrait becomes fiction. Matthew Rolston photographs Hollywood as Hollywood would like to be photographed. And sometimes, as Hollywood would like to be believed. WARHOL: CELEBRITY AS ARTWORK His connection to Andy Warhol is crucial here. Discovered by Warhol while still a student, Rolston entered Interview and encountered a world in which celebrity was no longer simply a media phenomenon: it had become artistic material. Warhol understood, earlier than many others, that the public persona could itself become a work of art, and that the manufacture of celebrity could become art’s true subject. Rolston inherits this intuition but shifts it. Warhol flattened celebrity into a Pop surface. Rolston stages it. One exposes the mechanical repetition of the icon; the other explores its theatrical possibilities. And yet they share the same fascination: what remains of a person when their image becomes more famous than they are? That question runs throughout Matthew Rolston’s work. RITTS, GORMAN, ROLSTON: THREE POLITICS OF PORTRAITURE This is also what makes the comparison between Matthew Rolston, Herb Ritts, and Greg Gorman particularly fertile. They are not three variations on the same style, but three ways of inhabiting the territory of glamour. Ritts works through physical presence, the sculptural body, geometry, and light. Gorman takes a more psychological approach to presence and the face. Rolston pushes portraiture toward theatre, character, and fiction. One might almost say that Ritts sculpts, Gorman reveals, while Rolston transforms. But their real point of connection lies elsewhere: all three understood that the icon never exists naturally. It has to be produced. This may be the deeper meaning of The School of Los Angeles. Not a school with a manifesto, doctrine, or shared aesthetic, but a constellation of photographers who understood, at roughly the same moment, that celebrity had become one of the great visual materials of contemporary culture. Churchward describes them as central figures in the return of Hollywood glamour and in the recognition of West Coast photography by major New York magazines. FROM ICON TO SELFIE And then the system turned itself inside out. Contemporary culture no longer needs photographers like Rolston to manufacture its characters on a daily basis. Everyone now has their own studio, their own lighting, their own art director, their own communications department: a phone. The selfie is the paradoxical culmination of this history. What Rolston once accomplished with an entire team—choosing a reference, constructing a scene, styling a body, inventing a light, producing an image can now be experimented with by anyone, alone. The old celebrity was photographed. The new celebrity photographs themselves. Matthew Rolston observes this shift with a certain distance. He recognizes that figures such as Kim Kardashian no longer need a photographer to reinvent their persona; they continuously produce their own visual mythology. Yet this transformation does not make his work obsolete. On the contrary, it allows us to understand it more clearly. Rolston had already identified, long before the explosion of social media, what our era has since generalized: identity is a visual construction. We are no longer simply what we are. We are also the way we choose to appear. THE IMAGE AFTER THE PERSON Ultimately, this is where Matthew Rolston’s critical force lies. His photographs of the 1980’s speak less about celebrity than about the progressive disappearance of the boundary between person and character. He does not ask his subjects to be themselves. He gives them the possibility of being several people at once. Madonna becomes Dietrich in order to become Madonna again. Prince becomes indeterminate so that his identity can precisely escape definition. Michael Jackson becomes king before the title is officially bestowed upon him. Jodie Foster becomes Dorothy Arzner. Each time, the invented character does not necessarily conceal identity: it expands it. Matthew Rolston does not photograph identity. He photographs its potential for transformation. Perhaps this is what distinguishes his work from straightforward glamour photography. Glamour becomes a language, costume a syntax, reference a form of memory, and the face a territory of fiction. At a time when everyone can become their own photographer, stylist, and director, his work takes on an almost archaeological dimension. It preserves the memory of a moment when the manufacture of the icon was still a profession. But it also contains something prophetic. What Rolston staged in the 1980s, a transformed identity, a face turned, tongue-in-cheek, into a character, a person turned into an image, has now become one of the ordinary gestures of contemporary culture. The difference may simply be this: Matthew Rolston knew that it was a performance. Carole Schmitz Add to bookmarks Show bookmarks

**Mouraux Durand-Ruel Gallery : Lucien Clergue : Written by the Sun**

For its inaugural exhibition, Mouraux Durand-Ruel Gallery presentsLucien Clergue: Written by the Sun, on view from September 23 to October 24 at 555 West 25th Street in Chelsea. Bringing together 50 photographs spanning 50 years of photography, from the 1950s to the early 2000s, the exhibition traces the different worlds through which Clergue developed his photographic language: theater and memory, music and community, and the interplay of light, poetry, and the human body. The exhibition takes its title from the Mediterranean light that shaped his vision, from the early Saltimbanques and Gypsies series to Brasília, his celebrated nudes, and intimate portraits of Pablo Picasso and Jean Cocteau. Clergue’s stature in French photography is without parallel. He was the first photographer elected to France’s Académie des Beaux-Arts and the founder of the Rencontres d’Arles, one of the world’s foremost photography festivals. Born in Arles in 1934, his love for photography started at a young age. At thirteen, he received his first camera and at nineteen, he met Pablo Picasso at a bullfight in the Roman arena of Arles. Over the next two decades, Clergue photographed the artist from the villa La Californie in Cannes to Notre-Dame-de-Vie in Mougins, where he made his final portrait of Picasso in 1971. These photographs reveal the artist at work, at rest, and in moments of quiet intimacy, reflecting the trust that existed between the two men. Among the earliest works in the exhibition are Clergue’s Saltimbanques, begun in 1954. Fascinated by theater from a young age, he staged children as Harlequins, Pierrots, violinists, and circus performers among the ruins of postwar Arles, creating poetic tableaux shaped by memory and loss. The following year, Clergue turned his attention to the Gypsies. Having grown up alongside families from the community in Arles, he photographed them with unusual intimacy. Clergue deliberately resisted presenting their lives primarily through hardship. What fascinated him was movement: dance, music, rituals. “Gypsies are people to whom the present only exists,” he later wrote. His photographs captured the musical composition of these scenes. These photographs led to his friendship with Jean Cocteau, whom Picasso introduced to Clergue in 1956. Cocteau was one of the great multidisciplinary artists of twentieth-century French culture. Cocteau described him as a “poet photographer.” In 1959, he invited Clergue to photograph the making of his final film, Le Testament d’Orphée, giving him complete freedom to interpret what he saw. Clergue’s photographs inhabit the same boundary between reality and invention. Portraits of Cocteau directing the movie coexist with theatrical visions of mythological characters. A radically different landscape appears in Brasília. Captured in 1963, these photographs depicted the newly created capital through Oscar Niemeyer’s monumental architecture. Clergue recognized in Niemeyer’s architecture a language close to his own treatment of the female body. Abstract compositions of light, concrete, curve, and volume appear. The Brasília series is unique, as Clergue was really attached to these ones and printed them the last year of his life. Very few lifetime prints from this body of work exist today. That dialogue between body, landscape, and light lies at the heart of Clergue’s iconic Nudes of the Sea. Photographed along the shores of the Camargue in France, these close-up images transform the female body into an elemental landscape. Emerging from the surf, the figures become timeless symbols of nature, sensuality, and the Mediterranean Sea. Water continually reshapes the body, dissolving the boundary between the human figure and the natural world. These photographs helped establish Clergue internationally as Edward Steichen acquired nine of his prints for the Museum of Modern Art in 1960, where Clergue was invited to exhibit the following year in Diogenes with a Camera V (September 26 to November 12, 1961). If the Nudes of the Sea belong to the open horizons of the Mediterranean, Clergue’s later Zebra Nudes shift into an urban interior. Inspired by Venetian blinds in a New York apartment, the series uses bands of light and shadow to fragment and reconstruct the female body. Here, light no longer simply reveals the figure; it draws directly across the skin and becomes the image’s principal subject. “Opening with Lucien Clergue felt inevitable. His work is deeply connected to the South of France, a region that is particularly close to my heart, but this exhibition also reveals his innovative vision. I wanted to reintroduce his creations to an American audience, and especially to New York, a city that played an important role in his career. I hope this exhibition will encourage a new generation to rediscover his work,” shared Victoria Mouraux Durand-Ruel. This exhibition has been organized in close collaboration with Anne Clergue, the artist’s daughter, who directs the Atelier Lucien Clergue in Arles and oversees the preservation and promotion of his work through exhibitions, publications, and cultural projects. **Two Cameras, One City – What Gear Choice Taught Me About Shooting Barcelona in Black and White**
Nota editorial: Cobertura cruzada detectada. Este tema o artista también se publica hoy en: EMULSIVE

I’ve been photographing for twenty-five years, and for most of that time I’ve lived just outside Barcelona, in Castelldefels — close enough to be in the city in twenty minutes, far enough that I never take it for granted. This year I finally did something I’d been circling for a while: a black-and-white project built entirely around one idea — contrast — shot across the two Barcelonas that don’t usually end up in the same photo essay: the eight-century-old Gothic Quarter, and the glass-and-steel city being built around it right now. I didn’t go into it expecting a piece about gear. I came out of it with one anyway, because the split ended up being less about taste and more about which camera actually earns its place in the bag for a given kind of light. Gothic Quarter, Barcelona. Shot on the Leica Q3. Why Two Cameras, Not One I shoot with a Sony a7R IV and a Leica Q3, and for most of my work I don’t think too hard about which one comes out of the bag. For this project, the split became almost a rule of its own — not a stylistic flourish, but a response to two completely different sets of shooting conditions that happened to sit five kilometres apart in the same city. The Gothic Quarter is close, tight, and full of people — narrow lanes, sudden shadows, someone stepping into frame with about half a second of warning. That’s Q3 territory. It’s quiet, it’s fast to bring up, and — this matters more than people admit — it doesn’t look like a “serious camera” to a stranger on the street. Nobody tenses up. The fixed 28mm forces the compositional decision before you even lift the camera, which turns out to matter a lot when you have one chance and no time to zoom your way out of a bad frame. Gothic Quarter at night. Leica Q3. The contemporary architecture chapter was the opposite problem. Buildings like the old Montjuïc communications tower or MACBA’s sculptural white walls need to be photographed against real dynamic range — blown-out sky in one corner of the frame, deep shadow in another, often at the same time. That’s where the a7R IV earns its keep. The extra resolution meant I could crop aggressively in post when a composition needed tightening, and the sensor held detail in both the highlights and the shadows in a way I don’t think the Q3 would have managed as cleanly at that scale. Montjuïc communications tower. Sony a7R IV. MACBA, Barcelona. Sony a7R IV. The Q3: Shooting for a person The Leica’s whole case for itself, for me, is discretion plus commitment. No zoom, no silent second-guessing about focal length, a shutter you barely hear. I got a shot of a mannequin dressed as Chaplin reflected in a shop window at night, layered with bokeh and a passerby, that I’m fairly sure I wouldn’t have gotten with anything bigger or slower — by the time a larger body would have focused and fired, the moment reflected in that glass would have moved on. The Q3 rewards standing still and waiting, because the 28mm doesn’t give you anywhere else to hide compositionally. You either found the frame or you didn’t. A Chaplin mannequin reflected in a Gothic Quarter shop window. Leica Q3. Sant Pere, Barcelona, at night. Leica Q3. The A7R IV – Shooting for a Building The Sony’s case is almost the inverse. Architecture doesn’t move, but it also doesn’t compromise on how much of the tonal range you need to hold onto. A brutalist tower in the Raval, its facade a grid of small irregular window openings with graffiti and protest text scrawled floor-to-ceiling at its base, needed both ends of the histogram intact in a single frame — sky, glass, and shadowed concrete all exposed correctly at once. That’s a sensor problem, not a composition problem, and it’s the one thing the extra resolution and dynamic range headroom solved cleanly, letting me tighten the crop afterward without shredding detail in the process. El Raval, Barcelona. Sony a7R IV. Barcelona. Sony a7R IV. Gear as a Method, Not Identity I’m not precious about gear-as-identity — I don’t think either camera is “better,” they’re just built for different kinds of waiting. The Leica is for waiting for a person. The Sony is for waiting for a building to give you its best light. What surprised me, going into this project without a thesis, is how much that distinction ended up organising the whole shoot before I’d consciously decided anything: I found myself reaching for one camera or the other based on the shape of the wait, not the subject. Barcelona. What I Took from it Going back through my own archive while assembling this project, I noticed something that had nothing to do with either camera directly: the photographs I still liked a year later were almost always the ones that would have worked fine without colour — meaning the composition and the light were doing the actual work, not the sky. Twenty-five years in, that’s still the thing I’m chasing. Barcelona, it turns out, is an unreasonably good city for that kind of hunting, if you’re willing to walk both halves of it with the right camera in hand for each. Anella Olímpica, Barcelona. **Transparent: The Optics of Erasure**

In the ‘Transparent’ project, photographer William Josephs Radford explores the relationship between memory and absence through the use of artificial intelligence. Rather than recreating figures who are no longer with us, Radford removes his father’s presence from photographs of his own childhood using generative editing tools and reproduces the images using lenticular printing. By tilting the print, the viewer triggers a visual shift: the father’s figure appears and disappears with a slight movement. The scenes depict domestic moments, such as a man on a floral-patterned sofa, a parent standing beside their child by the sea, or playing with bricks and spades. The variation extends to other settings, from the blue-polka-dot armchair in the living room to everyday gestures, such as a kiss, a toast amongst cacti, a family portrait in front of a pink house, or embracing a newborn in hospital. The work challenges Roland Barthes’s notion of photography as irrefutable proof of what has been. Radford presents the family photograph as a space in which memory is composed of verifiable elements, inherited narratives and personal projections. This approach draws on Jacques Derrida’s concept of the ‘spectre’: the father is not eliminated in order to deceive, but appears as a presence that oscillates between visibility and disappearance. Unlike technologies designed to prolong the presence of the deceased through digital avatars, Radford uses digital tools to subtract. The intervention is made explicit and does not aim to offer consolation, but rather to make the retroactive nature of loss perceptible. The project thus transforms the algorithm into a tool for reflecting on vulnerability and the cultural significance of mourning. Explore the full project at josephsradford.com.

In an age where artificial reality can recreate almost anything, memory remains one of the last untouchables. If you are reading this article, you are a keen film photographer and have most likely experienced the joys of film photography across a range of film types, such as monochrome or colour negatives and colour transparencies. But black and white “slides”? Whoever heard of that? Go back 55 years to the time when I was a kid, black and white movies, black and white “film strips” were an everyday event. You could borrow a travel or cartoon “film strip”, (invariably black and white transparencies) from the local library, load it up on the family slide projector, then spend hours marveling at the stunning monochrome images. Black and white transparencies aren’t consigned to the history books, however. By the end of this piece, I am hoping to enthuse you to experiment with DIY black and white transparencies from typical black and white negative film, and experience the joy of creating your own stunning images. Here’s a sneak peek at the final result! Ilford FP4 PLUS Developed as a Transparency. Contax G2 with 35-70mm Zoom. Light-Box view. Producing your own it is not going to break the bank — it just takes a bit of patience, organisation and persistence. Which is a good thing we film photographers have that in spades! I use black and white transparencies created with ILFORD’s FP4 PLUS to illustrate this article but, as you’ll see further down the page, you can use almost any other black and white film. Slide/reversal films, such as Kodak Ektachrome and Fuji Provia 100F are not cheap, and their exposure latitude is much more restricted (narrower) than negative film, so extra attention must be taken to ensure accurate exposure. Then comes the development… Exposed slide film generally goes off to the lab for E6 processing and if the lab processor didn’t break down and ruin your film (as has happened to me at least 3 times, including my wedding photos!) a hefty processing fee occurs. The effort, expense and wait are worthwhile however, because those transparencies (slides) are brilliant in colour saturation which, along with the clarity just jump out at you from the light-box, no matter what the format. 35mm slides look great, but a 4×5 transparency is awesome! In my opinion, no digital camera screen can match the magic of a colour transparency on a light-box! Why is this? In the development of a negative, the tonal range of an image is compressed to allow for printing on media. In comparison, a slide (transparency) has a greater expanded tonal range, which is especially evident when viewed by transmitted light. Hands-on stuff: Printing vs reversal processing There are two main ways to create black and white transparencies. The first is known as “printing”; the second is a wet chemistry process that requires a few more steps to the normal negative process using standard developers, making it a “ reversal process “. “Printing” is very similar to traditional printing using an enlarger. If you have seen a movie shot in monochrome, this is the process involved: Steven Spielberg’s “Schindlers’s List” was shot on Kodak Double-X 5222 negative film. It was then processed using a low contrast developer, and then every frame was “printed” onto a high-contrast projection-quality negative and redeveloped. So, a negative of a negative creates a positive. Since this article is about the second method: reversal processing via wet chemistry into a positive image we won’t cover “Printing” in a lot of detail. If you want to try printing yourself, I would recommend buying either a slide duplicator attachment for your camera/lens (they are cheap) or simply photographing a negative of a negative on a light-box using a macro lens. “Printing” will invariably lead to a loss of image quality, which is why wet chemistry processing makes good sense. The positive images are created on the original film straight out of the camera. Can I make transparencies (slides) from other black and white films? In short: YES. I chose ILFORD FP4 PLUS because it is a film I am very familiar with and produces great monochrome images. I wanted a medium-speed film because I always use a coloured filter to enhance colour separation in the image (in this case, a yellow filter), which will reduce the effective ISO by a stop, yet still allow hand-held exposures. If you want to experiment, slower, traditional ISO rating films such as ILFORD’s PANF F PLUS have fine grain, are higher in contrast, don’t fog as much as faster emulsions and give richer blacks. I have also made very acceptable slides from a Kodak T-MAX 100, a modern tabular grained film. Let’s take a look at the process… An overview of a black and white reversal development process The back and white reversal development process contains a few extra steps over typical black and white film development. Here are the main steps: The 1st step is negative development — similar to developing any monochrome negative. Added to the developer is a silver “solvent” such as sodium thiosulphate (Hypo) to remove the silver halide crystals present in all film emulsions, but which are inert and not sensitive to light. If not removed, these crystals will degrade highlights. The 2nd step is bleaching to remove the silver image, which leaves behind a positive image of the remaining unexposed and undeveloped emulsion. This is followed by a dunk in a clearing solution to remove stain. The 3rd step is a fogging process that facilitates the rapid development of the remaining silver halide. This can be done in 2 ways: either by exposing the film to tungsten or fluorescent lighting, or by a chemical process involving tin (stannous) chloride as is used in the E6 process. The 4th step is a second development of the film to develop the positive image. The final step is fixing and washing . Does this seem like something you would like to try? If so, ILFORD have a very informative PDF on reversal processing available here: ILFORD Photo Black and White film reversal processing (PDF, 99KB) This document gives a great overview of the process, including recipes for the chemistry and details on processing times and temperatures. I didn’t follow the ILFORD protocol exactly but it’s an excellent place to start, then add your own tweaks. Here are some tips from my personal experience which you may find helpful: Exposing the film (making photographs with transparencies in mind) When photographing the initial images, I would advise bracketing each shot one stop below its normal exposure (over-exposing the film) and one stop above normal exposure (under-exposing the film) to give yourself three exposures of the same scene to help you dial in your own process. For ILFORD FP4 PLUS, that would be: +1 stop = ISO 64 (over-exposing) Normal = ISO 125 -1 stop = ISO 250 (under exposing) In practice, you can follow this approach: Other interesting reads How-to: Convert an automatic Polaroid pack film camera to use manual shutter speeds Ultreia: Making pilgrimage to Santiago de Compostela Meter the scene / set your desired shutter speed and lens aperture Frame 1: Shoot at a shutter speed one stop faster than the one in step 1 (over-exposing it). Frame 2: Shoot at the desired shutter speed. Frame 3: Shoot at a shutter speed one stop slower than step 1 (under-exposing it). Repeat for the entire roll to have a consistent “ Over-Metered-Under ” sequence of frames for each subject. Tip: If you are using a modern autofocus camera, see if it has an automatic bracketing option and use that instead to take three shots in sequence without any fiddling about (i.e. Nikon F100, Nikon F6, etc.) I recommend bracketing your shots because transparencies have reduced exposure latitude, and the process needs to be calibrated for your situation, including variables such as developer concentration, agitation frequency and temperature. Use the results to help you better understand how to expose specific scenes and really lock-in your own specific look. For the frames you see below, I used a Contax G2 with a Zeiss 35-70mm zoom as I knew I would get well-exposed tack sharp negatives, but any good quality film camera will excel. Finding and using the additional chemicals for your reversal process In the linked ILFORD PDF above, most instructions are straightforward, requiring a mass of chemicals to be weighed out and made up to the given volume. Note that you may need to purchase an electronic scale for measuring out chemicals accurately (available online and not expensive). Important: When you make up solutions, always use safety glasses, suitable gloves and a mask. Make sure your workspace is well ventilated. The extra chemicals needed for the reversal process can be purchased as follows: Photo store suppliers: Sodium thiosulphate and sodium or potassium metabisulphite (also home beer brewing suppliers for the metabisulphite!); Pharmacy or online: Potassium permanganate (Condy’s crystals); Auto shop: Sulphuric acid (used in car batteries). ALWAYS use safety glasses, and ALWAYS add the acid to water and not the other way around!! Careful with the acid! Concentrated (100%) sulphuric acid is nasty. I would recommend purchasing battery acid from your local auto shop instead. This is still very corrosive but generally around 30% concentrated acid. My bottle stated a concentration of sulphuric acid at 640 g / litre. To work out the actual dilution you need, divide this number by 1900 (the amount of sulphuric acid in concentrated or 100% acid) and multiply by 100. Example: My auto shop acid was 33%. The ILFORD instructions recommend 10ml of concentrated acid added to 490ml water. In the case of my 33% auto shop acid, I needed 3 times as much compared to 100% concentrated acid. That made my measurements 30ml of battery acid to 470 ml of water. Hope that has been explained clearly! Alternatives to ILFORD PQ Universal I didn’t use ILFORD’s PQ Universal as detailed in the above PDF. If you have trouble sourcing it yourself, you can follow my alternative: a 1+ 5 dilution of Rodinal R09 plus 2g of Hypo in 500ml of developer solution. Developing time at 20°C was 10 minutes vs 12 minutes at at 20°C for the PQ Universal. Replace the second exposure step with a chemical process I didn’t like the idea of handling wet film, exposing it to light and then having to re-spool and develop a second time. So instead I used stannous chloride, which is added to the second developer to create a Reversal Developer. Very little stannous chloride is needed — only 0.10 g per 500ml of solution. Rodinal was diluted 1+9. The developing time was 8 minutes at 20°C. Online textile dye suppliers stock stannous chloride, which is used to enhance the colours of natural dyes. Evaluating your results Remember that making black and white transparencies (slides) is a reversal process. A darker-than-ideal image means either underexposure or insufficient development (and vice versa). To calibrate my setup, I prefer to adjust the film’s ISO, rather than adjust processing, as reversal processing has many steps. Using ILFORD FP4 PLUS as an example: If an image taken at ISO 125 appears a little “light” after reversal processing, I would increase the ISO to 200. This calibrates the system, and in the future, I know the effective ISO of FP4 Plus for a black and white transparency is 200 — so long as I process in exactly the same way . If there is an image you really want to nail, then bracketing in half stops around the effective ISO should ensure a well-exposed black and white transparency. Exposing for the Highlights One more general piece of advice is about agitation. When processing by hand, the normal procedure is to invert the tank at set time intervals 8 or 10 times. I have found this is inadequate for some developer and film combinations and can lead to uneven processing, such as streaks and blotches. To help overcome this, I also rotate the tank with each inversion so that solution moves both vertically and horizontally with respect to the film. That’s all for now!
