**Capturing the City with the Lomo MC-A: LomoAmigo Jeon Sangyeol**
Fotógrafos: hey_springtime

Meet LomoAmigo photographer Jeon Sangyeol, who captures the ever-changing cityscape and the small, easily overlooked moments of everyday life with a warm and thoughtful eye. From the satisfying hands-on feel of the Lomo MC-A to the unique experience of developing his own film, discover his honest take on the enduring charm of analogue photography. © Jeon Sangyeol ,  Lomo MC-A Hello! It’s been a while! Could you start by introducing yourself to our readers? I’m Jeon Sangyeol, a street photographer based in Korea. I photograph the changing scenery of the city, along with the small, everyday moments that are easy to overlook. You’ve been shooting film for a long time. What does film photography mean to you? Still, film has a kind of “inconvenient fun” that you don’t get with digital photography. With a limited number of shots, you naturally take more care with every frame. And since you can’t see the results right away, there’s always that excitement of waiting. I also love the warmth and analogue feel of film. Whenever there’s a scene I want to remember for a long time, I find myself choosing film. © Jeon Sangyeol ,  Lomo MC-A What was your first impression of the Lomo MC-A? I had only seen it on social media before, so I first tried it at a LomoWalk last April. I was surprised by how solid and substantial it felt. Unlike the cute, lightweight cameras with plastic bodies I was used to, the Lomo MC-A has a simple but sturdy design. After using it myself, I liked it even more. It doesn’t do everything for you, and that gives it a certain tactile feel. The mix of automatic and manual controls made shooting more enjoyable. © Jeon Sangyeol ,  Lomo MC-A The Lomo MC-A lets photographers switch between automatic and manual settings. How did this help you while shooting? It gave me more freedom to decide how I wanted each photo to look. I could focus on a specific subject and blur the background, or keep everything in focus to emphasize the whole scene. For this shoot, I mostly used the automatic mode. The only time I switched to a manual setting was at a café. I set the aperture to f/2.8 to make the coffee stand out. It came out sharp and clear, just as I had hoped. © Jeon Sangyeol ,  Lomo MC-A How did you mainly use the Lomo MC-A during the shoot? I shot about 95% of the photos in automatic mode. The LiDAR sensor was fast and accurate, so I didn’t have to worry about much. I could simply press the shutter. The camera didn’t miss focus once, which I was really happy with. It also handled backlighting very well. The exposure was accurate, so I was able to keep plenty of detail in the photos. I also enjoyed using multiple exposure (MX), one of the charms of Lomo cameras. Having a dedicated button made it really easy to try. © Jeon Sangyeol ,  Lomo MC-A Do you have a favorite photo from this shoot? What makes it special to you? My favorite is the photo of the city at sunset. I decided on “a walk through the city” as the theme from the beginning. I spent quite a bit of time looking for the right place and timing because I wanted to capture the sunset over the city. The weather had been cloudy for days, and I had also missed the flower season I was hoping for. I was a little disappointed. Then I saw that the forecast was finally clear, so I headed out without hesitation. Luckily, there were still plenty of beautiful flowers in bloom. I waited for several hours and finally got the shot I had been hoping for. © Jeon Sangyeol ,  Lomo MC-A Even though I was shooting against the light, the photo captured everything I wanted – the silhouettes of the buildings and bridge, the yellow sky, the sunlight sparkling on the Han River, and the colorful flowers in the foreground. I’m really happy with how it turned out. It feels like a photo that made all those hours of waiting worthwhile. © Jeon Sangyeol ,  Lomo MC-A For the B&W shoot, you developed your film with the Daylight Developing Tank and scanned it yourself with the DigitaLIZA Max. How was the experience of developing and scanning your own film? I had always sent my film to a lab, so this was my first time developing it myself. There are quite a few steps, so it was a little difficult at first. But once I understood how the process worked, it wasn’t as complicated as I expected. It was really fun to develop photos that I had taken myself. Watching an image slowly appear on what had been a blank strip of film was also fascinating. I also liked being able to scan the film in high resolution without paying extra. © Jeon Sangyeol ,  Lomo MC-A ,  Self-developed with the Daylight Developing Tank and scanned with the DigitaLIZA Max What do you think is the charm of developing film yourself? There are a lot of steps, and you have to take care of everything yourself. But I really like being able to see my photos at home in a short amount of time, instead of waiting several days for a lab. Once you have the equipment, you can use it again and again. So for people who shoot a lot of film, I think it can also be a cost-effective option. As you get more experienced, you can experiment with the developing time, temperature, and chemical concentrations. You can find what works for you and create different kinds of results. That freedom to experiment is what I find most appealing about developing film yourself. © Jeon Sangyeol ,  Lomo MC-A ,  Self-developed with the Daylight Developing Tank and scanned with the DigitaLIZA Max Do you think film photography has a special value that digital photography can’t replace? I love the colors and grain that are unique to film. But more than anything, I love how the whole process helps me remember the moment and the place where a photo was taken. Sometimes the film doesn’t turn out exactly as planned. You might even get a result you never expected. But those photos can become even more special because you know you can never take the exact same photo again. Digital photography is fast and convenient. You can take a photo and edit it right away. Film is slower and more complicated. But I think that’s what makes it special. The photos stay with you longer, and the memories do, too. Finally, is there anything you’d like to say to the Lomography Community and our readers? If you want to slow down for a moment in this fast-moving world, I hope you’ll take some time to experience the warmth and analogue charm of film photography with Lomography. And I hope you’ll look forward to the photos I’ll be taking in the future! Thank you, Jeon Sangyeol, for sharing your warm perspective on the city and the moments you discovered along the way. Want to see more of his film photography? Visit his Instagram account and follow along.

Fotógrafos: lgideonts, francinegaebriele

We’ve come to know the LomoChrome Classicolor 200 for its warm colors and nostalgic character, but there’s more to this film stock than its signature look. In 32-year-old Indonesian film photographer Gideon’s (@lgideonts) latest portrait shoots, we get to see its range as he takes the film through two contrasting portraiture concepts – one bright and dreamy, another other dark and rebellious. In this interview, we’re getting to know Gideon and the process behind his conceptual photoshoots. Let’s take a closer look at how he brought out the film’s versatility through his creative vision. Could you please give a brief introduction about yourself? Hey there! I’m Gideon, 32, from Indonesia. I’m working in the oil and gas industry as a reservoir geomodeler and shooting film as a hobby. Tell us about your photography background. How long have you been shooting, and how did you first get into analogue photography? I don’t really have a solid background in photography. At first, I started taking pictures simply as a way to take a break from my old hobby – drawing and sketching. As time went by, life got busier, and I had less and less time for myself to slow down and have something that could help me release some stress. Then one day, I saw one of my friends shooting film as a little escape from our busy routine. What caught my attention wasn’t just the photos, but the whole process behind them. He was shooting with so much patience and effort. He used his smartphone as a light meter, manually adjusted every setting on his analogue camera, and had to carefully pay attention to the lighting because the ISO was already fixed by the film. Sometimes, he would even hold his breath just to take a single shot. Watching all those little struggles somehow made me curious. I thought, “Okay, I want to try this.” So I asked him to teach me how to play this game. From there, I started experimenting with different films, learning from my mistakes, and slowly figuring things out one roll at a time. What subjects or themes do you enjoy capturing the most? My photography is mostly about people, moments, and atmosphere. I started with photography as a way to escape from the routine and eventually became fascinated by film because of how intentional the process feels. I enjoy photographing portraits, fashion, city life, and everyday moments, especially when I can capture something spontaneous or emotionally honest. I tend to look for interesting light, unusual perspectives, colors, and imperfections. I like images that feel cinematic but still authentic – like a frame from a movie or a memory you happened to capture. You recently worked on two portrait shoots using the Classicolor 200. What were the concepts behind each shoot and what inspired their visual directions? The two shoots had quite different concepts, even though both were centered around fashion and character. The first shoot was more playful and glamorous. We wanted to create something that felt like an old-school fashion editorial – bold styling, expressive poses, and a slightly dreamy atmosphere. The Classicolor 200 worked really well for that because of the warm colors and softer film character. The second shoot was darker and more rebellious. We were inspired by alternative fashion, nightlife, and a little bit of that underground rock or punk aesthetic. I wanted the lighting to feel more dramatic, with deep shadows, strong highlights, and a sense of rawness rather than making everything look clean and polished. We used a mix of available light and direct artificial light to create the mood, especially for the darker shots. Classicolor 200 handled both conditions really well, giving warm skin tones in brighter light and deeper shadows with some nice grain in low light. I wasn’t aiming for technically perfect shots – I wanted the grain, contrast, and light to become part of the overall look. What did you like most about the results when you received the scans? Was there anything that surprised you? What I liked most was how the film gave the images a character that I wasn’t completely able to predict beforehand. The colors came out really nicely, especially the warm skin tones and the way the highlights rolled off. The grain also worked really well with the mood of both shoots – it made the images feel more tactile and nostalgic without taking too much attention away from the subject. **FotoFocus 2026 Biennial : Cincinnati Art Museum : Nancy Rexroth**

Nancy Rexroth: Secrets of My Power Secrets of My Power is the first major exhibition to explore the life and work of American photographer Nancy Rexroth. Born in 1946, Rexroth is known for a unique and powerful body of work made with a simple plastic camera published in 1977 under the title IOWA. A book unlike anything that came before it, IOWA remains a touchstone for many photographers nearly fifty years later. This exhibition tells a richly detailed story of Rexroth’s original, often defiant artistic vision amid the changing social, institutional, and creative landscapes of the 1960s, 70s, and early 80s. Drawing on extraordinary access to the artist’s archive, it sheds new light on her most beloved photographs and enables a previously unavailable perspective on her work across five decades. Along with documents, snapshots, and personal possessions, the exhibition features nearly 150 artworks. Nancy Rexroth: Secrets of My Power represents the public debut of the Nancy Rexroth Collection, an unparalleled archive of her life and work stewarded by the Cincinnati Art Museum. Curated by Nathaniel M. Stein, Ph.D., Curator of Photography at the Cincinnati Art Museum. **Archives : Gustave Gain’s autochromes: A bath of color in Agon-Coutainville**

Archives – September 25, 2017 Photography has made itself at home in Agon-Coutainville, a small commune of three thousand inhabitants in the Cotentin Peninsula. At the initiative of Emmanuelle Polle, the new Councilor responsible for culture, each year large format photographic prints are exhibited in public spaces. “One of the themes of the campaign was turning our nearly three-kilometer-long seawall promenade into an open-air gallery,” she noted. While the concept itself may not be new, the proximity of the sea, the idea of a stroll in fresh air, rain or shine, and the size and the quality of the custom-made brushed steel frames give each photo rare attention. Autochromes in the open air Launched last summer with the photographs of the architect and designer Marc Held, taken while he was a gym instructor on this beach in the 1950s, the principle of outdoors exhibition is again at work this year with a “bath of color.” “We didn’t want to limit ourselves to black and white or to local history. We went in a whole new direction, with color photographs that have no relationship to the commune,” explained Emmanuelle Polle. It was by rummaging through the online Archives de la Manche that they stumbled across Gustave Gain, a Norman photographer born in Cherbourg. Nearly five hundred of Gustave Gain’s autochromes have been preserved. They include landscapes, sunsets, travel photos, as well as a lot of family portraits in which his wife Adeline poses in various colored outfits. Adeline in green bathing suit, Adeline on top of dunes in a white dress with a lemon-yellow scarf, Adeline in her flowery floppy hat or her turquoise shalwar. Magnificent. The appeal of the color in Gustave Gain’s work combines with his photographic research, even though this was never his profession, since he practiced photography as an amateur, on several occasions winning the recognition of the Société française de photographie. A scientific study of color Gustave Gain (1876–1945) was a scientist. Having studied chemistry, he became a researcher at the Muséum national d’histoire naturelle, and it was as a scientist that he conducted his color experiments. Invented in 1907 by the Lumière brothers, color photography was a rarely employed technique at the time of Gustave Gain’s first experiments (his earliest autochromes date to 1909). While the experimentation behind the research into color and image composition is undeniable, the true force of Gustave Gain’s autochromes resides in the modernity of his framing. “I am not a photographer, but if I know how to look, I owe it to Gustave,” admitted his nephew, the filmmaker Jean Rouch, born in 1917. There is a certain softness to these photographs. They deploy a palette of faded flowers. Their pointillism is accentuated by the large scale of the prints on display in Agon-Coutainville. Here is the beginning of a story, of a history of color photography, the story of a family that, between the sons and the nephew, would perpetuate a talent. There is an incontestable pleasure in being in the company of these hundred-year-old images, one’s back turned to the English Channel. Living up here in the Sierra Nevada Mountains of northern California has its perks, even if they are a bit off-beat. There’s a popular movement called ‘The State of Jefferson’ hoping to have this region split from California to create a new state. There’s another robust movement to have this area split from the US and form a separate country. I don’t know who, or what, they are shooting at. It’s a multi-task event that separates the men from the boys, and strong women from both. Events include the axe throw, where competitors aim to hit a bullseye and explode a beer can inside the target. Not joking about this. The judges vigorously shake the cans before inserting them into the wooden target. The ‘Walk of Death’ involves walking out onto the end of a bouncy branch with a running chainsaw and cutting exactly 18 inches of wood, and then walking back without falling. **Video of the Week: Water, Get Enemy? Directed by Daniel Obasi**

On the edge of Lagos, where the city meets the Atlantic, Water, Get Enemy? follows a community of young surfers who have grown up around the waters of Tarkwa Bay. Directed by Daniel Obasi, the short film looks beyond surfing as a sport to explore the relationships, routines and sense of freedom that have formed around it. Through intimate testimonies and images moving between the ocean and the city beyond it, the film presents Tarkwa Bay as both an escape from the pressures of Lagos and a space for self-expression, friendship and belonging. For its surfers, the water is not simply somewhere to ride waves, but a place where an alternative way of living together has taken shape. C41 spoke with Obasi about documenting this community, challenging established representations of surf culture, and the role of the ocean in shaping a different image of contemporary Lagos. &nbsp; Gaia V. Marraffa: Surf culture has long been framed through a predominantly Eurocentric, Californian imaginary. Where does Water, Get Enemy? sit within your wider visual practice of repositioning West Africa as a centre of contemporary, hedonistic subcultures? Daniel Obasi: Surf culture is often seen through a Western lens. With Water, Get Enemy?, I wanted to centre West Africa and show Lagos as a place with its own youth culture, freedom and pleasure. GVM: Throughout the film, low-angle shots turn the young surfers into almost statuesque silhouettes against the skies of Tarkwa Bay. How did you develop this visual language, and what interested you in the relationship between their physicality, style and the particular grace of this community? DO: I was drawn to their physicality and the way they move through the water. The low angles give them presence and scale while keeping their individuality and style visible. GVM: Through the voice-overs, the ocean emerges as more than a playground: it becomes a place to decompress, away from the anxiety and depression that can accompany life in Lagos. What does the act of reclaiming mental wellbeing through nature mean to you, both personally and politically? DO: For me, the ocean represents release. It offers a space away from Lagos’s noise and pressure, where you can reconnect with yourself, nature and others. GVM: Some of the surfers speak about their families’ disapproval of the sport, while describing surfing itself as an essential expression of personal freedom. Do you see Lagos’s younger generation redefining ideas of priority, passion and self-fulfilment in relation to more traditional expectations? DO: I think so. There’s a generation questioning what they’ve been told their lives should look like and making space for genuine happiness and fulfilment. GVM: The film celebrates a remarkably close-knit collective, formed in and around the water. Against the intensity and complexity of Lagos, how important was it for you to portray the ocean as a place where intimacy, community and chosen families can take shape? DO: That community was central to the film. I wanted the water to feel like a meeting point—a place where people could connect, build relationships and create a sense of family beyond traditional structures. GVM: Beyond the waves, we catch glimpses of Lagos’s modern skyline and industrial coastline. How did you approach the visual and spatial tension between the creative isolation of Tarkwa Bay and the metropolis that surrounds it? DO: I wanted that contrast to feel natural. Tarkwa Bay seems removed from Lagos, but the city is always visible in the distance. That tension became part of the film’s visual language. GVM: Water, Get Enemy? captures a particular feeling of freedom and belonging. Do you see the film as a self-contained chapter in your exploration of youth identity in Lagos, or as part of a wider cinematic language you want to continue developing? DO: It’s part of a wider exploration. I’m interested in young people, identity, freedom and the ways Lagos shapes how we see ourselves. I’d like to keep developing those ideas through film. &nbsp;

**Salone Raritas: this is how it went**

Salone Raritas brought one-off and limited edition design pieces, antiques, and high craftsmanship to the trade fair for the first time this year. The exhibitors Nina Yashar, Massimo Lunardon, Bruno Botticelli, and Francesco Faccin share their thoughts. Salone Raritas. Curated icons, unique objects, and outsider pieces in Pavilion 9 move the spotlight on beauty, craftsmanship, and sustainability, amongst other things. Curated by Annalisa Rosso, Editorial Director and Cultural Advisor to the Salone del Mobile.Milano, and with an exhibition display designed by Formafantasma, the platform showcased some of the most influential realities of the global scene, as well as some rare true gems. Seen from inside, it was the jewel in the crown of the Salone del Mobile; a small event that, while showcasing antique objects and pieces from a more recent past, felt decidedly innovative and “young.” It was also extremely satisfying in terms of turnout, with lots of really interesting visits on the first day from people culturally prepared for what they were going to see, and a creditable press presence from all the leading newspapers. There was a different sort of public on the following days, but all very curious about what they were looking at.’ Bruno Botticelli, President of Associazione Antiquari d’Italia (Italian Antique Dealers Association), described Salone Raritas as an interesting experience with no shortage of proposals. ‘We certainly met a different sort of public, keen to find fresh inspiration, but I believe an educational element is needed to introduce a world that is unknown to most people, especially to non-Italians. We are living in a time when there is a clear need for uniqueness in every aspect of our lives. This need is even stronger among the younger generations who, despite having partly moved on from their fixation with logos, need new keys for interpreting the concept of collectible objects that resonate with the new languages of design.’ Salone Raritas is therefore not just an exhibition space, but a diffusor of a new education in design. Not so much as a trend, rather as an attitude and a receptivity: a way of observing, selecting and connecting objects and visions.’ After all, Salone Raritas and Nilufar are not such different projects: ‘Nilufar stemmed precisely from this constant desire to explore what has not yet been fully told, affording space to different languages in dialogue with one another without hierarchies. At Raritas, I found this same openness and desire to create a space dedicated to research, experimentation, and vision.’ Lastly, she mentioned the importance of the visitors: ‘We were also pleasantly impressed by the quality and breadth of the public. There was an opportunity to engage with interior designers and industry professionals from diverse backgrounds, as well as key figures in the furnishings industry. This broad-based interest is, in my view, one of the most significant factors to emerge from this first edition, highlighting an increasing curiosity towards collectible design, and a greater willingness to recognise its value even in wider contexts. It is an important signal for Milan and for the future prospects of this field, allowing it to grow whilst maintaining its own identity and interacting increasingly with other worlds.’ Looking ahead to next year, designer Francesco Faccin stressed that Italian design has always trodden a fine line between art, research, high craftsmanship, and industry: ‘A humanistic rather than a functionalist approach, that defines “our” way of designing. Sottsass, Mendini, Munari, Mari, Branzi, De Lucchi, Pesce, are just some examples.’ As curator Annalisa Rosso stressed, Salone Raritas is a natural evolution within the Salone del Mobile ecosystem. Along with industrial design, which remains central, a number of different, heterogeneous elements are introduced, chosen from all over the world in a conscious response to an ongoing transformation on the international architectural and interior design scene, in which different languages already coexist and support each other.

Hoy es el último día para apuntarte. Organizar y coordinar un estudio profesional de fotografía, una modelo y una maquilladora lleva su tiempo y tenemos que ponernos en marcha. Es normal, para ello hace falta una buena planificación anterior. Tienes que tener claro previamente lo que quieres hacer, donde y cómo lo quieres.
