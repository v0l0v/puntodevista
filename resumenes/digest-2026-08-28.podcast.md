On June 19 , 2024 , Kristina Rozhkova was arrested in Russia alongside three others – Anna Panteleeva, Yaroslava Guminaya, and Sergey Evstyukhin. First, they were taken to a temporary detention centre ( IVS ), before Rozhkova was transferred to a pre-trial detention ( SIZO ) facility for two months. For the next year and a half, she had to attend ​ “ endless” court hearings. It came after a photoshoot they had organised in a Russian Orthodox cemetery. ​ “ There were no victims, no damaged property and no physical evidence,” she says. ​ “ It featured elements of shibari [Japanese rope bondage], which was a technique I frequently incorporated into my work. We wore structured outfits, and the focus was entirely aesthetic rather than overtly political – there was nothing vulgar or inherently provocative.” But Rozhkova claims that the group were the targets of a co-ordinated attack from a group known as Muzhskoye Gosudarstvo , which translates to ​ ‘ Male State’, who reported them to the authorities, even though none of the final photographs had been published. It came after one of the models in the shoot had publicly called out a male artist for sexual assault prior to the shoot. Being in SIZO at first was completely unbearable – those two months were genuinely agonizing,” she recalls. ​ “ My cellmates used to tell me that the first two months are always the hardest period to survive, and after that, it gradually becomes easier to manage month by month.” House arrest was hardly easier. ​ “ At first, house arrest felt like absolute paradise – you sleep in a normal bed, watch movies, read and focus on hobbies,” she continues. ​ “ But after two months, I realised it was its own form of hell. I couldn’t go for a walk or step outside, and I had to watch my life pass me by without understanding why.” That period, which ultimately consumed two years of her life, without considering the long-term mental effects since, inspired her to create her series and newly published photobook Gut Feeling . Its photographs – which are scatty, disquieting and occasionally even violent – explore the physical experience of being trapped with no control or way out. Photos with chains and ropes are sequenced alongside people physically holding others down – one shot sees a person squatting, with their feet obscuring a man’s face. How many different film stocks have existed throughout the years? The answer is probably far more than you’d expect. Every Film Ever is an project dedicated to documenting the history of film stocks, from familiar favorites to obscure and long-forgotten emulsions. We talked with founder Lenny Alvarez about his inspiration for the site, the process of building an archive of thousands of films, and why preserving the history (and even the packaging!) of film photography matters. Visit everyfilmever.online Tell us a little about Every Film Ever. Every Film Ever holds most of the film stocks that were ever made. I wanted it to be an archive, something that preserves the knowledge, but wrapped in something beautiful and easy to use. Knowledge exists on the internet and you can always find it eventually, but preserving it properly means making it easy to reach and enjoyable to interact with. So it was built for that, and honestly for the geekiness of it, too. The packaging and the aesthetics of film are something really special to me. So the site lets you look at all of it, but also save what you liked, see examples shot on a stock, even buy it, and open a profile to mark what you've shot with. It's not a social network in any way, but I did want it to be a bit more than just a museum. What's your background, and what inspired you to create Every Film Ever? My name is Lenny Alvarez. I'm a film photographer and an engineering student, though the photography defines me far more than the engineering does. I started about seven years ago and never shot digital at all, I went straight to film. I learned to develop and scan with my friend Jonathan Jakoby. Then, started printing and got completely addicted, spending every free hour in the darkroom. We ran a lab out of the house for a while, developing for friends. Over a hundred rolls a month with no professional machine. It was amazing until it became a nightmare. Now, I shoot commissioned work, brand campaigns, album covers, hospitality, and Jonathan and I teach analog photography through workshops and courses. People come in with nothing and leave having developed and scanned their own film. That part matters most to me. I really dislike gatekeeping; I think it ruins the things that are actually good. Alongside that, I have a strong pull towards technology, and my projects sit between the two. Every Film Ever came out of exactly that, and so does HALIGHT, the app I'm building now. (You can learn more about HALIGHT and get on the waitlist here.) What got me started was that most of the analog knowledge online is scattered. It's in forum threads, on Reddit, in books. I wanted to combine my love for this world with the technical side I enjoy putting into things, and pull it all into one place. I should also name the thing that inspired the form of it. I came across a site that catalogs Chinese cigarette packaging, every single one, laid out like a museum. It stuck with me, and I thought film deserved the same treatment and nobody had done it. I'd rather say that openly than have people wonder, because the two do feel similar in spirit. As for why it matters: it's part of the whole story. So many of these films have genuinely strange histories behind them, and it feels like a waste not to write that down. We're living in a world where maybe forty films are still being manufactured, and I'm being generous with that number. Knowing where we came from thirty years ago feels like part of the identity for a lot of the analog photographers I know. What's your process for documenting film stocks? The research was relatively simple, actually. Most of the analog knowledge online is sitting in forums, on Reddit, and in books, and if you dig enough it's all there. That's how I found a few incredible databases, which of course I credited. It felt like finding a box of gold in an attic. Genuinely exciting. The packaging images come from those same databases, credited on every page. The bulk is the DX Database, just over three thousand of them, plus around four hundred high resolution scans from dekuNukem's Film Packaging Archive. The rest come from manufacturers and retailers, each credited on the film's own page. Out of 3,631 films, only eight have no image at all. I'll be upfront about one more thing, since it comes up: I used AI tools to help build the site, and I don't see a reason to hide it. What the tools couldn't do is know that Rollei Retro 80S is a Belgian emulsion originally made for military use, or that LomoChrome Purple is C-41 and not a slide film, or decide in the first place that film packaging was worth preserving. There were 151 wrong entries in the data that got corrected because someone who actually shoots film sat and looked at them. That part is me. What are some of the features on the site? Sorting film stocks by color felt essential from the very beginning. The way I see it, the site isn't only for analog photographers, it's for anyone who likes design and aesthetics. That's what the color filter is for. If you're just passing through, it's genuinely fun to filter everything down to the colors you like. It's easy and it's nice, and maybe it keeps people around a bit longer to wonder about things. Honestly, the color sort was a feature I added for fun. Beyond that you can filter by brand, by country, by whether it's still in production or long gone, by format, and by color or black and white. All of it is there to let people dig around as much as they want. There are no ads on the site. I want people to stay long enough to get curious and maybe start shooting a little more. Tell us more about the film stocks on the site. There are 3,631 films in there right now, across 457 brands and 35 countries, and it only keeps growing. I just added personal accounts, and I plan to keep expanding it with whatever features I think of or people suggest. My favorite film has to be Rollei Retro 80S. It's a Belgian emulsion originally made for military use, and because of that it has extraordinary contrast and very fine grain. It's my go-to whenever I can get my hands on it, and I usually buy it in bulk. Most of the black and white frames in the folder below are shot on it. As for the rare ones, I found a Hello Kitty film on there. That was funny. You can talk for hours about Aerochrome and all the special stuff, but the beauty is in the silly things. How is Every Film Ever different from the other film archives out there? What I'm doing is going wide. dekuNukem's Film Packaging Archive is one of the sources I built on, and it's credited throughout the site, and they go deep. Proper high resolution scans of the actual boxes, the physical object preserved properly. I'm trying to list every film that ever existed, with the specs and the history, so you can find things and compare them. FP-Archive's scans make my entries far better than they would have been on their own. It's not the same job, and it's definitely not a competition. More broadly, the way I see it, my work was making the knowledge accessible. The databases already exist, I'm not reinventing the wheel, I'm just making it accessible in a more tangible way. The goal was to combine my love for this world with the technical, nerdy side I like putting into things. What has surprised you most since launching the project? Two things. **A Behind the Scenes Look at Shooting the World Cup**

This summer the World Cup came to my hometown. This is a story about how a fantasy of shooting the tournament morphed into a dream, which turned into a goal, and then became reality. It ended where it does for every photographer; grumbling about my seating assignment. Trigger warning: This is a long post with a lot of information about what photographers go through to get a field level seat at a World Cup game. I that doesn’t interest you, I hope you enjoy the pictures In the 1970’s, I was playing high school soccer in Hawaii and learning photography with using a Minolta SRT 101. The world Cup was known to me, but it was a strange foreign thing. There was no internet or social media or ESPN or streaming services. That started to change in 1994 when the World Cup was played here in the states, and we got a taste of the games and the crazy fandom that is such a big part of the competition. Not long after that, the internet and updated television technology made it possible to see every game of each tournament, but it was still a long way between watching a game on TV to sitting on the sidelines with a camera. I was still in the dream state. Sweden vs the Netherlands. A view from the sidelines captured with a Nikon Z6iii and Nikon 20mm lens For me, that changed a couple of years ago, when Lionel Messi signed on to play with Inter Miami, the MLS team that David Beckham (partially) owns. I was a long-time freelance contributor to the Dallas Observer and while the paper doesn’t have a lot of sports coverage, they do cover local “events”. Messi and Inter were scheduled to play our local lads (FC Dallas) and it would be Messi’s first road game in an MLS shirt. The game had “big event” written all over it. My editor at the time, who just happened to be an Argentinian, asked if I would like to go cover the scene. My answer was yes, or maybe it was “oh hell yes” or something to that effect. A dream was in the very early stages of becoming a goal. Three years later, I got to see Lionel Messi score a historic goal in a World Cup game This goal would make Messi (briefly) the all time World cup goals leader Messi celebrates with a teammate Messi acknowledges the thousands of Argentina loyalists All of these shots were taken with a Nikon Z9 and 400mm lens with the teleconverter on to give a focal length of 560mm @ f4 This may be too much history for some of you, but it has relevance. After the Inter Miami game, and armed with a full 90 minutes of experience, I started shooting as much “soccer” as I could. I got a season pass to shoot FC Dallas, I started shooting our new women’s pro team (Trinity FC) and even shot the new USL II team up the road where I live. All the while I was collecting contact names and email addresses. USL teams are pro teams, but just barely. But as it turns out one of the kids on the team has a father who would come to watch, and his father is also a primary investor in a new team higher up the USL pyramid that will be starting play in 2027. And that team just signed the Mexican legend Chicharito. Needless to say, dad got pictures of his kid. Now here’s the relevance to the World Cup. Shooting a FIFA event is not impossible, but it is complicated. Three months before the first game they opened up the World Cup media center for applications. Step one in the process is to get FIFA accreditation, and for photographers they want a picture of your current photo passes and a link to any recent published work. Accreditation is also on multiple levels, and in the end the Dallas Observer was accredited as a media organization along with me personally for photography, and my editor and a couple of staff writers who would sit up high in the press box. Accreditation also comes in different flavors depending on the reach of your publication, national or regional. If you were from a country that had a team in the tournament, that was a point in your favor as well. I really can’t say how many flavors there are, or how many factors FIFA has for determining this first layer of access. At the time it seemed like we were navigating Dante’s nine circles of hell, but in FIFA’s defense it is a huge undertaking with 48 countries sending teams and multiple venues across Mexico, Canada and the US. At the end of the day, we were accredited as a local/regional outlet and could apply for any of the games in Dallas (9) or Houston (6). Packed and ready to head to the game with a Nikon Z9 and 400mm, Z8 with my old F mount 70-200 and a Z6iii with the 20mm for crowd and reaction shots The media line to get through bag check could take up to an hour At this point, we had made progress but still did not have approval for the coveted game day pass that gets you in the door for a specific match. I guess we were in about the third circle of the inferno. As you may have guessed, there is an additional process for getting in the game. And an additional app. Group stage games are scheduled well in advance, so you know the times, venue and teams involved a solid month before kickoff. This is important for out-of-town visitors who are following a specific team and becomes one of the factors involved in game day approvals. Knockout games follow a similar process, but applications don’t open until the teams have been determined. At the Observer, we filled out our applications for the group stage well in advance and then waited with some trepidation for the approval confirmation. And waited. And waited. About a week before the first game, I ran into another photographer I know who shoots for the Associated Press and asked him if he had gotten his approvals yet and he looked at me curiously. He had been approved weeks ago. This brings up the whole discussion around pecking order, which I’ll get to in a second. The good news is that my editor and I got our email for the first game a couple days later and many happy face emojis were exchanged. Apparently, FIFA waits as long as they can to get a full list of applicants before passing judgement on who gets in. By the end of the tournament, we were approved for all the games we applied for (12) so I’m not complaining, but there were some nervous moments before that first notification arrived. Photographers checking their messages at half time Photographer pile up for team photos Remote cameras behind the goal For those of you who want to know about what it’s like to actually shoot a game, this is where it starts. Kind of. For photographers, there is one more level of hell to pass through, and it directly impacts the pictures you will capture. Each photographer is assigned a seat for the game, and there is an app to make your selection. When photographers get their approval notification, they are sectioned into groups A, B or C. The app opens up about 24 hours before kick-off. 10 AM local for A listers, 11 AM for the B group and 12 PM for the C group, which for photographers is kind of our very own group of death. Group A is basically the big wire services, and they normally have multiple shooters at every game. The most coveted seating positions are in the two corners on the side of the field with the team benches, because those corners give you the best opportunities to get celebration shots. There are about a dozen chairs in each corner, and they will all be taken when groups B and C get to make their selections. I was lucky enough to be in group B for 11 of the 12 games I shot, which means at best I would have access to the 30 th or so most desirable location and at worst 75th. When 11 AM rolls around, everyone in group B would click the link to get in the seating assignment queue. Click fast on a good internet connection and you may go to the front of the line, click slow and you will be in the back. A count down timer shows your progression in line, along with the available seats. When it is your turn you have 45 seconds to select a seat or you go to the back of the queue. Another complication is the configuration of American football stadiums. I prefer to sit on the sideline just outside the 6-yard box. But because the fields had to be widened for World Cup soccer, these sections of the field were extremely tight, and no seating was available until almost the 18-yard box. And yes, that was the grumbling part of the story. Happily, I’m over it. All I ever wanted was a seat at the table and I got one. Most of the games in Texas were played in the middle of the day, and you’ll want to show up 3 – 4 hours early to get a halfway decent parking spot. Along with 100 to 150 photographers, there are live broadcasts from the field, team photographers, FIFA photographers and the dreaded FIFA media partners with their i-phones and lack of any awareness about getting in the way. All media goes through the same security gate, and it could take as long as one hour to get in. Add in all the cops and bouncers and field marshals and hundreds of volunteers and the sidelines become pretty chaotic before the game starts. For the game, photographers are packed together cheek to cheek. Think of the space on an economy airline except you all have multiple cameras and 400mm lenses, laptops and wired internet connections. In my bag I carried a Nikon Z9 with the Z mount 400 f 2.8, a Z8 with a 70-200 and a Z6iii with a wider lens (normally 20mm). For the most part, everyone around you has a similar kit, and we are all watching the same game. For competent photographers, it’s straightforward to get a good picture at the World Cup. To get a picture that is unique is a whole different challenge, and to get a picture that no one else got is almost impossible. **Flowers of Speech**

News - August 28, 2026 by Jonas Cuénin In 1965, the poet Allen Ginsberg coined the phrase flower power to turn protest into peaceful spectacle. Two years later, photographer Bernie Boston made it an icon: a young man slipping a carnation into the barrel of a rifle at the foot of the Pentagon. Fifty-nine years have passed. The flower has not changed its name, but it has changed sides: it no longer disarms violence, it is now the object of an almost clinical suspicion. At the Jardin d’été in Arles, five artists brought together by Beata Nowak and Michel Poivert, curators of the project and authors of the book published by Textuel, no longer look to the flower for a symbol of peace. They look to it for clues. Alice Pallot, a member of the Tendance Floue collective, followed architects Elena Seegers and Simon de Dreuille, founders of Botanical Agency, into the heart of a contradiction: floral foam, the material found in nearly every florist’s bouquet, becomes underwater “a persistent and pervasive source of pollution.” Pallot photographs the particles that break off it under horticultural LED light, invisible to the naked eye, revealed at last under a light designed to make things grow. The gesture is no laboratory accident, as the artist explains: “Bringing together scientists and artists is, for me, a way of making visible and understandable studies and articles that are sometimes impenetrable because of their inaccessible language.” After that, it is hard to look at a flower without looking at what makes it. © Alice Pallot © Alice Pallot © Matei Bejenaru © Matei Bejenaru This same distrust of appearance drives Matei Bejenaru, who reaches back to the Brendel models, three-dimensional teaching aids made of papier-mâché, plaster, or wood, produced in Germany for the study of botany. He photographs them in color, almost like watercolors, where Karl Blossfeldt would have captured them in clinical black and white a century earlier. From a distance, the image reads as a plant. Up close, it reveals the artificiality of the replica. The doubt, for Bejenaru, is the point: it echoes a very real urgency, that of species now facing extinction. Where Bejenaru plays on the confusion between plant and replica, Suzanne Lafont takes on a more ordinary form of blindness: the one we direct at the weeds of sidewalks, tree grates, and vacant lots. Twelve municipalities of Bordeaux Métropole on foot, plants placed on a light table to intensify their colors, then digitally inverted in reference to Goethe’s Theory of Colors . “Wild nature no longer exists,” the artist told the director of the Musée des Beaux-Arts in Bordeaux, where the work was first shown. What Lafont is fighting has a name: “plant blindness,” the cultural indifference to the vegetal world that lets us step over dozens of quiet lives every day. © Suzanne Lafont © Suzanne Lafont © Anais Tondeur © Anais Tondeur The attention Lafont demands for urban weeds, Anaïs Tondeur carries even further, to the contaminated soils of the Land of Fires, near Naples, where toxic waste is buried. The plants that still grow there overproduce polyphenols, making it possible to create phytographs, chemical traces inscribed directly onto the emulsion. “Without extracting the plants from the soil, this photographic encounter takes place through nothing more than contact between the elements present,” the photographer explains. “It is one of the most environmentally respectful photographic protocols there is.” Philosopher Michael Marder then writes a letter to each plant; Tondeur reads it back to them; a second phytograph is born from that reading. This correspondence between the human and the vegetal finds an even more intimate echo in the work of Jiang Zhi, who sets flowers alight in full bloom for his series ( Love Letters ), dedicated to the memory of his wife Ren Lan, whose name means “orchid” in Chinese. Ikebana, the Japanese art of embracing the ephemeral, is here subverted: the burning flower no longer seeks eternity, it sets fire to it. “The fire is as beautiful as the flower, and the flower too is entirely without flaw,” the artist says, “but of course, this is only a temporary beauty.” The flower burns. **Dust and Decibels: Rave on Film**

News - August 28, 2026 by Guénola Pellen You can feel it: these two women lived the rave from within. It was woven into their daily lives, anchored by ritual, carried by tribe. And yet today, our photographs blend together perfectly.” From this alchemy between two eyes, no individual signature remains: throughout the book, the photographers chose never to specify who took which picture. “Maybe that’s exactly the point,” Valentina suggests: “two different perspectives that, over time, became a shared memory.” A deliberate anonymity, true to the spirit of the movement it immortalises. The book is equally striking for its total absence of visual hierarchy. A wall of speakers is photographed with the same gravity as a child playing with a dog in the dust of an encampment. “It tells exactly what we experienced: situations, speakers, trucks, children, dogs and people.” Conspiracy Sound Squad Truck Luigi and Adriano Post-party moments Eviction Attempt, CzechTek, Czech Republic That levelling of gaze builds, page after page, into the portrait of an entire life rather than a mere nocturnal pastime. Bodies stretched out by the water, a black dog standing sentinel before a tower of speakers, whole convoys of camper vans lined up on some improvised lot. “For more than seven years, we lived on four wheels, in trucks, moving between the road and squatted social centres. Never Alone is infused with that sense of freedom and community that defined our lives,” Cheyenne and Valentina explain. The sound system here is no mere party backdrop, but an itinerant domestic architecture in which the speaker stack stands in for the hearth, the truck for the sitting room, and the road for a permanent address. That happy nomadism, though, sits at odds with another constant running through the book, one that reads as stranger still today: the almost benevolent presence of the police. It is hard, from where we stand now, to picture a uniform ever blending into the scenery of a free party. And yet even in the rare frames where law enforcement meets the lens, no tension shows on any face. “What we experienced at the end of the 1990s can never be repeated,” they observe. “The police didn’t really know how to deal with us yet, and we were living in a time that was far less controlled than today. Now algorithms, cameras and surveillance permeate every aspect of our lives.” Children of the Bassline That sense of a lost innocence finds a pointed echo in the book’s foreword, written by Tobia D’Onofrio, who suggests that the children pictured here may be the very same young people now protesting against the authorities. In France, several bills aimed at further criminalising free parties are currently under consideration, following in the wake of the anti-rave decree passed by the Meloni government in late 2022. It is a lineage the photographers acknowledge without grandstanding. “Free-party culture hasn’t disappeared, and fortunately the new generations haven’t allowed themselves to be intimidated. Illegal rave parties are still being organised, less frequently and a little more under the radar, and we can only be happy about that.” And yet this continuity, however reassuring, raises an almost vertiginous question about the photographic act itself. The American writer Hakim Bey once theorised the rave as a “temporary autonomous zone,” a gesture designed by its very nature to vanish. Photography does precisely the opposite: it arrests time, fixes it onto a light-sensitive surface. Isn’t there something paradoxical in that? The two women wave the objection away with disarming simplicity. “When we took those photographs, our intention wasn’t to ‘document’ anything. We both had a passion for photography, and for us these were simply images of our everyday lives.” Cheyenne portrait of Toppy. Miki portrait, Level 57, Bologna, Italy. That same absence of calculation runs through their choice of medium: there is no retro affectation behind the exclusive use of black and white. “It was an editorial choice, since most of the photographs were originally shot in black and white. We have always loved black-and-white film. We also used to print the photographs ourselves in the darkroom, using an enlarger.” Out of that artisanal darkroom labour, certain images resurface with particular force. “Every photograph brings something back and stirs something in me,” Cheyenne confides. Valentina, for her part, remembers with precision Tequinox, a party held in Bologna in 1999: “We found the location for the party only a few days before it began, thinking it was abandoned. Several sound systems arrived, along with people from all over Europe.” One senses that night in one of the book’s photographs: a square overrun with vans and huddled figures beneath a low sky, where you can almost feel the bass still trembling underfoot. Rave Tekno Mobil Squad, Bologna, Italy, 2002. CzechTek, Czech Republic, 2004. In the end, the party itself was only ever the backdrop to something larger: the wanderings of a generation in perpetual motion, for whom the collective was the answer. Few places remain today where such lives would still be possible. The free party endures as one of the last spaces of self-organised, collective and free celebration, and it is precisely for that reason that it finds itself, across Europe, in the crosshairs of politics.
