**A Behind the Scenes Look at Shooting the World Cup**

This summer the World Cup came to my hometown. This is a story about how a fantasy of shooting the tournament morphed into a dream, which turned into a goal, and then became reality. It ended where it does for every photographer; grumbling about my seating assignment. Trigger warning: This is a long post with a lot of information about what photographers go through to get a field level seat at a World Cup game. I that doesn’t interest you, I hope you enjoy the pictures In the 1970’s, I was playing high school soccer in Hawaii and learning photography with using a Minolta SRT 101. The world Cup was known to me, but it was a strange foreign thing. There was no internet or social media or ESPN or streaming services. That started to change in 1994 when the World Cup was played here in the states, and we got a taste of the games and the crazy fandom that is such a big part of the competition. Not long after that, the internet and updated television technology made it possible to see every game of each tournament, but it was still a long way between watching a game on TV to sitting on the sidelines with a camera. I was still in the dream state. Sweden vs the Netherlands. A view from the sidelines captured with a Nikon Z6iii and Nikon 20mm lens For me, that changed a couple of years ago, when Lionel Messi signed on to play with Inter Miami, the MLS team that David Beckham (partially) owns. I was a long-time freelance contributor to the Dallas Observer and while the paper doesn’t have a lot of sports coverage, they do cover local “events”. Messi and Inter were scheduled to play our local lads (FC Dallas) and it would be Messi’s first road game in an MLS shirt. The game had “big event” written all over it. My editor at the time, who just happened to be an Argentinian, asked if I would like to go cover the scene. My answer was yes, or maybe it was “oh hell yes” or something to that effect. A dream was in the very early stages of becoming a goal. Three years later, I got to see Lionel Messi score a historic goal in a World Cup game This goal would make Messi (briefly) the all time World cup goals leader Messi celebrates with a teammate Messi acknowledges the thousands of Argentina loyalists All of these shots were taken with a Nikon Z9 and 400mm lens with the teleconverter on to give a focal length of 560mm @ f4 This may be too much history for some of you, but it has relevance. After the Inter Miami game, and armed with a full 90 minutes of experience, I started shooting as much “soccer” as I could. I got a season pass to shoot FC Dallas, I started shooting our new women’s pro team (Trinity FC) and even shot the new USL II team up the road where I live. All the while I was collecting contact names and email addresses. USL teams are pro teams, but just barely. But as it turns out one of the kids on the team has a father who would come to watch, and his father is also a primary investor in a new team higher up the USL pyramid that will be starting play in 2027. And that team just signed the Mexican legend Chicharito. Needless to say, dad got pictures of his kid. Now here’s the relevance to the World Cup. Shooting a FIFA event is not impossible, but it is complicated. Three months before the first game they opened up the World Cup media center for applications. Step one in the process is to get FIFA accreditation, and for photographers they want a picture of your current photo passes and a link to any recent published work. Accreditation is also on multiple levels, and in the end the Dallas Observer was accredited as a media organization along with me personally for photography, and my editor and a couple of staff writers who would sit up high in the press box. Accreditation also comes in different flavors depending on the reach of your publication, national or regional. If you were from a country that had a team in the tournament, that was a point in your favor as well. I really can’t say how many flavors there are, or how many factors FIFA has for determining this first layer of access. At the time it seemed like we were navigating Dante’s nine circles of hell, but in FIFA’s defense it is a huge undertaking with 48 countries sending teams and multiple venues across Mexico, Canada and the US. At the end of the day, we were accredited as a local/regional outlet and could apply for any of the games in Dallas (9) or Houston (6). Packed and ready to head to the game with a Nikon Z9 and 400mm, Z8 with my old F mount 70-200 and a Z6iii with the 20mm for crowd and reaction shots The media line to get through bag check could take up to an hour At this point, we had made progress but still did not have approval for the coveted game day pass that gets you in the door for a specific match. I guess we were in about the third circle of the inferno. As you may have guessed, there is an additional process for getting in the game. And an additional app. Group stage games are scheduled well in advance, so you know the times, venue and teams involved a solid month before kickoff. This is important for out-of-town visitors who are following a specific team and becomes one of the factors involved in game day approvals. Knockout games follow a similar process, but applications don’t open until the teams have been determined. At the Observer, we filled out our applications for the group stage well in advance and then waited with some trepidation for the approval confirmation. And waited. And waited. About a week before the first game, I ran into another photographer I know who shoots for the Associated Press and asked him if he had gotten his approvals yet and he looked at me curiously. He had been approved weeks ago. This brings up the whole discussion around pecking order, which I’ll get to in a second. The good news is that my editor and I got our email for the first game a couple days later and many happy face emojis were exchanged. Apparently, FIFA waits as long as they can to get a full list of applicants before passing judgement on who gets in. By the end of the tournament, we were approved for all the games we applied for (12) so I’m not complaining, but there were some nervous moments before that first notification arrived. Photographers checking their messages at half time Photographer pile up for team photos Remote cameras behind the goal For those of you who want to know about what it’s like to actually shoot a game, this is where it starts. Kind of. For photographers, there is one more level of hell to pass through, and it directly impacts the pictures you will capture. Each photographer is assigned a seat for the game, and there is an app to make your selection. When photographers get their approval notification, they are sectioned into groups A, B or C. The app opens up about 24 hours before kick-off. 10 AM local for A listers, 11 AM for the B group and 12 PM for the C group, which for photographers is kind of our very own group of death. Group A is basically the big wire services, and they normally have multiple shooters at every game. The most coveted seating positions are in the two corners on the side of the field with the team benches, because those corners give you the best opportunities to get celebration shots. There are about a dozen chairs in each corner, and they will all be taken when groups B and C get to make their selections. I was lucky enough to be in group B for 11 of the 12 games I shot, which means at best I would have access to the 30 th or so most desirable location and at worst 75th. When 11 AM rolls around, everyone in group B would click the link to get in the seating assignment queue. Click fast on a good internet connection and you may go to the front of the line, click slow and you will be in the back. A count down timer shows your progression in line, along with the available seats. When it is your turn you have 45 seconds to select a seat or you go to the back of the queue. Another complication is the configuration of American football stadiums. I prefer to sit on the sideline just outside the 6-yard box. But because the fields had to be widened for World Cup soccer, these sections of the field were extremely tight, and no seating was available until almost the 18-yard box. And yes, that was the grumbling part of the story. Happily, I’m over it. All I ever wanted was a seat at the table and I got one. Watching the clock click down while waiting to make my seat selection Once in line, you can see which seats are still available along with which seats will be taken by the time you get to choose. Group B, number 12. As they said at Chernobyl, “not great, not terrible”. Game days started early. Most of the games in Texas were played in the middle of the day, and you’ll want to show up 3 – 4 hours early to get a halfway decent parking spot. Along with 100 to 150 photographers, there are live broadcasts from the field, team photographers, FIFA photographers and the dreaded FIFA media partners with their i-phones and lack of any awareness about getting in the way. All media goes through the same security gate, and it could take as long as one hour to get in. Add in all the cops and bouncers and field marshals and hundreds of volunteers and the sidelines become pretty chaotic before the game starts. For the game, photographers are packed together cheek to cheek. Think of the space on an economy airline except you all have multiple cameras and 400mm lenses, laptops and wired internet connections. In my bag I carried a Nikon Z9 with the Z mount 400 f 2.8, a Z8 with a 70-200 and a Z6iii with a wider lens (normally 20mm). For the most part, everyone around you has a similar kit, and we are all watching the same game. For competent photographers, it’s straightforward to get a good picture at the World Cup. To get a picture that is unique is a whole different challenge, and to get a picture that no one else got is almost impossible. I know there are shots I got that made me giggle with happiness, and shots I missed that made me sick to my stomach on the entire drive home. Croatia v England. At a World Cup, the fan engagement is almost as big of a story as the games. This is especially true here in the States, where we just don’t see this kind of soccer loyalty. The visitors were great. Japan, England, Croatia, the Netherlands and swarms of Argentinians all brought international soccer joy to little old Dallas, drinking all our beer and eating up barbeque like they had just discovered the wheel. **5 frames with a Minox 35 GT and Kodak Ultramax 400**

The Minox 35 GT is a very small camera with zone focus, an impressive meter and wonderful sharp 35mm f/2.8 lens which belies its diminutive size. Its perceived virtues: – Small and light, being made of hard (high quality?) plastic. – I’ve heard claims it might be the smallest 35mm camera. – Resolution of the lens is quite lovely for its size. – Love the folding mechanism used to retract the lens. charming! – Yellow shutter button. a pop of colour – Works with modern battery: 4x LR44 Its perceived flaws: – A CdS light meter. you’ll pray to your favourite saint it does not spoil – Shutter will not fire without a battery. – Removing the film compartment is a bit of a pain. This wonderful review by Review by Gaston should be helpful to you if you’d like to know more details. Kodak Ultramax 400 Film is ISO 400 color negative film that is affordable, relatively, and much enjoyed. I feel it has vibrant, punchy colours with a nice amount of grain which I really appreciate. Its higher ISO allows it to be used for indoors and lower light work, though it definitely won’t catch a bat defecating in the Batu Caves. Its color palette is warm like Kodak Gold 200, but with subtler, less pronounced cool greens. Opinions on one’s inclination to these colours will abound and debated just as fiercely as tea versus coffee. The Flea Market I love the vibrancy of a flea market, the history of a place coming to life in the physicality (and admittedly dust and rust) of objects. I was particularly intrigued seeing children leafing through the old postcards. It reminds me of myself digging through my grandfather’s old bookshelf when I was little. The Minox’s metering is more than passable here. It uses CdS (Cadmium Sulfide) cells, more than a few copies do not work, my copy thankfully did. It appears to be a center-weighted metering, exposing for the mid frame. favouring exposure of the girl’s face rather than the dark jacket of the gentleman next to her. Like time, this metering seems to favour the young. The Eroded Groin of a Greek Statue. I started marshalling at motorsport events here in the UK about ten or so years ago. It followed going to a very sparsely attended event on a very cold, windy, and wet September day when the rain was horizontal and spiteful. I was hoping that the racing would take my mind off the weather, but in reality, it was an endurance race. Possibly to test the endurance of the punters like me standing around waiting for the cars to trundle around — which they did in ever-decreasing numbers. As for the spectators, it was a test of attrition slowly being lost. I’d been dimly aware of marshals before going to Silverstone that fateful day and had often wondered how they managed it. How they ‘got in’. At Silverstone they were the only people who seemed to be enjoying themselves. Crucially and unlike me, they appeared to be dressed for the weather. And either because of that, or just because they were marshals and were therefore ‘in’, were basking in warm banter and camaraderie. Chatting to them, it transpired that anyone can be a marshal, and it’s surprisingly easy to get in. Unless, of course, it’s the end of the season, which it pretty much was. So, the following March, there I was, a trainee marshal. Fast forward ten years, and many race meetings and circuits later, there I was in the pit lane at Brands Hatch, Kent, in southeast England. There are many marshalling disciplines but incident (running towards a burning car when everyone else is running away from it) and flag (analogue communication with a racing car driver ) are the main ones. And then there’s being in the pit lane and on the starting grid — which is where I currently volunteer. I occasionally sneak a point and shoot into my pocket to use in whatever downtime there is between qualifying or race sessions. The Nikon L35AF is perfect in these situations because it auto-everything, and fast and wide with its 35mm f/2.8 Sonnar design lens . Whip it out, frame the shot, press the button, and it’s back in the pocket. The reassuringly solid — and brick-like — L35AF handles focus, exposure, and film wind-on duties all by itself. My Nikon L35AF What it’s not so brilliant at is reading your mind and knowing when you want deep or shallow depth of field, and guessing where you want the focus point to be. That said, I think it does have an AF hold capability that I’ve so far failed to take advantage of. Some of these images suffer from these 1980s limitations and my inability to use the camera properly. **Dust and Decibels: Rave on Film**

News - August 28, 2026 by Guénola Pellen You can feel it: these two women lived the rave from within. It was woven into their daily lives, anchored by ritual, carried by tribe. A mosaic of faces and figures caught in the grain of the film: closed eyes, a bride in a tulle veil toasting amid a circle of laughing friends, half-smiles of ecstasy wrung out at dawn while the bassline pounds on. The book stands as an ode to the great family of the free party, in all its European guises: from Italy to the Czech Republic, where CzechTek drew crowds in the thousands, by way of the Basque Country and the Provençal countryside around Aix-en-Provence. That family, Cheyenne Clementi and Valentina Morandi first came to know separately. They crossed paths on the benches of the same art school in Milan, long before truly meeting in the mud and feedback of a wall of speakers. Both were already taking photographs, each in her own way, before finding common ground. “We had two different ways of looking at things, even though we were experiencing the same parties and were often together,” Cheyenne recalls. “One of us might be drawn to a face or a particular situation, while the other would be attracted to something completely different. And yet today, our photographs blend together perfectly.” From this alchemy between two eyes, no individual signature remains: throughout the book, the photographers chose never to specify who took which picture. “Maybe that’s exactly the point,” Valentina suggests: “two different perspectives that, over time, became a shared memory.” A deliberate anonymity, true to the spirit of the movement it immortalises. The book is equally striking for its total absence of visual hierarchy. A wall of speakers is photographed with the same gravity as a child playing with a dog in the dust of an encampment. “It tells exactly what we experienced: situations, speakers, trucks, children, dogs and people.” Conspiracy Sound Squad Truck Luigi and Adriano Post-party moments Eviction Attempt, CzechTek, Czech Republic That levelling of gaze builds, page after page, into the portrait of an entire life rather than a mere nocturnal pastime. Bodies stretched out by the water, a black dog standing sentinel before a tower of speakers, whole convoys of camper vans lined up on some improvised lot. “For more than seven years, we lived on four wheels, in trucks, moving between the road and squatted social centres. Never Alone is infused with that sense of freedom and community that defined our lives,” Cheyenne and Valentina explain. The sound system here is no mere party backdrop, but an itinerant domestic architecture in which the speaker stack stands in for the hearth, the truck for the sitting room, and the road for a permanent address. That happy nomadism, though, sits at odds with another constant running through the book, one that reads as stranger still today: the almost benevolent presence of the police. It is hard, from where we stand now, to picture a uniform ever blending into the scenery of a free party. And yet even in the rare frames where law enforcement meets the lens, no tension shows on any face. “What we experienced at the end of the 1990s can never be repeated,” they observe. “The police didn’t really know how to deal with us yet, and we were living in a time that was far less controlled than today. Now algorithms, cameras and surveillance permeate every aspect of our lives.” Children of the Bassline That sense of a lost innocence finds a pointed echo in the book’s foreword, written by Tobia D’Onofrio, who suggests that the children pictured here may be the very same young people now protesting against the authorities. In France, several bills aimed at further criminalising free parties are currently under consideration, following in the wake of the anti-rave decree passed by the Meloni government in late 2022. It is a lineage the photographers acknowledge without grandstanding. “Free-party culture hasn’t disappeared, and fortunately the new generations haven’t allowed themselves to be intimidated. Illegal rave parties are still being organised, less frequently and a little more under the radar, and we can only be happy about that.” And yet this continuity, however reassuring, raises an almost vertiginous question about the photographic act itself. The American writer Hakim Bey once theorised the rave as a “temporary autonomous zone,” a gesture designed by its very nature to vanish. Photography does precisely the opposite: it arrests time, fixes it onto a light-sensitive surface. Isn’t there something paradoxical in that? The two women wave the objection away with disarming simplicity. “When we took those photographs, our intention wasn’t to ‘document’ anything. We both had a passion for photography, and for us these were simply images of our everyday lives.” Cheyenne portrait of Toppy. Miki portrait, Level 57, Bologna, Italy. That same absence of calculation runs through their choice of medium: there is no retro affectation behind the exclusive use of black and white. “It was an editorial choice, since most of the photographs were originally shot in black and white. We have always loved black-and-white film. We also used to print the photographs ourselves in the darkroom, using an enlarger.” Out of that artisanal darkroom labour, certain images resurface with particular force. “Every photograph brings something back and stirs something in me,” Cheyenne confides. Valentina, for her part, remembers with precision Tequinox, a party held in Bologna in 1999: “We found the location for the party only a few days before it began, thinking it was abandoned. Several sound systems arrived, along with people from all over Europe.” One senses that night in one of the book’s photographs: a square overrun with vans and huddled figures beneath a low sky, where you can almost feel the bass still trembling underfoot. Rave Tekno Mobil Squad, Bologna, Italy, 2002. CzechTek, Czech Republic, 2004. In the end, the party itself was only ever the backdrop to something larger: the wanderings of a generation in perpetual motion, for whom the collective was the answer. Few places remain today where such lives would still be possible. The free party endures as one of the last spaces of self-organised, collective and free celebration, and it is precisely for that reason that it finds itself, across Europe, in the crosshairs of politics. **A Scorching Summer in Paris, with Augustin Pasquini**

Stories - August 27, 2026 by Jonas Cuénin A green metal footbridge, slightly battered, over the Canal Saint-Martin. Augustin Pasquini knows it by heart, keeps coming back, has learned to spot its regulars. This is where, he says, “the light hits best.” An open-air studio set, free of charge, courtesy of a municipal decision and a heat that refuses to let up. Summer 2026 will stay lodged in Parisian memory as the one where people stopped fleeing the city and learned to inhabit it differently, at a slower pace. Pasquini chose to stay, “for reasons of my own,” he says, almost bashfully. A lifelong Parisian, he had never seen a summer like this one. The temperature climbs, the pace collapses. “Goodbye to productivity,” he says, half-amused, half-dazed, like everyone else that summer. A floating slowness set in as early as June, an in-between state he tracked with his camera: “A gesture, a light, glistening skin.” The city, usually electric, turned humid, almost sleepwalking. Pasquini, used to capturing effervescence, had to learn to photograph waiting instead. Paris, 2026 © Augustin Pasquini Paris, 2026 © Augustin Pasquini Paris, 2026 © Augustin Pasquini Paris, 2026 © Augustin Pasquini Paris, 2026 © Augustin Pasquini It was in this humidity that the eclipse arrived. Montmartre, a late-summer afternoon, the crowd looking up, cardboard glasses strapped on. Pasquini remembers the 1999 eclipse, which he never photographed, and which had stayed with him. This time, he went with his sister. But when the moment came, he barely looked through his own glasses — he shot instead. “I did what I had to do, where I find the most meaning,” he says, true to an instinct that never fails him: stay close to the subject, even if it means missing the spectacle itself. He speaks of “a suspended moment of communion, a little strange, a little apocalyptic.” The phrase reads like an omen for the rest of the summer. Because it was the canal that occupied the photographer, body and soul, all season long. A political decision, the legalization of swimming, and suddenly the Canal Saint-Martin changed status, changed crowd, changed skyline. “The canal belongs to everyone now,” Pasquini observes, having grown up in the neighborhood and understanding, better than anyone, the scale of the shift. “It can never go back to how it was,” he repeats, almost incredulous at the force of a simple municipal decree. One might smile at that, except the phrase happens to be true: inflatable mattresses, paddleboards, soon jet-skis too, people joke on the embankment. Paris, 2026 © Augustin Pasquini Paris, 2026 © Augustin Pasquini Paris, 2026 © Augustin Pasquini Paris, 2026 © Augustin Pasquini Paris, 2026 © Augustin Pasquini Paris, 2026 © Augustin Pasquini Paris, 2026 © Augustin Pasquini Paris, 2026 © Augustin Pasquini The photographer settled in as a regular, a neighbor more than a hunter of images. Always with a 35mm: “I prefer to shoot everything at 35 millimeters, on Fujifilm,” a focal length that “forces you to move, to be close, present, connected, not to cheat.” On this green footbridge, he recognizes faces, greets local celebrity Hamza la douane and his friends, warns the more reckless jumpers: “Two meters fifty of depth, that’s not much.” A drunk young Englishman jumps anyway, gets hurt, comes back proud. Pasquini watches, photographs a few of the jumps, nothing more. There was also the day a man drowned in front of him, the stunned crowd, himself included, watching rescuers struggle in vain. He chose not to photograph it. Sometimes silence is worth more than a shutter click. From this suspended summer, Pasquini says he drew a confirmation rather than a revelation — color, 35mm, a warm color palette pushed even further. “When I started sorting through my images, and I saw my closeness, and these bodies, I told myself I had something,” he confides. Not a commissioned report, not confined to his own neighborhood — the street entire, all of Paris, under a heat that reshaped bodies and habits. A practice sharpened by repetition, the calm of a photographer who now knows exactly where he needs to be on the fifteenth of August. **Rania Matar’s Where Do I Go? documents Lebanon’s women through a question the country won’t stop asking**

From Where Do I Go © Rania Matar Rania Matar returned to Lebanon for years to photograph the women navigating a country in perpetual crisis. By the time her book was published, war had returned with a question impossible to ignore The Holiday Inn Beirut has not been repaired in 50 years. Back when it opened in 1974, it was a monument to the city’s glamour – a 28-floor tower of modernist optimism overlooking the Mediterranean. It was gutted in the first years of the Lebanese Civil War, when militias fought floor by floor for control of its height, using its rooftop as a sniper nest, burning it from the inside out. The Palestinian-Lebanese-American artist and photographer Rania Matar was living nearby, and remembers watching the ‘Battle of the Hotels’ from her apartment, as “every single room burned one by one.” Matar was a child when she watched the building die; half a century later she returned to photograph young women inside it, and this act is perhaps the quintessence of what Where Do I Go? لوين روح؟ is about. It took Matar “years” to get access to the former hotel, which is off-limits to the public and restricted under Lebanese Army control; even when she had permission to shoot, she had to climb a spiral staircase for 24 floors with her subject and two army escorts to do so. Since the Civil War, a deadlock has kept the hotel frozen, its ownership split between a Lebanese company that wants to convert it into condominiums and a Kuwaiti group that wants to demolish it. Its heavily damaged carcass continues to pose the question: what do you do with a wound you cannot seem to close? But revisiting it to take photographs was “pretty exhilarating”, Matar says. “Exhilarating, healing, whatever you want.” --> From Where Do I Go? © Rania Matar From Where Do I Go? © Rania Matar “If they're replying, then they're willing to be part of this, and I figure they're interesting enough to be willing to put themselves out there.” Rania Matar In psychology, the process of revisiting a charged experience to honour the feeling without being imprisoned by it is known as memory reconsolidation; for Matar, the project started organically after she visited Lebanon following the 2020 port explosion. Observing women working to reconstruct the city, she realised there was a wave of young people who wanted to leave the country, but unsure whether they should. “I saw myself in them,” she says. Matar left Lebanon in 1984, when she was 20; these women were the same age now. “My friends who didn't leave, their kids are leaving. It feels like there's a sense of history that keeps repeating itself,” she reflects. Over multiple visits, her approach stayed the same – put a call for subjects on Instagram, speak with whoever responded, and build a collaboration from there. “I don't ask for pictures or anything [before meeting],” she says. “If they're replying, then they're willing to be part of this, and I figure they're interesting enough to be willing to put themselves out there.” Despite the age gap, collaboration bloomed between Matar and her subjects, and their shared memories of the same country. One woman led her to the south of Lebanon, to a gigantic bomb crater in the middle of a gorgeous landscape, and a scene built around sandbags. “I give them so much credit that they're willing to do this,” Matar shares. “We went to this place, and she's standing on these sandbags where snipers hid during the war. And she was wearing, by her own choice, her mother's engagement dress. So there were two layers of memory here in some way. I would have never known these areas if she hadn't taken me there.” The same woman later took her to another site and told her, ‘I'm part of the land. The land is part of me. I want to be photographed naked in it’. “I'm so honoured that they would trust me to do things like that,” says Matar. From Where Do I Go? © Rania Matar From Where Do I Go? © Rania Matar This trust is evident in the work itself, in the 135 portraits that make up Where Do I Go?, an accumulated act of women – including Matar herself – inscribing themselves back into a still-contested landscape. Shot between 2020 and 2025, the monograph was co-published by Beirut’s Kaph Books and the Sidney and Lois Eskenazi Museum of Art last November; in March, full-scale war resumed in Lebanon, with Israeli ground operations in the south. One of the deadliest airstrikes of the conflict occurred in June, just before a ceasefire renewed on 19 June, and on the same day an exhibition of the work in Boston closed. The project was on gallery walls throughout. When I ask Matar whether work published by a Western-distributed press, and destined for those gallery walls, risks becoming the kind of consumable Middle Eastern grief Western audiences have long had an appetite for. She doesn't hesitate in her reply. “I completely am aware of a Western audience. But that's exactly why I don't want to include this,” she says, referring to straightforward images of destruction. Matar shot those images, and says doing so was important to her, but she deliberately decided to keep them out of the book. Such images tell the one truth Western audiences already know how to receive, she reflects – without being altered by it. She once walked out of an exhibition of Arab photographers, she says, because it was “all about the hijab and wars, catered to an American audience the way they want to see it”. What might seem like the most predictable question an interviewer can ask a photographer is crucial in this case – what do you hope someone will feel, looking at this in 30 years? “They might just look at it and say, ‘Here we are again’,” Matar laughs, burdened by the dark humour, by the idea that hope might be a luxury she can’t afford. “I'm hoping people would look at it like, ‘Oh my God, look how far we've come’.” Matar’s book has now been out for months, and will be republished in time for this year’s Paris Photo in November. The war continues to hang like a pendulum, casting its arc over everything beneath it. In the shadow, the question she found on a crumbling wall remains urgent: Where do I go?
