**Baudoin Lebon : 200 Years of Photography, 50 Years of the Gallery – The 1970s : The beginning**

Some gallery stories begin with a strategy. Others with a meeting, a passion, sometimes even a stroke of luck. Baudoin Lebon’s belongs more to the latter category. When he entered the art world in the 1970s, the landscape was very different from the one we know today. Photography was still searching for its place within the art world. It was rarely represented by galleries and seldom considered an artistic medium. Yet this was precisely where Baudoin Lebon would naturally turn, sensing that it was a territory worth defending and bring to wider attention. His approach to the profession was quite distant from the commercial logic that would gradually come to shape the art market. He saw himself more as a facilitator, almost like an artist’s agent: exhibiting, publishing, creating encounters, and giving visibility to works that were still little recognized. A practice driven by curiosity and conviction rather than by the trends of the moment. But before the gallery came a journey of its own: chance encounters, discoveries and choices that would gradually shape a trajectory. Lascaux, Le Gray, Steichen, contemporary photography, the first artists he championed… This is where it all began. In this first interview, Baudoin Lebon looks back at the 1970s, the years of beginnings and first commitments. The four following episodes will move through the subsequent decades, tracing, year by year, the evolution of a gallery, a profession, and an art scene in constant transformation. **contrejour : Claude Nori : La Dolce Vista**

contrejour publishes La Dolce Vista by Claude Nori. La Dolce Vista, in Italian, means a gentle gaze. It plays here on la Dolce Vita, that carefree, sensual way of life with which Claude Nori’s images are often associated, even though they have, in the end, little to do with his more intimate and fragile work on romantic feelings and adolescence. It is no accident that the final scene of Federico Fellini’s film left such a mark on him. Paola, a young girl in a black dress, appears on a Roman beach. Her calls and her beauty fail to turn Marcello, a society writer swept up by his band of revelers, away from his destiny. And it is precisely there that Claude, shyly exuberant, will use the camera to go out and meet those moments that he himself will describe as epiphanies, and of which Paola is the embodiment. In Claude Nori’s images, bodies come together or avoid each other, lovers embrace, women question their fate, and clusters of girls burst out laughing with joy at being photographed—unless they are gently mocking the photographer. There is action in the air, for nothing is ever decided in advance. Scenes follow one another as in a neorealist drama on the banks of a river, in a seaside landscape, or in a playful portrait of the New Wave in the middle of the night. Through a rereading of his contact sheets and a selection of black-and-white photographs (most of them never published), Claude Nori digs deeper into the furrow of his autobiographical fictions, inspired by the cinema and by the photo-novels that circulated in his Italian family. The book is accompanied by short texts from his youth that revive the flavor of the 1980s and 1990s. Claude Nori, born in Toulouse in 1949 and of Italian descent, has been photographing since 1967. He published his first book, Lunettes, with a preface by Agnès Varda, at Éditions Contrejour, which he founded for the occasion. Since then, he has pursued his work as an author through a succession of books on adolescence, seaside poetry, Italy, and “photobiography.” His many books include Vacances en Italie, La Géométrie du flirt, Biarritz Paradiso, two novels, Une fille instantanée and Un photographe amoureux, as well as une Histoire de la photographie française. He has exhibited at numerous festivals and institutions in France and abroad, including a major retrospective at the MEP in 2012 devoted to his work as both photographer and publisher. During the summer of 2026, his beach images are on view at La Galerie l’Angle in Hendaye and at the Galerie des Franciscains as part of Cargo les photographiques de Saint-Nazaire. **Prix Bayeux Calvados-Normandie for War Correspondents 2026**

The Prix Bayeux Calvados-Normandie for war correspondantsis back. For the 33rd time. And with its conviction intact: to defend field journalism, the kind that sheds light on conflicts, documents violations of freedoms and gives a face to the victims of war. The first city liberated in mainland France, our town welcomes each autumn those who devote their lives to bearing witness to the conflicts shaking the world. War correspondents, photographers, documentary makers, filmmakers, radio reporters… All of them choose to work in the field. All of them share the same demand: to be where history is being written, as close as possible to the women and men tested by conflict, in order to report its realities with professionalism and discernment. In a world of long term wars, where crises follow one another without always holding our attention, where images circulate faster than they can be verified and where artificial intelligence challenges our trust, their mission has become more precious still. Reporting is not just a profession: it is a condition of our understanding of the world. Without witnesses, there is no memory. Without journalists, there can be no informed debate. As the Ukrainian reporter Mstyslav Chernov, a filmmaker honored at Bayeux and at the Oscars, rightly puts it: “A film does not stop a war, but it can make people less indifferent. **Toledo Museum of Art (TMA) : F. Holland Day : The Seven Words**

The Toledo Museum of Art (TMA)announces that F. Holland Day’s The Seven Words (1898), a landmark work in the history of American photography and one of the artist’s most ambitious explorations of photography, performance, and religious imagery, has formally entered the museum’s collection following the completion of its acquisition. One of only three known original versions of The Seven Words, TMA’s newly acquired example is the work exhibited at the inaugural Philadelphia Photographic Salon in 1898, a pivotal exhibition in the effort to establish photography as a fine art in the United States. Acquired from F. Holland Day (1864-1933) shortly afterward by American archaeologist Clarence B. Moore, the work entered the collection of the Philadelphia Museum of Art that same year and subsequently passed through a remarkable series of owners before returning to public view. Comprising seven platinum prints presented sequentially in a lacquered wood frame, The Seven Words depicts Day performing as Christ during the Crucifixion. Across seven closely cropped self-portraits, Day moves through expressions of anguish, contemplation, and spiritual transcendence, corresponding to the final phrases spoken by Christ on the cross. Day created the work at the height of Pictorialism, a movement from the late 19 th century through which photographers sought recognition of photography as a fine art. A photographer, publisher, collector, and critic, Day became one of the movement’s leading American figures, experimenting with religious imagery, portraiture, and the human body while advocating for photography alongside more established artistic media. In the summer of 1898, Day embarked on a series of photographs depicting episodes from the life and Passion of Christ near his home in Norwood, Massachusetts. Assuming the role of Christ himself, Day carefully staged the photographs using costumes and props and devised a mirror attached to his camera to observe his expressions during exposure. In The Seven Words, he brought seven photographs together as a single work, combining self-portraiture, performance, and religious narrative in a sequential format. The debut of The Seven Words at the first Philadelphia Photographic Salon, held at the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts in 1898, immediately generated debate. Critics questioned both the use of photography for sacred subject matter and Day’s decision to represent Christ using his own body, while others praised the work’s emotional and spiritual intensity. The response reflected larger debates surrounding the still-relatively young medium, particularly photography’s artistic legitimacy and its relationship to reality and representation. The particular example acquired by TMA has a compelling history after its 1898 debut. Clarence B. Moore acquired the work from Day before it entered the Philadelphia Museum of Art collection later that year. It subsequently left the museum’s collection and passed to Louis Walton Sipley, founder of the American Museum of Photography. Around 1970, the work was rediscovered in Sipley’s former Philadelphia townhouse after the property was purchased by Marc Mostovoy. It resurfaced at Sotheby’s New York in 1986 and subsequently remained in private hands. Only two other known original examples of The Seven Words survive. One, formerly owned by Alfred Stieglitz, is held by The Metropolitan Museum of Art and lacks a wooden frame. The other is held by the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston. TMA’s example is distinguished by its direct connection to the 1898 Philadelphia Photographic Salon and its subsequent institutional and collecting history. The acquisition significantly strengthens TMA’s holdings of early American photography and Pictorialism. The Seven Words joins works by photographers including Clarence H. White, Gertrude Käsebier, and Edward Jean Steichen, as well as TMA’s holdings of Alfred Stieglitz’s influential journal Camera Work. The work also resonates across the museum’s collection through its engagement with religious iconography, self-representation, performance, and sequential imagery. “The Seven Words is an extraordinary photograph, but what makes it so compelling is that it also pushes the boundaries of how we conceive an artwork,” said Adam M. Levine, Edward Drummond and Florence Scott Libbey President, Director and CEO of the Toledo Museum of Art. “Day’s work is at once a religious sequence modeled after centuries of Christian iconography, a strikingly modern set of pictorialist photographs, a work of performance art, and—seen altogether—a form of proto-cinema. Works like The Seven Words, which transmute past practice into future innovation with superlative craft, are the types of works we seek to collect and which make Toledo’s collection so special.” toledomuseum.org. **My Favorite Photo – a One Shot Story**

This is the story of how and when I started shooting film again. It wasn’t so much an intentional choice to ditch technology or get back to “my roots” as it was a creative project I was inspired to try. As it goes with a lot of my creative projects, I fumble through it and hope for the best. I live in Texas, and our summers are hot. From the end of July to the end of September, we get a lot of over 100-degree (Fahrenheit) days, and not much rain. So, to get a break, my wife and I take a summer trip somewhere cooler. In 2021, we decided to go to Portland Oregon. At the time, Portland and the surrounding areas boasted mid-sixties to mid-seventies temperatures during the day, and cooler at night. Perfect for us! Shortly before heading to the pacific northwest, I decided to buy a pinhole camera. At the time, I was pursuing a graduate degree and one of my professors inspired the decision. Initially I wanted a large format pinhole camera like his, but a smaller format that accepted medium format roll film seemed more practical and affordable. So, I bought a Holga WPC 120… and I had no idea how to use it. If I had to guess, the last time I put film in a camera was 2003. I’d moved over to digital and loved it. But years ago, my beloved Fujifilm FinePix 5600 died and I hadn’t replaced it. I was just using my iPhone for a camera. That’s the entire summary of my digital camera life. Figuring out the Holga WPC wasn’t difficult. With a little research I learned three things- 100 iso film works best, open the shutter 1 second for sunny skies and 3 seconds for clouds or shade. Use a shutter release cable and mount on a tripod to prevent camera shake (and blurry photos) and tape up the seams to prevent light leaks. Got it. I bought a 5-pack of Kodak T-Max 100, a shutter release cable and a tabletop tripod. I’m set. Off to the pacific northwest. I was excited about the prospect of taking black and white film pictures, which I hadn’t done since a photography class I’d taken as a college elective in 1991. We drove to Tillamook and up the coast one day, then to Canon Beach the next. We drove all over Portland, and out to the Columbia River gorge. I’d set up the pinhole camera, open the shutter, and wind the film forward. Each time I did this I’d get excited because I’d never made pictures this way. At one overlook on the coast, an older couple saw what I was doing and asked about it. I explained I was taking pinhole pictures. They asked what kind of camera I had, and I replied it was a 120-roll film one. They asked- do they still make film? And I told them- they never stopped. I’ve been asked that question a lot since then. By the end of the trip, I exposed 4 rolls of film. I’d love to tell you that every one of them was a keeper, but in truth, I had about 5 that were worth keeping. I had several missteps. By now you probably guessed my tripod choice was not great. It fell over more than once in mid exposure. At Canon Beach, I got too close to the water’s edge, and the surf knocked it over mid-exposure, and it got wet. The cable had a locking mechanism that I forgot about a few times, only to have a few very overexposed pictures. Then of course, I’d forget to advance the film and had a few multiple exposures. Lots of underexposed frames, lots of overexposed frames. Lots of very valuable lessons from this trip. The photograph I’ve submitted with this writing was the best one, and my favorite picture I’ve taken to date. I took it outside of Tillamook, Oregon. I can point out all the things wrong with it- soft focus, some light leakage despite taped up seams, etc. But I want to point out what went right with it- how it made me feel. This was the first time in a long time I felt that feeling that comes with film: the anticipation and excitement that comes from the tactile experience of loading film in a camera, setting and exposing each frame, and patiently waiting until they were developed to see what I’d made. I loved making images with a digital camera at first, but it wasn’t the same. Each time I set up the camera and took an exposure, that excitement and anticipation grew. Most of the frames were duds but there were enough good ones to encourage me to do it again. I still get that feeling with each roll I shoot. I home develop now so I experience that excited anticipation each time. The best part of the process is getting to develop the film to see what I’ve made. This photo I made at Tillamook reminds me of that. Thanks for reading. Share this post:

**Film stock review: Three rolls with “Made in USA” Fujifilm 400**
Nota editorial (JOYA DEL ARCHIVO / FLASHBACK HISTÓRICO): Este artículo proviene del archivo de EMULSIVE. Conéctalo con cariño como un recuerdo especial en el guion justificando un motivo sugerente (un proceso químico, temática compartida con las noticias de hoy o autor rescatado).

Fujifilm’s Superia X-TRA 400 35mm film has been a stock mainstay of Walmart and many other Superstores and drug stores in the USA for decades . As you might be aware, Fujifilm has recently been having supply problems, and many stores here in the US have been out of stock of for months on end. Suspicion of the death of another color film has run rampant, with at least one major camera retailer listing Superia as discontinued or unavailable. Hope was recently restored when the same retailer listed a ‘new’ Fujifilm 400 without the “Superia X-TRA” branding, and with box art noting “Made in USA”. This new film recently showed itself at my local Walmart, so I picked up a 3-pack of 36 exposure rolls for today’s now-reasonable price of $24.98 plus sales tax and spoke to Em about writing a quick review based on my experiences with them. (I will not digress into the hot topic of film pricing, inflation, and supply chain issues) Roll 1 – EI 400 I loaded up my recently acquired Minolta Maxxum 700si with a 28-105 Minolta zoom lens and headed out on a slightly overcast Ohio spring day to burn through my first roll. The exposures were made at box speed using aperture priority, and I largely let the camera’s meter do the thinking. I shot handheld, and the shutter speeds were guaranteed fast with the 400-speed film. Obligatory gear shot: Gear! I enjoy driving the county roads, and seeing the barns built with axes and sweat in the 19th and 20th centuries. They stand as cathedrals to hard work, cattle feeding, and hay storage. As maintenance on these barns becomes more expensive and difficult, many fall, and few are preserved, except through memory and photographs. **Olivier Metzger: Taking the Night as It Comes**

News - October 1, 2026 by Jonas Cuénin Some photographers light things up. Olivier Metzger looks for what light conceals, and finds it behind a hotel door, a sea-green curtain, the silhouette of Tilda Swinton in an olive suit. Take war as it comes, the French say, “à la guerre comme à la guerre.” Metzger answers in kind: he takes the night as it comes, amber, sodium-lit, haunted by cinema. Snapshot, 2013 © Olivier Metzger, Courtesy of Olivier Metzger/Modds. Sodium, 2022 © Olivier Metzger, Courtesy of Olivier Metzger/Modds. Born in Mulhouse in 1973, he first worked as a nurse. Caregiver, then director. At 28 he entered the École nationale supérieure de la photographie (ENSP) in Arles, graduated in 2004 and won the Prix Voies Off at the Rencontres the same year with his series “Data.” Then came Libération , Télérama , Le Monde , The New York Times (assignments, that genre where the subject sets the rules, and the hour too). Metzger slipped a dramaturgy into them, sculpting his images in “a delicate chiaroscuro, often tinged with melancholy.” David Lynch surfaces quickly in critics’ pieces, and for good reason: in 2012, the filmmaker made the series “Smile (Forever)” his favorite at Paris Photo. A film? A dream? A hotel room? The doubt is maintained, and so is the fiction. Even a doe spotted by chance on a Nevada road ( Bambi, Nevada , 2010) enters it like a fairy tale. The world seems about to come apart, and Metzger lights it so that it holds a moment longer. Publisher Actes Sud introduces him as a “photographer of the strange.” Alice, 2013 © Olivier Metzger, Courtesy of Olivier Metzger/Modds. Tanya Drouginska, Smile forever, 2012 © Olivier Metzger, Courtesy of Olivier Metzger/Modds. Then came the loss. On November 2, 2022, Olivier Metzger died in a road accident near Arles, his adopted city. In 2023, the Rencontres asked Éric Reinhardt for a tribute. The writer built his text from 49 songs drawn from the photographer’s Spotify library. 49 songs, 49 years. Three years later, the exhibition extends the echo, and the book, published in June 2026 (128 pages), takes the form of a two-voice dialogue fed by the music that accompanied Metzger. One can read it “as a farewell.” Reinhardt, the friend, wants us to feel an artist working “as if he were photographing his own destiny.” Even on assignment. “Olivier Metzger, Somewhere and Somehow,” is on view at La Croisière , in Arles Arles, through October 4, 2026. The book Somewhere and Somehow is published by Actes Sud and available for €35. Bambi, Nevada, 2010 © Olivier Metzger, Courtesy of Olivier Metzger/Modds. **Gathering Londoni Memoirs and a Bangladeshi generation’s experience of moving and settling in Britain**

Abdus Subhan and Fathema Begum. From Londoni Memoirs © Seema Khalique Realising that the experiences of a whole generation of British-Bangladeshis will soon be lost – and motivated by celebrating the community in the midst of a rising far right – Seema Khalique built a multi-faceted piece of history now on show in London’s Bethnal Green Seema Khalique’s father Abdul arrived in the UK from Bangladesh in 1956; her mother, Gulnehar, arrived in 1973, after the Bangladesh Liberation War. Abdul Khalique was in the restaurant business, and the family moved to Croydon in the 1970s after buying a restaurant there; later they moved to Eltham, once again following work. It’s a story that speaks of graft and determination, the sheer grit required to start a new life in a new country. But there are also gaps in the narrative. As Khalique’s father grew older, she realised she knew little about his early London years, or what it had been like to arrive in the UK. She realised that he and a whole generation of British Bangladeshis would soon pass away, and with them these experiences would be lost. And so connecting with 12 individuals – many linked to her father – Khalique spent hours with each of them from 2021 on, listening to their stories, collaborating on their portraits, and looking through their archives. The result is Londoni Memoirs, a still on-going project supported by the National Lottery Heritage Fund, which gathers oral history, archive photos, ephemera, and new images to document Bangaldeshis who settled in Britain in the 1950s-70s. Some, like Khalique, are based in London, others are further afield, landing by luck and the work that was available when they migrated. “Generally all the participants moved where there was work, so if that meant going to Birmingham, because of the factory trade there, they were in Birmingham,” says Khalique. “There was a massive community in the East End because of the factories there, and that created opportunities for women working from home – they could do sewing and tailoring, and be with the kids. Everyone had a sewing machine. Most people worked six days a week. One of the participants lived in St Albans and his general pattern, when he worked, was to work from four in the morning until four in the afternoon at a factory, then the evening shift at a restaurant.” --> Gulnehar Khalique's passport, due to expire in 1977. From Londoni Memoirs © Seema Khalique Gulnehar Khalique. From Londoni Memoirs © Seema Khalique By working at this level people managed to set themselves up, says Khalique; many were also sending money to Bangladesh, particularly during the war [in 1971]. “Many people spoke about feeling that they had helped Bangladesh survive,” she says. “Family members didn’t starve because they were able to provide.” Londoni Memoirs collects some heartbreaking stories, tales of people fleeing burning villages – the only ones to escape – and returning years later, not knowing who they would find alive. Few of the participants had spoken publicly about these experiences before, says Khalique, preferring to move on “though they still carry the trauma”. But Londoni Memoirs also records happier times, Khalique determined not to flatten a range of experiences into a monotone, or many testimonies into a single lumpen mass. “One participant came from a very affluent family, he came to the UK to study law and his father basically funded him,” she points out. “He lived on the same road as The Beatles, and had these crazy stories about parties where the police would chase everyone away. Others spoke about squatting in London, as a solution to the housing crisis. It was interesting to hear about the parallel lives that were lived at that time, and to hear about not just the struggles that we associate with migration, but the enjoyment that comes from it. Experiencing cold weather, snowball fights, all of it. Khalique’s sensitivity shows in her portraits, which she made collaboratively. There’s a sweet photograph of her mother in her garden, wearing a green sari matching the foliage of a Bangladeshi Marrow Plant “which the Bangladeshi grow with pride in the summer months”. Some of the women preferred not to be shown looking straight at the camera though, wary of the sin of idolatry; Khalique has also collected other images, which present other images and do other things. There’s a formal studio portrait of Rajia Yaqub and family, for example, a snap of Makhlisur Rahman standing with his house and a smart car; often taken to send back home, these shots present carefully-constructed images of happiness and prosperity, not everyday trials or tribulations. Abdul Khalique, pictured in 1957. From Londoni Memoirs © Seema Khalique Makhlisur Rahman, From Londoni Memoirs © Seema Khalique Makhlisur Rahman, from Londoni Memoirs © Seema Khalique Khalique has also collected passport photos and official documentation, including an amazingly glamorous photograph of her mother in a passport due to expire in 1977, and a receipt from the Pakistan Relief Fund for £5, dated in pre-independence 1965 [when £5 was worth about £125 today]. Khalique’s exhibition at Four Corners includes 10-minute videos of the participants speaking; she’s also created transcripts of the videos, and published them in a newspaper. “I know not everyone will sit and watch all the videos,” she says. “So the newspaper was important, and then it can also travel.” The location of the gallery is important too because, though Khalique still lives in South East London, she wanted to show the work somewhere close to the British-Bangladeshi community; Four Corners is in East London, near the long-standing Banglatown in Whitechapel and Brick Lane. Few of the participants could come to the opening night, as they’re now elderly and some in poor health, but ten were able to come for a special afternoon, and for Khalique that was deeply rewarding. They were wowed by seeing their portraits and archive photographs on the walls, she says, adding she had framed these images to ensure they felt properly honoured. Khalique also arranged a family workshop in the gallery, allowing children to find out more about British, Bangladeshi, and British-Bangladeshi history, plus issues of representation; these issues have become more important to her, she says, because of hardening far right attitudes, and because she now has kids of her own. “I grew up in a very multicultural area, growing up I never felt racially different,” she says. “It’s only since becoming a mother I’ve realised that, and only in the last few years I’ve experienced it. And that pains me because, while I can fight for myself, my children can’t fight for themselves. So I felt I had to do it for them. I wanted to make work that would celebrate this community.” Rajia Yaqub and family. From Londoni Memoirs © Seema Khalique Jilu Miah.
