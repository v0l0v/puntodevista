**Experimenting with the Invisible: Hugh Turvey on the Art of Xogram Photography**
Fotógrafos: xogram, lynne57

Hugh Turvey is a UK-based artist whose photography background led him into unique territory with the development of the xogram, an experimental technique using x-ray photography and black-and-white film. The xogram takes the historical techniques of photograms to a futuristic plane, bringing invisible imagery into the world of art and science. Hugh spoke to Lomography Magazine about some of his innovative installations and projects, from creating interactive smartwatch technologies to humanizing healthcare experiences through his discipline-defying work. Elephant Skull Part of Series for National Geographic Magazine, USA 2013. Femme Fatale, Artemi 2012 Limited Edition #50. Air 2018 Limited edition #60. © All Rights Reserved Hugh Turvey 2026. Hi, Hugh, and welcome to Lomography Magazine! Please introduce yourself to our readers and tell us how you first came into contact with the world of photography. Hello, Lomographers! I’m Hugh Turvey. My journey into photography started as a kid in the UK, influenced by my artist aunt in the Bahamas and foraging sessions in local camera shops near the Royal Photographic Society (RPS) in Bath. My first camera experience was with a Kodak Instamatic 104. Today, I’m an RPS Honorary Fellow, Accredited Senior Imaging Scientist and the inaugural Artist in Residence at the British Institute of Radiology in London. Through global brand collaborations, exhibitions, and talks, I continue to explore radiography as both scientific document and artistic expression. Shell Montage National Geographic, USA 2024. © All Rights Reserved Hugh Turvey 2026. You have developed a very unique use of black-and-white film – the xogram. Can you tell us more about the xogram process, and where your inspiration for using radiography came from? I originally started my career wanting to be a rock & roll photographer, but when I was asked to create a revealing image for a 7” single, it led to a serendipitous lunchtime meeting with the Royal Free Hospital imaging lead, who turned out to be a fellow photo nerd. He introduced me to 14”x17” film, and I’ve never looked back. My hands-on, unconventional photographic process relies on large 32 ISO B&W film, exposed with light you cannot see! I push the technique to its limits – overexposing, underexposing, multiple-exposing, dodging, burning, filtering, and always embracing happy accidents in pursuit of a radiographic aesthetic – which I call xograms. As a photographer, how do you approach the subject of a xogram? Does this medium require a different understanding of composition, light, and space compared to traditional photography? Traditional camera photography uses a lens, shutter, and aperture to capture light reflecting off a subject. Photograms – what Man Ray called Rayographs and William Henry Fox Talbot originally termed photogenic drawings – strip away those optical controls entirely. Made without a camera by placing objects directly onto light-sensitive material, they rely solely on contact, film speed, and exposure time to create 1:1 scale silhouettes from visible light. My xograms take that camera-less, tactile process into invisible territory. Instead of camera settings or traditional darkroom timers, I work with less familiar imaging parameters: kV (kilovolts) controls the radiation's energy and penetration depth, while mA (milliamps) and time determine the total intensity and exposure (mAs) needed to capture internal detail. Blue Peter Appearance with Barney Harwood 2014 Paul Stuart © All rights reserved Paul Stuart 2026. Simply put, the fundamental difference between my xograms and historic photograms is the frequency of the radiation. Both visible light and X-rays exist on the electromagnetic spectrum, but X-rays operate at vastly higher frequencies (30 petahertz to 30 exahertz) than visible light (400 to 790 terahertz). Because of those shorter wavelengths and higher energy, X-rays pass directly through solid objects – allowing me to compose not just with the surface shadows of a subject, but with its hidden internal structure and density. Do you have a favorite xogram series so far, and why? I have several that I like for different reasons, be that either sentimental, big sale, technical achievement, subject matter or surprise result. Choosing a favorite project is tough because each one serves a different purpose, but one that holds a special place for its sheer surprise factor is my global collaboration with Google for Android Wear OS. I was one of ten artists worldwide commissioned to design interactive watch faces for the launch of their smartwatch platform. What made this project so distinct was translating my work into a dynamic, real-time digital experience on someone’s wrist. As the time shifted or as you interacted with the watch face, the layers of the floral xograms would subtly adjust in a kaleidoscopic form. It stands out to me because it completely flipped how people encounter radiographic art. Google suggested calling the watch face XOGRAM…(FYI, sadly no longer available on the App Store, before you all start looking for it…soz). St Pauls Walden Bury, Snow Drops 2023. His Parents' Stuff Series 2022. © All Rights Reserved Hugh Turvey 2026. In 2009, you became the first artist in residence at The British Institute of Radiology. Art and science often appear to be very unrelated fields. In your experience, how can art and science work together today, and how does the medical world influence your creative vision personally? People often talk about art and science as opposites, but in practice, they’re driven by the exact same instinct, intense curiosity about what’s hidden metaphorically ‘beneath the surface’. Science gives us the tools to observe reality at a level our eyes can't naturally see, and art gives us a way to interpret, humanize, and feel something about those discoveries. The entanglement of art and science is profound – where science explains, art expresses, and in my opinion, art and science are clearly paramours. “Science gives us the tools to observe reality at a level our eyes can't naturally see, and art gives us a way to interpret, humanize, and feel something about those discoveries.” My residency at The British Institute of Radiology was a turning point for me. It was a formal recognition and appreciation of how the radiographic aesthetic can engage people more profoundly with the world around them. Another incredible benefit is that it keeps me abreast of the latest trends and technological developments. You have also hosted many workshops with the public. Has the outcome or response from this type of collaboration ever surprised you? Do you have any favorite moments you’d like to share? When you mention X-rays or hospital imaging departments, people naturally think of clinical diagnoses or illness. During my residency at Yeovil District Hospital – supported by the Wellcome Trust – we ran workshops where hospital staff, patients, and the public brought in personal items to be x-rayed. But as soon as they see x-rays of more familiar objects, a switch flips – they stop seeing medical machinery as intimidating, and it humanizes the process. Xogram handshake 2006. © All Rights Reserved Hugh Turvey 2026. A favorite moment, in another workshop to demo the differences between visible light and x-rays at the Cheltenham Science Festival – supported by Siemens – I provided sharpened yellow pencils with a rubber to represent part of the electromagnetic spectrum...the idea being they poke each other with the rubber end (visible light that cannot penetrate and reflects from it) and then poke each other with the sharpened end (x-ray ‘light’ that can penetrate). Obviously, it was meant to be a silly metaphor, and I didn’t expect the participants to poke each other so enthusiastically with the sharpened end! This activity lasted one workshop before health and safety intervened. Many of your artistic projects have a social and community-inspired purpose. Can you tell us a bit more about your intended audience and the goals of your recent public-facing work? My intended audience extends far beyond traditional gallery visitors – it’s anyone navigating the liminal spaces of everyday life, whether a patient in a hospital corridor or a commuter passing through a public garden. My goal is to spark curiosity and demystify technology. In healthcare, where clinical settings can trigger anxiety, community-focused art is central to my practice. A key milestone is an ongoing project with Benenden Hospital in Kent, where we integrated radiographic art directly into the building's architecture. I collaborated directly with healthcare professionals, interior designers, and architects to create large-scale xogram murals. By expanding these invisible structures into immersive wall installations, we turn cold waiting rooms and corridors into calming, restorative spaces – offering a calming visual distraction for patients in vulnerable moments while creating an inspiring workplace for healthcare teams. The installation engages the public with themes of sustainability, habitat, and natural medicine in high-footfall public spaces. In “Rowers” Faulkner turns his attention to his collegiate rowing team to explore his own relationship to the act of looking. Portraits of rowers drenched in sweat, sprawled on the ground, and gasping for breath explore the erotic masochism of self-induced pain endemic to male sports culture; this excess of exhaustion becomes a metaphor for the exhaustion of hegemonic masculinity.” Jacob Faulkner participated in our 2026 Book Award open call and made our shortlist.

**Panasonic 30mm f/2.8 macro – A Compact “Normal” Lens for Micro Four Thirds**

Being interested in both the mountains and photography , I am frequently on the lookout for small, light and affordable equipment. In the digital realm, I find that micro four thirds (m43) satisfies my needs (I rarely print above A3 size) and generally exceeds my abilities and shot discipline. Some of the smallest interchangeable-lens digital cameras are of this format (although there was the Nikon 1 series), and most importantly, when considering the system as a whole, the small sensor size and short back-flange distance of the m43 standard are directly reflected in the modest size and weight of many of the lenses. The Panasonic/Lumix 30mm f/2.8 is a 9-element (one aspheric) 9-group lens that can reproduce at up to 1:1 magnification. It has no controls other than an electronic focus ring (no focus limiter, a feature of some other macro lenses). It was introduced in 2015, and perhaps because of its age and because m43 manufacturers have other, higher-specification lenses for macro photography (the Olympus 90mm comes to mind), retails sometimes for a modest price. It weighs only 180 grams, and together with the (discontinued, and now unobtanium ) Lumix GM5 camera (211 grams with just battery and card; 254 grams configured with a minimalist strap and a small Arca-compatible plate that does not block the battery/card door), makes the nearly perfect normal-angle compact kit for me. The “nearly” is because neither the lens nor the camera is weather-resistant, and because the tiny electronic viewfinder of the GM5 is woefully inadequate. The front element of the 30mm macro lens is very exposed; I leave a 46mm-thread lens hood (from Kenko, which itself has a 49 mm front thread) attached at all times. Importantly, when using with the GM5 or other cameras lacking this feature, the 30mm macro has image stabilization. If used on Panasonic cameras that have in-body stabilization, the 30mm can provide “dual” stabilization, which further increases the shooting envelope of the system. Sadly, for those mixing-and-matching Olympus bodies and Panasonic lenses, the built-in stabilization of the latter cannot coordinate with the in-body stabilization of cameras from Olympus/OM System. So, small, light and cheap(ish), but is the Panasonic 30mm macro any good? Here are three photographs taken in the mountains of central Japan under a steady drizzle (which the camera and lens survived). The 100% crops show that the lens is wringing resolution out of the ancient 16 megapixel GM5 sensor into the corners of the frames. The rear bokeh can leave something to be desired, and is the only substantial shortcoming I have seen. In my hands, the 30mm lens’ image stabilizer helps obtain, reproducibly, sharp shots down to about 1/8 sec. Tsubakurodake, a popular mountain in central Japan (a clutch of climbers is visible on the summit) 100% crop shows sharpness limited by sensor noise Komakusa (Dicentra peregrina), the “queen of Japanese Alpine flowers” 100% crop shows little field curvature Bilberries (busy rear bokeh) In urban settings, the 30mm lens is unobtrusive and cheap-looking and generally does not attract much attention (I’ve only gotten comments from other photography enthusiasts, and it’s usually been about selling them my GM5, not about the attached lens.) As a “normal” lens, it is slightly longer than typical now (think 58mm lens on a 35mm camera; the m43 format diagonal is about 22mm). I like my normal lenses on the longer side, and I use the 30mm even in situations where weight is not at an absolute premium. I think its sharpness, versatility and spirit (small, not very fast, and can be called on to do macro) make it perhaps analogous ( mutatis mutandis ) to the storied 50mm f/2.0 Dual-Range Summicron of yore. **Curtain of Divinity**

As she glides past, her hair flowing, the flowers falling, she spins into a perfect planet grounded by its own gravitation. Somewhere between performance and expression there is abandon. A sweet surrender to ecstasy and the body moves as it wills; dangerous, powerful, soft, sharp, abrupt and smooth. Curtains of divinity, explores the South Asian and Sufi principles of the body as vessel of expression and movement with purpose, meaning, direction and rasa (essence/taste). Inspired by the muse of French couturier Paul Poiret, Indian-Franco dancer Nyota Inyoka, a classical dancer who blended the sacred with the contemporary in 1920s Paris. Famed for her eclectic performances that became a source of inspiration and renewed interest in Indian aesthetics. How can fashion answer the questions of abstraction, mysticism and style, is what curtains of divinity explores through the body. The body has always served as a source of inspiration, aspiration and even control. Stepping towards a different narrative, this project explores the intimate, deep and fleeting moments that define existence via movement. The shapes we make, the personas we adapt, the direction we take, our physical existence is marked by the depth of our sentiments and heritage. As a dancer enacts a story through the body, or spins into perfect unison to tether closer to the divinity of existence; in that brief moment, abandon is immortalized. Raw abandon, fana, annihilation, the absolute ecstasy of existence; a lovestruck fool, what oceans are crossed for, the space between words, the sophistication of simplicity, in those moments’ life soars. That fleeting beauty is difficult to capture, but that’s what should define artistic vision. Our lives are increasingly defined by what we have access to in terms of materiality, clothes, and the roles we are expected to perform based on those markers. Designer labels are attached to a brand identity that we are, at times, conditioned to embody. Yet, the very platforms that base themselves on artistic freedom become the ones that control the conditions of expression. In an oversaturated existence, it can seem like a challenge to break beyond and express with the body as it exists in space, true to its own soul, allowing the clothes to live out. Where so much of our movement has become regulated and curated, where do we find the essence of our true selves? As we try to make sense of a world that is obsessed with speed: fast fashion, quick delivery, options, easy access, the world at your fingertips, and any and every information you could need with a tap of a finger. Every step we take from the moment we wake up to when we sleep can be branded, monetized and regulated into a ‘lifestyle’. In the race to make some aspect of our existence reap rewards, we have quite simple forgotten how to let ourselves live without inhibition. From our skin, the food we eat, getting ready, a walk in the park, our day at work we managed to tie it all into an aesthetically crafted displayable cinematic scene tied with a bow of a socially popular trend. In all this mess, we have forgotten what we truly want and even to sit with that question. The connection to ourselves is falling apart. Curtains of divinity answers this with raw vulnerability, paying homage to heritage, history and structure yet allowing personal expression to come through in space as a free body.

**Performance-to-value: A quick look at some vintage East German Carl Zeiss Jena lenses**
Nota editorial (JOYA DEL ARCHIVO / FLASHBACK HISTÓRICO): Este artículo proviene del archivo de EMULSIVE. Conéctalo con cariño como un recuerdo especial en el guion justificando un motivo sugerente (un proceso químico, temática compartida con las noticias de hoy o autor rescatado).

When I grew up during the Cold War, every product from the Eastern Bloc was considered junk. Take the Trabant and its 2-stroke engine. While it’s gone on to cult status, it remains a laughably horrible vehicle. You might think lenses from East Germany’s Carl Zeiss Jena (CZJ) are in the same camp — trendy with a cult following — but they deliver top-tier performance and a pleasing look. They are for the most part, very affordable, at least at present. That is changing as more photographers become interested in them and discover their performance-to-value ratio. Left to Right: Contax RF CZJ Mount Biometar 35 f2.8, Pentacon Mount CZJ Flektogon 50 f4; Pentacon Mount Biometar 80 f2.8 For me, lens performance is more than the highest sharpness, acutance, and micro contrast. I prize that, but it’s also about the bokeh (the out-of-focus area) and how the lens balances acutance, contrast, and bokeh to deliver a unique rendering. The CZJ lenses have a good balance in this regard. In this short article, I cover my experience with three East German CZJ lenses: In Pentacon Six mount , the Biometar f/2.8 80mm and the Flektogon f/4 50mm In Contax RF mount, the Biometar f/2.8 35mm. First, the warts The soft alloys used in the construction of these lenses were designed to cut costs, and the consequence is potentially worn mounts and/or seized focus helicoids. I say potentially , as I’ve had lenses that are just fine, and others that had issues. Cosmetic appearance of the lens is not the best indicator. The alloys in the Pentacon Six mount lenses are consistently better than the Contax rangefinder mount lenses. The danger with the Contax mount Biometar is that the all-aluminum construction invites galvanic reaction exhibiting as self-welding of the lens cartridge to the focusing assembly. This can result in super stiff focusing and can be unrepairable. Some lenses have it, some don’t. The quality control on lens focusing is next: I’ve had one CZJ lens where the focus was improperly set. whether it was factory or reseller-based in origin, I’m not sure. But it was entirely fixable. That’s it. The optical formulas and coatings (while not modern Zeiss T level) are well done, and I’ve not seen any issues with flaws (peeling) or soft coatings. What about the cost? The Pentacon Six lenses are generally very affordable compared to West German equivalents. It’s interesting that at the time of sale, the CZJ lenses were around 45% of the cost of the West German lenses. That gap is still there but slowly closing today. The exception is the Contax II mount Biometar 35 f/2.8 which is now more expensive that a Zeiss Option Biogon or the Zeiss 35 Planar, but that’s due to the rarity of having only 2500 CZJ Contax mount Biometars made. Optical design? CZJ lenses utilise high-performing designs. The history of the post-World War II split of Carl Zeiss into Carl Zeiss Jena and Zeiss Opton/Zeiss Oberkochen is outside the scope of the article. But it’s worth remembering that Zeiss Jena was the original location of Zeiss. Wide open under bright skies, the Biometar can show slight corner darkening, which goes away quickly by f/4 – 5.6. Focusing: once I had the lens’ old marine-based lubricants replaced with modern ones, the Contax mount Biometar focus was smooth and pleasant. Pentacon Six mount Biometar 80 and Flektogon 50 The Pentacon mount Biometar 80 f/2.8 and the Flektogon 50mm are on par with the equivalent genre Zeiss lenses – the C lenses of the Hasselblad system of the 60s/70s. The aperture clicks don’t quite have the same rock-solid feel and aperture clicks as the West German Zeiss lens, but they are still positive and smooth. But is the Flektogon still competent for stunning 30×30 enlargements? Absolutely. I prefer the out-of-focus bokeh of the CZJ lenses. To try or not to try? If you’re a Contax II/ IIa RF fan like me, owning the CZJ Biogon 35mm is a treasured experience using a rare piece of glass. The performance trumps its Zeiss Opton cousins, but it’s not without significant risk mechanically. It is a bit of DDR roulette. If you’re considering the CZJ Pentacon 6 mount lenses as an alternative to pricier glass, dive in. The results are very satisfying. **Melissa Auf der Maur’s ’90s Grunge Diary**

JFK Jr. is back on television, everyone is wearing straight-leg jeans, and carrying an analog camera around your neck is no longer seen as an affectation of hipster-dom. It’s 2026, and the perpetually online masses are sighing as they romanticize how great it must have been to be young in 1996, with lives yet to be suffocated by social media. But what so much of this current 1990s nostalgia overlooks is the anger and restlessness of those growing up in that era of seismic change—their cynicism and disdain for capitalist avarice and their inviolable distrust of corporations, just before everything was corporatized. To the then-teenage Gen Xers who were becoming adults in the 1990s, “selling out”—now a shameless imperative—was an unforgivable sin. It was this moral compass that, in 1994, led a twenty-two-year-old musician from Montreal named Melissa Auf der Maur to reject the Smashing Pumpkins frontman Billy Corgan’s initial attempt to recruit her to Hole following the death of their bassist Kristen Pfaff. So skeptical was Auf der Maur of success and celebrity that she hesitated for a while aboutjoining the band—abrasively antiestablishment but signed to a major label—until Courtney Love (affectionately) bullied her into acquiescence. A photography student at Concordia University, Auf der Maur had taught herself how to use a camera by photographing herself nude in her bedroom from different angles. At most, she thought that she would end up a working photographer with some involvement in music. “That’s sort of what I assumed my job would be in life, because it seemed more realistic than a musician,” she told me. Melissa Auf der Maur, Self-portrait on Stage with the Smashing Pumpkins, Cape Town, South Africa, 2000 Melissa Auf der Maur, Self-portrait with Hole, Lollapalooza, 1995 Despite her modest ambitions, she went on to spend nearly a decade traveling and performing on stages across the world as the bass player for Hole and the Smashing Pumpkins, two of the biggest alternative rock bands of the 1990s. Determined to continue her photography, she became a relentless documentarian of her life, relying on a 28mm point-and-shoot Nikon and the occasional one-hour photo lab whenever she could find one in between tour stops. During that period, she took nearly ten thousand photographs, most of which have languished in her attic in upstate New York—until now. Shop Now This September, the exhibition Melissa Auf der Maur: My ’90s Rock Photographs opened at the Art Gallery of Ontario (AGO), where two hundred photographs from her personal archive will be on display. The show comes on the heels of Auf der Maur’s memoir, Even the Good Girls Will Cry: A ’90s Rock Memoir, published this past March. In 2020, when Auf der Maur began working on her memoir, she revisited the photographs for the first time and was struck by how she’d managed to capture an almost inconceivable dissonance in time and culture, back when the massive public fame that rock musicians experienced hadn’t entirely been subsumed by corporate machinery and the demands of the platform economy. “Even though from the outside my generation looked like disaster and self-destruction, on the inside it was so delicate and human,” she said. “These were armies of misunderstood people taken hostage by a corporate, digitizing, greedy world.” Youth culture was undergoing a “brutal loss of innocence,” she added. “It was death, death, death, plus fame, fame, fame. I was shocked by their shock.” So much 1990s nostalgia overlooks the anger and restlessness of those growing up in an era of seismic change. In tour buses, hotel rooms, dressing rooms, sets, stadiums, and arenas—where thousands of people convulsed around her electric presence onstage—Auf der Maur photographed her life as a musician and the people in it with a raw spontaneity and irreverence. In a hazy self-portrait, she curls her lip in a mock come-hither expression, ginger ringlets cascading around her face, as she’s playfully jostled by Love, a tender moment between two girls—except that they happen to be members of one of the most successful bands in the world. Behind her, an arm attends to a drooping frond, which we now know will be set on fire, since Auf der Maur snapped this selfie avant la lettre on the set of the photoshoot for the cover of Hole’s album Celebrity Skin (1998), which features the band posed against a conflagrant tree. The infamous blazing billboard and palm trees of Hole’s “Malibu” music video also make an appearance in an off-hand behind-the-scenes photograph of the band on set (the fires were all real). Melissa Auf der Maur, Burning Billboard from “Malibu” music video set, 1998 Melissa Auf der Maur, Courtney on Tarmac, 1999 In a photograph dated 1999, a disheveled Love lounges on a tarmac in an anonymous location while a plane idles in the background. Tinted a toxic green, the picture seems to have been taken at some ungodly hour—could be midnight, could be morning. Such is life for musicians on the road. Auf der Maur captures her bandmate in a rare moment of vulnerability, the belligerence of her onstage (and offstage) persona set aside in favor of what Sophie Hackett, AGO’s curator of photography, calls Love’s “playfulness, quietude, and tenderness.” Here, Love looks a little drowsy: cigarette in hand, headphones on, flip-flopped, rummaging in her handbag, exuding an air of unguarded exhaustion. Advertisement Advertisement Sometimes painterly, sometimes full of mischief, Auf der Maur’s photographs treat both the musicians and their fans—and the relationship between them—with a deep compassion, revealing the human and fallible faces of what was a generation-defining phenomenon. “Because the corporate greed of the ’90s music scene was so fast rising, I was also capturing the magic while it still lasted,” she said. To those of us who have only ever known fandom dictated by algorithms, overpriced concert tickets as Amex exclusives, and self-portraits referred to as “selfies,” the photographs are proof that there was, in fact, a brief moment in time when you could be famous but still have the freedom to revel in all your ugliness, both internal and external; when your imperfections, filth, and mess were not meant to be swept away but were proof of a full life while it was being lived. But that was 1996. Thirty years later, in Auf der Maur’s own words, “Our world is on fire, and our minds are melting.” This article originally appeared in Aperture No. 264, “Stop Time,” under the column Redux.

### British Journal of Photography (1854)

**Thinking through technology**

Requiem for an Exit, 2025 © Kvam Oldereid A new exhibition and the THEA space in Groningen, Netherlands, use art to think through technology and human experience, explains curator and director Roosje Klap Last year the Nooderlicht Biennale was themed Machine Entanglements and, including exhibitions by 75 artists across the northern Dutch city of Groningen, explored “how we now live, eat, sleep, dream and even date through technology,” says Roosje Klap, director of Nooderlicht, the platform for photography and lens-based media. The shows in one venue, THEA, focused on extraction – the “ugly monster” which makes technology, as Klap puts it – and, working in the building, she was called to reception one day. “There was this guy, maybe about 55, and he was very emotional because he had seen all this for the first time,” she explains. “He had known about it, but this was the first time he had really seen it. That was very important for me, very touching, but then he asked, ‘Is there any hope? Because I don’t see any hope, only sadness and terrible feelings’. And that’s when I started to wonder, what’s the way out? There must be a way out, how can we escape, or what’s the antidote?” That insight led to the latest exhibition at THEA, Thinking Hands, which explores how embodied knowledge endures in our ever-more technological age. --> Curated by Klap with Musoke Nalwoga, Thinking Hands includes both still and moving work, and finds quirky, thought-provoking twists on the theme. There’s Paul Kooiker’s series Talking Hands, for example, a playful look at body language, and there’s Martina Bacigalupo’s found series Gulu Real Art Studio – a series of studio portraits, in which the heads had been clipped out of the image to make passport photos. Removing the face as a point of focus, Gulu Real Art Studio throws the attention on the sitters’ hands and postures, and what they express. Andi Gáldi Vinkó’s Sorry I Gave Birth I Disappeared But Now I’m Back explores the deeply embodied experience of early motherhood, a time in which the baby is technically separate but is often ‘joined at the hip’; Martha Rosler’s Semiotics of the Kitchen, from the generation before Gáldi Vinkó, rethinks cookery skills as enforced, internalised physical socialisation. Laure Prouvost’s Taking Care (Love letter to a fellow art work) depicts the artist as art handler, talking to a viewer as if he or she is a painting or object; Requiem for an Exit by Kvam/Oldereid shows an eerie armless robot, whose disembodied projected face and AI-generated voice speak of genocide and human displacement. “All the thinking that is somehow stored within the body, all the knowledge that isn’t necessarily in the head, that’s what makes us human. Roosje Klap From Talking Hands, 2025 © Paul Kooiker Taking Care, Love letter to fellow art work, 2019 (video still) © Laure Prouvost Taking Care, Love letter to fellow art work, 2019 (video still) © Laure Prouvost --> Klap takes me on a virtual tour, walking me through the space via her laptop; we’re 300 miles apart but she’s warm and funny, cracking jokes through cyberspace. “All the thinking that is somehow stored within the body, all the knowledge that isn’t necessarily in the head, that’s what makes us human,” she smiles, turning the laptop back to herself and reflecting on how instinctively we now use technology. And photography, of course, is part of that process. “The apparatus is part of the body, and the decision to make that photo – yes or no – is also an embodied decision,” Klap muses. “I think it is really a mind/body-correlative decision, and I feel that’s very much at the basis of what’s at stake now [as technology expands], because we are taking ourselves out of that whole system.” And her lively engagement with technology is important because, as well as being director of Nooderlicht, Klap is now Cultural Development Lead at Niemeyer. The Niemeyer is a former tobacco factory now being developed into a public culture and tech hub and THEA has some 8000 square metres of space in it, spread over three floors; THEA is backed by the Mondriaan Fund and support from the City of Groningen, and its mission is to explore the impact of technology via art and culture. The project is a two-year startup so far but, says Klap, she’s “here to stay”, and she is co-developing THEA with Rosa Wevers, an independent curator and senior researcher in Art &amp; Technology at Hanze University. 5000 times, 2020 © Kiki Mager THEA is also developing alongside the other initiatives at the Niemeyer, which include projects such as the AI Factory; part of an EU-backed network of projects using AI to help research healthcare and agriculture, the AI Factory aims to rethink the technology with privacy, security, and digital sovereignty in mind. “The AI Factories are being put into place as alternatives to big tech, so they are interesting and important,” says Klap. “But that’s where THEA is important too. That work is all happening in computers; we can help make it more visible. We can also be a critical little sister to Big Brother and the tech bros. THEA and the tech companies [which are moving into Niemeyer] are an integral part of each other.” In fact THEA is named after a rebellious female figure; Theodora Niemeyer was the daughter of tobacco company founder Theodorous Niemeyer but, rather than pursuing the bourgeois life into which she was born in 1912, went to the Hague and became a painter. She used her inheritance to create an art fund. “I wanted to honour her, and so the programme is divided into three areas,” says Klap. “It’s a combination of making, doing and talking about art, so there’s art and technology; care and technology, which includes symposia and critical thinking; and make and technology, which for example includes hackerspace.” Roosje Klap at the Niemyer, November 2025 © Casper Maas At the opening of Thinking Hands, THEA, 13 September 2026 THEA will be a key venue for the Noorderlicht Biennale next summer, which will be themed PLAY; the theme suggests latitude to explore computer games and gaming culture, and while Klap – who will be artistic lead – is keen to embrace these forms, she’s also thinking less literally. She wants to include Debi Cornwall’s Necessary Fictions / Model Citizens, for example, which explore Hollywood film tropes and their impact on people’s perceptions, the stereotypes which inform our reactions without us being consciously aware of them. As before the biennale will be spread over six or seven locations across Groningen, both the city and the wider province; Klap was born in Amsterdam and studied in the Gerrit Rietveld Academie, but moved to Groningen two years ago and says it’s a traditionally radical city. “Groningen is known as the red heart of the Netherlands,” says Klap, who I interviewed shortly before far right clashes in The Hague. “Something I find really great here is that I don’t have to explain to politicians that culture is important for society.” Thinking Hands is on show at THEA, Groningen until 01 November noorderlicht.com/en/whats-on/thinking-hands The post Thinking through technology appeared first on 1854 Photography.
