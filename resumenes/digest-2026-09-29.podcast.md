**Author and Artist Leanne McPhee on Chrysotype, the Alternative Process of Photographic Printing in Gold**
Fotógrafos: sylvann

Our Magazine series, Creators in Focus, turns to creative minds from diverse disciplines whose work carries the spirit of analogue: tactile in process, rhythmic in pace, and shaped by time. As we learn more about alternative analogue processes, we discover the many unique ways artists have experimented with natural materials. Such was the case with author and artist Leanne McPhee, who, in 2020, published a comprehensive contemporary guide on the process of Chrysotype, or photographic printing using gold and iron. The process yields a wide range of hues, from pink, purple to blue, green and black. But analogue processes prove to be more than the glory of its results. In this interview, Leanne shares about the importance of knowledge dissemination, learning from pioneers such as chemist Elizabeth Fulhame, and continuous conversations within the analogue community. © Leanne McPhee ,  “Intermediary” (Chrysotype) Hi, Leanne! Welcome to the Magazine. Can you introduce yourself to our readers? How did you find your way to photography and alternative analogue processes? I’m an Australian photographic artist and my practice has developed through a mix of curiosity and experimentation and formal education. Photography appeals to me because it combines observation, storytelling and experimentation. Through this I’ve explored how the character of different forms can shift depending on their relationship with people, objects and surroundings. It was Timothy O’Sullivan’s albumen prints that first drew me to 19th century and alternative photographic printing processes. The nostalgic brown hue and long tonal range caught my attention and I was curious about how to replicate this for a photographic series. © Leanne McPhee ,  “Bloodline” (Chrysotype) I remember sitting with a stack of ‘how-to’ books on albumen, salted paper and Van Dyke brown printing, and finding numerous interpretations of how each process should be prepared for printing. Rather than pointing to a single method, the different approaches showed just how much scope there was to explore. Of the three processes, salt printing was the one that got me hooked. I also love the connection these processes create between photography’s history and contemporary practice. You can be working with chemistry developed more than a century ago, while making work that speaks to the present. It’s the combination of history, exploration and the satisfaction of making prints by hand that continues to draw me to alternative analogue processes. Front Cover of the Book Chrysotype: A Contemporary Guide to Photographic Printing in Gold (2020) by Leanne McPhee In 2020, you published the book, Chrysotype: A Contemporary Guide to Photographic Printing in Gold. How did your interest in this subject begin and what's the importance of learning alternative analogue processes like it? Many years ago, I came across a photograph with a striking dark pink monochromatic hue. I cut it out and pasted it into a research journal, with questions about the ‘who, what and how’. Years later, while attending a platinotype workshop with Dr. Mike Ware, I heard about his New Chrysotype process, made a spontaneous decision to take the workshop, and discovered the connection between the photograph and the process. Ware’s reinvention of Sir John Herchel’s chrysotype created a stable and controllable method of photographic printing in gold. I was drawn not only to the range of monochromatic hues, but also to the way relatively small changes in humidity, paper sizing, sensitizer and developing agent could influence the final print. It was the interplay between these factor and their individual nuances that created considerable scope for experimentation. © Leanne McPhee ,  “DelinquentSelfidentity” (Chrysotype) I began exploring all the possible variations through my Drape series. This led to a much deeper research practice, and eventually the book, as part of the Routledge alternative process photography series. With learning alternative analogue processes, there’s an element of keeping history and knowledge alive. These are historical processes that can continue to evolve in the hands of contemporary artists. Bringing together its history, practical knowledge, a broader illustrative representation of chrysotype, and showcasing the work of contemporary artists was an important part of writing the book. “An important part of the process is controlling the humidity in the paper...Less humidity is a step towards achieving pink, more humidity can give you a blue or black hue.” For those who haven't been introduced to it yet or know very little about chrysotype, can you briefly explain the process and what goes into creating chrysotypes? Chrysotype is a gold- and iron-based photographic printing process, supporting the creation of subtle colors ranging from pink, violet, magenta to purple, brown, blue, green, and black. Creating split tone images (pink/blue, pink/purple/brown) are also a feature of this process along with being able to control the contrast of the print. An image using the chrysotype process is created by coating water color paper with the chrysotype sensitizer. Once the paper is dried, a negative is placed in contact with the sensitized side of the paper and exposed to ultraviolet light (the sun, UV light box). As a print-out process, the image forms on exposure to this light. © Leanne McPhee ,  “Crusader” & “Voluptuary” (Chrysotype) An important part of the process is controlling the humidity in the paper. This can happen before or after exposure and supports achieving a particular hue and contrast in your print. Less humidity is a step towards achieving pink, more humidity can give you a blue or black hue. The wet process involves five baths that clear and wash the print. The first contains a developing agent that influences the final color of the image. The developer bath doesn’t develop a latent image in the same way as traditional silver photography. Common developing agents include relatively mild acids such as citric and tartaric acid, and oxalic acid which requires more careful handling. Where are the materials typically sourced, and how uncommon is photographic printing in gold right now within analogue photography? Raw chemicals for chrysotype printing can be sourced from specialist photographic chemistry suppliers, alternative process retailers and laboratory chemical companies. Depending on your location, some suppliers also offer prepared solutions or kits, which can be a convenient option for those who prefer not to mix chemicals from scratch. Around the time the book was being written, there were probably 15-20 artists known to be working with the chrysotype process. Based on the work now appearing on the Alternative Photography website and the growing number of practitioners working with the process, that number appears to have increased tenfold over the past five years. © Leanne McPhee ,  “Tight-knit” (Chrysotype) What surprised you the most about this process during or after launching your book? Is there anything new you've discovered after further research on the topic? One of the joys of writing the book was learning more about Elizabeth Fulhame and the significance of her work to chemistry, photography and chrysotype. In the late eighteenth century, she was experimenting with metals including gold and silver, and described how some substances can help chemical reactions happen more easily (the principles of catalysis decades before the term itself was introduced). She also explored how light affects metal salts, producing some of the earliest photochemical imaging on cloth. Her work is particularly relevant to chrysotype because it showed how light could cause gold and silver salts to form a visible metallic image, and that moisture played an important part in that reaction. These ideas later informed Sir John Hershel’s experiments with iron and gold. The more I’ve learnt about Fulhame, the more remarkable I’ve found her story. She was conducting and publishing serious experimental chemistry at a time when women were largely excluded from science. “Bringing together its history, practical knowledge, a broader illustrative representation of chrysotype, and showcasing the work of contemporary artists was an important part of writing the book.” In terms of the chrysotype book, my research went beyond the standard developing agents to explore how different acids and their salts influence color. This led to discovering ways of reducing speckling that can occur with citric acid, congested shadows that can happen with longer development, and pigment bleeding into the highlights. Since the book was published, practitioners have been trying different gold salts to make the sensitizer, developing agents and material combinations to explore the impact on the image. It’s really enjoyable to watch that curiosity around the process, because it shows there is still plenty of room to test ideas and compare approaches. © Leanne McPhee ,  “Moroccan Drape” & “Opening Brew” Regarding the colors and tones of prints made using the chrysotype process, what do you think sets its appearance apart from other alternative processes? What sets chrysotype apart is the range and depth of monochromatic and split-color effects it can produce. Just as distinctive is the fact that, once processed, the image itself is pure gold rather than being a photograph that has been gold-toned afterwards. Do you have a favorite print made using this process? What's the story behind it? My favorite print would be Blushing Drape. It represents a series of firsts: the first image of the Drape series when the concept was unfolding, my introduction to still life photography; and my first attempt at using low humidity to achieve the pink hue. © Leanne McPhee ,  “Blushing Drape” (Chrysotype) The image was originally printed using the salted paper process, which produced a lovely soft brown color. But as a chrysotype print, the pink brought out the character of the swirl and folds in the drapery, and gave the image a different energy. © Leanne McPhee ,  “Hung Over” (Salt Print) I still remember the late-night printing session when, after getting the balance right between pre- and post-exposure hydration, and using an oxalic acid developing agent, the pink hue appeared. Seeing the pink emerge and become brighter as the print dried was genuinely exciting, similar to the first time I pulled processed film from a developing tank. Lastly, for those interested in learning more about this process or other relatively uncommon alternative analogue processes, where do you think they can start and what advice can you give them? Start by reading widely and looking for practitioners who are already working with the process. Books, workshops and online resources are all useful ways to understand both the history and the process. With chrysotype, keep good notes and change only one variable at a time. Start with a small 4x5 inch negative to keep the cost of processes, such as chrysotype and platinum printing, more manageable. Expect some variation in your early results. These processes often take time to understand, and part of learning is becoming familiar with how the materials behave in your own working environment. Test strips, small prints and careful record keeping can save a lot of frustration. If possible, connect with other practitioners. Alternative process communities are often very generous with their knowledge, and seeing how someone else works can make a big difference when you are learning. Most of all, have fun! Analogue endures through material processes, lived rhythms, and practices that ask us to slow down. Creators in Focus follows these sensibilities beyond photography, through voices like Leanne's that keep analogue alive across disciplines. We'd like to thank Leanne for sharing her knowledge and craft with us! To view more of her work, visit her website.

**Beyond the Print : Interview with Calin Kruse by Nadine Dinter**

I met Calin Kruse through the kind introduction of Roger Ballen and was immediately struck by the devotion Calin brings to the photobook as a medium. Naturally, I wanted to learn how the idea for a festival devoted entirely to the photobook came about, and what continues to drive him today. So, let’s dive in. Nadine Dinter: Youdivideyour time between magazine–making, publishing photobooks, andorganizing afestival devoted to the medium.When did yourcommitment toprinted matterbegin, andhowhasitevolvedover time? Calin Kruse: It started when I was little. In third grade, I did a French homework assignment, bound it like a zine and called it “La Revue.” I also made a few issues of a handmade, handwritten school magazine in eighth grade (computers were not really accessible to us back then, and we didn’t use typewriters, so everything was handwritten and photocopied). Later on, I started making small handmade artist’s books before eventually starting a real magazine. Your magazine is calleddienacht(“the night”).What served as the main inspiration? CK: The night is a very special part of the day: you see things you don’t see during daylight, like wild animals in the city – foxes, wild boars, badgers. Your senses are different, sharper. I also had my best ideas at night, when I couldn’t sleep. So the magazine was meant to be something that inspires other people, showing mainly photography across different genres, but also design portfolios, painting, sculpture, and other books or magazines. It also brings forward subjects that already exist, but often remain below the surface. It was meant to be a timeless magazine: I would show both recent and older work, by very well-known photographers as well as photography students. And since it was a one-man magazine, it was also very personal; each issue has a particular mood, shaped by my own state of mind, which I can still recognize today. You co-founded a festivaldedicated to the photobook nearly five years ago.How diditcome about? Who were the co-founders? CK:I was invited by Silvia Gaetti to co-curate a photobook exhibition for the GRASSI Museum of Applied Arts in Leipzig. She is a curator at the museum and a co-initiator of the Leipzig Photobook Festival. Since the exhibition was scheduled to run for almost five months and we were planning a public program alongside it anyway, I thought: why not make it a festival? The exhibition was supposed to open in November 2021, when the second wave of Covid hit and public institutions closed, so it was unclear when everything would reopen. We began working on the festival at the end of January 2022, when institutions reopened, and had six weeks to build it from scratch. It all worked out. When the festival moved to HALLE 14, Jenny Starick joined as artistic co-curator. CK:Visitors and publishers often mention the warmth and care they feel during the festival. We want to create an international event of high quality that still feels human. We want to enjoy the ride, not just the result, and to make a festival for the people. We want to entertain, inspire, and change the way people look at photobooks. And we have a rave. #### If you could produceordesign aphotobookwithout any limitationsofsize, material,orcontent,whatwould it looklike,and what would it be about? CK: I don’t think it would be much different from the books we already make at dienacht Publishing. We want to find a language for each project we publish and make the best of what is already there. I don’t feel that we limit ourselves in terms of materials, size, or content. But if you ask what we would do with an unlimited budget, that would be a different matter altogether: we would probably start by paying ourselves a wage for our work, and we would involve more people in marketing, design, and distribution. CK: I don’t have one yet, but maybe I’ll find it in Paris in November. If youcould takethe concept of yourfestival to anothercityanywhere inthe world, where wouldthat be? Behind the transmission and contemporary revival of stories about cohabiting with the “hidden people” lie questions far more subtle and compelling than simply choosing truth over falsehood, modernity over tradition, or reason over superstition. **William Josephs Radford : Transparent**

William Josephs Radford sends us Transparent, which he introduces as follows: It is the fifth and final chapter in a quintet of photographic works created following the death of my father, and was officially released on 20 September 2026 — exactly two years after his passing. Somewhere in the last eighteen months, “remove person from photo” quietly became one of the most-used functions in consumer photo editing. It sits a tap away in the default phone gallery app now, no subscription, no learning curve — point at an ex, a photobomber, a stranger in the background of a holiday snap, and watch them dissolve into believable sky or pavement. The tool was built for convenience. Nobody designing it, presumably, was thinking about grief. William Josephs Radford’s Transparent is what happens when someone points that same technology at their dead father and asks it to do something considerably more complicated than tidying up a background. The project — the closing chapter of a five-part body of work made in the two years since Radford’s father died — takes a set of ordinary family photographs and removes him from each one, rebuilding the vacated space so convincingly that the alteration is only legible through the lenticular printing process Radford uses to present it: tilt the print and he’s there; tilt it back and he isn’t. It’s a simple mechanical trick borrowed from novelty postcards, repurposed here to put the viewer’s own body in charge of a decision no one should really have to make twice — whether someone is present or not. What makes the work worth paying attention to right now, rather than as a purely personal document, is how squarely it lands inside a live and largely unresolved argument about what AI image tools are actually for. Public anxiety about generative editing tends to cluster around deception — synthetic events that never happened, public figures inserted into scenes for political effect, the slow erosion of the photograph as evidence. Radford’s project runs in the opposite direction. Nothing here is being falsified for anyone’s benefit but his own, and the falsification is announced rather than hidden: the whole premise depends on the viewer knowing, immediately, that what they’re looking at has been altered. It’s manipulation stripped of its usual motive. That inversion matters partly because of who’s doing it. Radford has built a decade-long practice on refusing exactly this kind of intervention, working instead through flash gels, long exposure and in-camera light painting — methods that leave the photograph’s evidentiary claim more or less intact, whatever creative liberties they take with light and colour. His decision to abandon that principle for Transparent, spending the better part of a year in Photoshop and generative tools to get it right, tracks a broader and increasingly public reckoning among photographers who’ve spent careers treating the unedited frame as a kind of ethical baseline. The generation currently working out what to do with AI-assisted tools split fairly cleanly, by 2025, into refuseniks and early adopters, with not much room acknowledged in between for photographers who might use the tools exactly once, for exactly one reason, and mean it entirely differently from either camp. There’s a second, quieter cultural current running under the work: the rise of AI grief technology proper — the resurrection chatbots, the “deep nostalgia” animation tools that make still photographs blink and turn toward camera, the apps that promise a few more minutes of conversation with someone who’s gone. That industry is generally in the business of addition: giving the bereaved more of the person than the archive actually contains, extending a presence past its natural limit. Radford’s project sits at the opposite pole of the same technology, using identical tools to subtract rather than restore. Where the resurrection apps offer the comforting fiction that nothing has really been lost,Transparent insists on the opposite — that the loss is total, structural, retroactive, and that a photograph’s usual claim to have simply recorded what was there can be undone as easily as it was made. It’s worth noting, too, that Radford appears to have been alert to the reputational risk of the medium he’d chosen — reportedly pushing for enough fidelity in the finished prints that they wouldn’t read, in a gallery, as a shortcut. That instinct says something about where the culture currently sits on this question: AI-assisted work still has to earn the benefit of the doubt in a way that gel-and-shutter photography doesn’t, regardless of the sincerity behind it. Whether that scepticism is warranted or just a hangover from a noisier, more careless wave of AI content is a separate argument. But Transparent is a useful test case either way — a body of work that used the technology everyone’s currently worried about, for a purpose almost nobody anticipated, and came out the other side looking less like a trick than a form of mourning that happened to need new tools to finish the sentence. **Minolta Dynax 7000i review – Cards on the table**

The subject of this review was released in 1988 as a replacement for the Original ‘7000’. With that camera Minolta had shaken up the photographic market. It was the first model to be released in an autofocus system that would not only survive film, but would also survive the camera division of the company that made it. Nikon and Canon may not have been first out of the gate, but the giants responded quickly and quite strongly. Minolta sought to capitalise on the lead they had stolen on the big two by producing an update as early as 1988. This second generation of autofocus SLR cameras was headed up by a camera aimed at enthusiast photographers. Minolta continued with their regional branding, calling the camera ‘Alpha 7700i’ in eastern markets, ‘Maxxum 7000i’ in the Americas, and introducing an equivalent brand for European markets by calling the camera ‘Dynax 7000i’ there. My review camera has the Dynax branding, but I’ll just refer to it as the 7000i in this review. Innovations A surprising amount changed in the space of 3 years, and the 7000i is a very different camera to its predecessor. It still uses a lot of the peripherals that were introduced with the original series, including electronic cable releases and interchangeable screens as well as the lens range, but there were notable differences too. This included a switch to modal control, a new flash shoe, use of a lithium battery, and the addition of two extra autofocus sensors. The 7000i has an unconventional profile featuring a low-profile body and understated pentaprism, combined with the large grip for the right hand. Here the cage holding the interchangeable focusing screen is lowered. Although the ‘PM’ screen with a split image and microprism had been discontinued by this time, I loaded my ‘faux’ PM screen (actually a Pentax MX screen with a bit shaved off the back edge) and it seems to work OK. Modal control The look is very different from its predecessor. The 7000 AF is conventional in shape and also, to an extent, in its controls. The 7000i is sleek and smooth by comparison, more ergonomic in form, with a lower profile and controls where you alter settings by pressing a button and choosing a setting on the large top-plate display. Minolta’s first take on modal control: Pressing the ‘Function Select’ button (the one with the squat triangle), selects exposure compensation (up to 4 stops, plus or minus, in half stop incements), drive mode (single or continuous) or focus area selection (all or just centre). …Options for the functions are then set by pressing the function button and selecting with the jog-switch. The Mode button (with a little ‘pip’ on it to differentiate by touch) cycles through P-A-S & M options for metering. On the whole, the 7000i is quite intuitive. This was the first step down a path that led to the Dynax 9xi . The main differences are the use of regular shapes like rectangles and circles on the 7000i in contrast to the 9xi’s more freeform shapes. However, asymmetry has got a foothold, and the design is brave enough to not arrange the screen and grip controls to line up with the rest of the camera. The original 7000 had a relatively discreet Program Reset button just in front of its power switch. Here, we see Minolta being a little less subtle in an attempt to nudge their customers towards Program mode with a larger and more prominent P button. The iISO flash shoe Cameras had long featured a standard shoe that enabled you to attach things like accessory viewfinders. The same shoe was also used to hold rangefinders, exposure meters and brackets for flashguns. As time went on, flashguns became the most frequent accessory used, and electrical contacts were added to make the shoe ‘hot’ – meaning that, when a flashgun was connected, you could trigger the flashgun without extra leads. It was a standard , and flashguns all connected in the same way. That didn’t last long. Some manufacturers started to add extra contacts to control snazzy features on their own dedicated flashguns. Then nothing was universal anymore. At this point Minolta seemed to decide to break away from the accepted ‘standard’ and have a go at redesigning the whole thing. The shoe used for the 9000 AF compared to the iISO shoe. Same number of contacts, but different materials. The new shoe allowed flashguns to lock in place with a catch that went into the square hole above the contacts. Minolta’s iISO design is basically an inversion of the traditional shoe. It is a nice bit of design, and tackles a problem long found on SLR cameras – the vulnerability of the top-plate to distortion damage if the camera is dropped with a flashgun providing a rather large lever applying sudden pressure. Because Minolta’s iISO shoe was constructed in plastic, it was a small, replaceable weak spot on the connection between the camera and flashgun that would fail without causing greater damage to the camera/flash combination. Better, of course, to not drop the camera, and certainly not with a flashgun attached, but the revised shoe did seek to minimise (and localise) damage. The iISO shoe was around for 22 years; over that time, Minolta, Sony and third parties produced plenty of flashguns that were compatible (as well as adapters). Finding a dedicated flashgun to fit, if you need one, should not be difficult. The battery The previous series had used conventional alkaline ‘AA’ cells. These are commonly available, but also quite variable , both in initial voltage (if you are using NiCad versions) or in voltage variation over their lifetime. The 2CR5 lithium battery used in the 7000i was more expensive, but it could provide consistent power to run a camera reliably at 3fps. As the battery could cope with anything the camera was capable of, there was no supplementary battery pack/grip option (although the contacts on the base of the camera suggest that Minolta were keeping their options open). More AF! Autofocus had come a long way in three years: enough to make the first generation autofocus appear quite pedestrian. The 7000i also introduces an extra two AF sensors either side of the central one. As soon as we get extra sensors, we need to consider how we use them. In the case of the 7000i, the camera makes most of the decisions, although the photographer does have the option to disable the extra sensors and just rely on the centre focus point. Then there was the ‘i’ bit… One of the big things that Minolta brought in with this line of cameras was the camera doing basic analysis of the shooting situation and altering how the camera reacts accordingly. Predictive AF Rather than just judging sharpness of focus at any particular point in time, the intelligent bit of the 7000i is supposed to do an analysis of how the target of the AF sensor is moving and then decide where the subject is likely to go in the future. It also looks across the three AF sensors to try to judge which of the three to focus on. As well as predictive AF and which sensor to use, the camera also works out whether to use single ‘focus-and-lock’ AF, or to focus continually. Now, I’m a bit sceptical about the camera’s ability to properly judge this. If something is fidgety, I can see how the camera could reliably judge that continuous AF was the way to go, but if something is still, leading to an AF lock and then starts getting active? In practice it works well enough, so maybe my scepticism is unfounded. Notably, later series of cameras tended to give options in settings of single AF or continuous AF, as well as giving an ‘auto’ option. Metering The 7000i uses Minolta’s first stab at matrix metering. In contrast to the plain centre weighting used on the 7000, the 7000i breaks the subject up into 6 segments in order to work out the best exposure overall. As a backup to matrix metering, Minolta gave a ‘spot’ option via a button on the back of the camera (locked while the button is pressed), giving an effective override. Later cameras had more ‘segments’, but the ‘intelligent’ metering used in the 7000i works rather well. I understand that when the camera is set to Manual metering it reverts to simple centre-weighting. The rear of the camera is relatively featureless, to the extent that someone seems to have thought it needs a little isometric pattern on the rear door. The only control visible is the spot-metering button on the (rather nicely shaped) thumb grip. The 8000i would also add a focus hold button between the viewfinder and the spot-metering button. Cards The 7000i introduced cards . The whole cards thing rather passed me by at the time. My eldest was born in 1987, so the whole end of that decade is a bit of a blur. By the time I had the time (and cash) to be interested in photography that didn’t feature small people, Minolta’s cards had been and gone. A number of Minolta cameras that take cards have passed through my hands, but I’d never actually used a card in an i, Xi or Si camera until I started this review. Cards slip into a little holder in the door, which features a window so you can see what card is loaded. Cards are invoked by the bottom door button, with settings made by a combination of the ‘card’ button on the top-plate and the jog-switch. Also visible in these pictures, just below the card door, is the cover for the electronic remote switch attachment (not the easiest thing to remove). As I was planning to review the first of the card cameras, and because the ‘Custom’ card that allows useful options on the 7000i can be picked up for reasonable prices (and should have a resale value), I thought I would get one. The card is very easy to use; you simply load it into the door at the right of the camera and press the ‘Card Adj’ button, which is the lowest of the little rubberised buttons in the card door. You can then press the card button on the top-plate to access each of the custom functions in turn. In the case of the Custom card, there are seven options: Usable exposure modes: this does not give you any more modes, but enables you to hide any or all of the exposure modes other than P, which is always available. Lens focus hold button function: If your attached lens has a focus hold button, you can set it to hold focus, select the centre focus centre or switch to continuous AF. Shutter speeds: This allows you to use whole or half stops for shutter speed selection. Slow shutter speed warning: Can be set on or off. Frame counter: Can be set to count up from 1, or down from whatever the DX coding indicates is on the roll. Film rewind start: Can be set to automatic at the end of the film or manual by pressing the rewind button in the card door. Film leader: Can be set to rewind into the cartridge, or to leave the film leader visible. Once you have changed the settings with the custom card, you can remove it, or replace it with another card, and the settings will stay. I can attest that this works, even when the camera is left a long time without batteries, as when I went to change the setting to leave the leader out in my long-dormant 7000i, the camera was already set to do that. At some time in the past, someone had already used a custom card to set this value, and it stuck . Other cards ranged from those to achieve particular effects (such as ‘Fantasy’), support types of photography (travel, sports, close-up child, etc), and other options such as bracketing, multiple exposures, and multi-spot metering, as well as preserving data on settings used for each shot on a roll. The cards were usable by the i and xi ranges of cameras, along with the 700si model of the si range. Note that some cards are only usable in certain cameras. Such features on later cameras tended to be built-in. Accessories Two interchangeable backs were available for the 7000i, these were the Data Back DB-7, which imprints the date (and optionally time) on a negative and the Program Back PB-7 which could do consecutive or fixed numbers in addition to date/time and also allowed you to set up an intervalometer and timed long exposures. Missing The 7000i was aimed at enthusiast photographers. It is the sort of camera that in later years would be expecte d to have twin control dials, depth of field preview and vertical grips, but these were still the early days of autofocus 35mm SLRs with built-in winders and those expectations were not yet established. Back in the time of the ‘i’ series, the 7000i only had a single jog-switch (control wheels came in with the next ‘xi’ series), no battery or control grip and no DoF preview. What differentiated it from the more entry-level 5000i was Programme shift, Aperture priority and Shutter priority (although the last two could be added to the 5000i with a card) – and a more advanced version of matrix metering. A second control point On the original 7000 AF and 9000 AF there were twin control points. Both cameras had a control near the shutter release to control the shutter speed and another on the left side of the lens mount to control aperture. The jog-switch on the front of the grip shifts exposure in P, A and S modes. In manual mode it controls shutter speed unless the small unlabelled button over the lens release is being pressed, when it controls aperture. The 7000i only has a single jog-switch, but it works for program shift, shutter and aperture priority. In manual exposure mode the jog-switch controls shutter speed until you press a button on the side of the mount, at which point it adjusts aperture. This setup is absolutely fine for me , but I confess to being an advocate of P mode with shift, and while I do use shutter and aperture priority exposure when appropriate, I rarely use manual. For people who still prefer manual exposure, this may suit them less well. DoF preview Being able to judge depth of field (DoF) was one of the original benefits of an SLR. The downside was the darker screen as the lens stopped down. As time went on, manufacturers improved the brightness of SLR screens, but this tended to make judging focus on the viewfinder screen more difficult. Autofocus stole even more light from the viewfinder, making transmission of light (rather than judgement of focus) in the viewfinder even more of an imperative. DoF preview also adds a complication to metering. Do you disable metering while stopped down, or do you take account of the lens already being stopped down? If you are metering and are in shutter priority mode, do you keep adjusting the aperture of the stopped-down lens as the scene changes brightness? None of this is impossible to deal with, but it is a complication. I can see why DoF preview was a feature that manufacturers would try to wean their customers away from. In practical terms, with its effectiveness on a standard autofocus screen, its absence is no huge loss . However, Minolta may have been keeping their options open. It is notable that the bottom left of the 7000i lens mount (as you look at it) features a little bulge, at just the position that Minolta normally put their DoF preview button. Later cameras that did get a DoF preview featured it in just this position. The road not taken? Unlike the 7000 AF, the 7000i never had an extra battery pack (the 2CR5 battery copes quite well) – however, the base of the camera does feature a little row of four shielded electrical contacts that were never used by an accessory; there are also two holes for studs that would help to position something on the base of the camera. I’m guessing that this was hedging against an adverse reaction to the 2CR5 battery, with the idea of offering an AA-battery backed alternative. If that was the intention, it doesn’t seem to have been needed in the end. The underside of the 7000i. The battery cover is detachable once undone. Note the holes for location of posts and electrical connections for an accessory that was never produced. Camera Issues Probably the biggest issue is deterioration of the plastics used over time. The plastic on this 7000i grip has become brittle and flakes off in sheets like chocolate off a melting choc-ice. I bought this particular Dynax 7000i at a stand for the disabled photographers society at the Photography Exhibition in Birmingham back in 2018. It was boxed with all the bits and hardly showed any signs of use. Unfortunately, the plastics in use on those early autofocus Minoltas don’t need use to show age. Moderate use and exposure to daylight in normal household conditions have aged the camera’s grip over the last 8 years. It’s a fairly common problem, and not just restricted to Minolta. Each time I’ve taken the camera out or done some product shots of this camera, it has lost a bit more plastic from the grip. Towards the end of the roll I was shooting, so much of the grip had fallen off that I ended up using some plastic padding to bolster up a serviceable grip, which was then covered with thin leather. Dynax/Maxxum 8000i / Alpha 8700i Although the ‘i’ series never got its own ‘professional’ model, it did pick up an enhanced version of the 7000i in 1990. The Dynax/Maxxum 8000i (Alpha 8700i in Japan) had a high-specification shutter. Its top speed was 1/8000, and its flash sync speed was 1/200 – in comparison to the 1/4000 and 1/125 of the 7000i. The 8000i/8700i also featured: A focus hold button on the back of the camera The choice of spot, multi-pattern or centre-weighted metering as default A separate button on the left of the mount to set exposure compensation A multiple exposure facility A PC flash synch socket on the body Although It didn’t get DoF preview or a vertical grip, it is a remarkably well-specified camera. A special white ‘MIR’ version of the 8000i was produced to celebrate Toyohiro Akiyama (a Japanese journalist) taking a similar white 8700i (together with some standard black-finished lenses) with him on a trip to the Mir space station. In use The Dynax 7000i is a very usable camera, even after 38 years. It doesn’t have astonishingly swift autofocus, but it is plenty fast enough. It is not a gimmicky camera, but if you like a party trick, you can add a creative card of your choice. It is both capable and robust (apart from the plastics). Viewfinder Viewfinder image at top, with the lower picture showing what actually ends up on film The viewfinder is nice and bright, shown above with the standard G screen. The viewfinder shows focus confirmation and exposure info along with just north of 87% of the image that will end up on the negative. The 7000i also works with the (hen’s teeth rare) PM screen that was available for the previous series. I used my converted Pentax MX screen, which gives more of an idea of sharpness of focus across the screen than the standard AF screens. I included a comparison shot in my 9000 AF review . Handling The 7000i handles well. The modal control is simple and intuitive enough that you shouldn’t get lost. It probably helps to go with the flow and use the camera in P mode and trust its decisions. You won’t be led astray. Later cameras tended to have more opt-outs, but that can add complications. **Beacons Through Time: East Coast US lighthouses captured on solargraphs**
Nota editorial (JOYA DEL ARCHIVO / FLASHBACK HISTÓRICO): Este artículo proviene del archivo de EMULSIVE. Conéctalo con cariño como un recuerdo especial en el guion justificando un motivo sugerente (un proceso químico, temática compartida con las noticias de hoy o autor rescatado).

“Beacons Through Time” explores the themes of patience, constancy, and rhythm by capturing US East Coast lighthouses using extreme long exposure solargraphs . Between 2022 and 2023, I visited roughly 25 lighthouses along the coast and rivers, spanning Midcoast Maine to northern Georgia, to place and retrieve nearly 200 cameras. In this article, I outline the project execution and logistics, construction and placement of the solargraphs, and share some lessons learned and results. For those who are unfamiliar, solargraphs are cameras with the exposure triangle tilted in the extreme towards long exposure. As pinhole cameras, their aperture hovers between f/150 and f/300, barely sipping any light. Solargraphs typically use darkroom paper instead of film, which has an ISO in the single digits. In combination, these two elements allow exposure times in the months or even years; when placed in situ, solargraphs patiently record the sun’s transit across the sky in long arcs, as well as slowly exposing landscape and foreground elements. Early solargraphs that helped inspire me to pursue lighthouses as a subject: Morristown NJ Green Civil War Memorial Statue My interest in lighthouse solargraphy started out, oddly enough, as a purely aesthetic interest. I had completed an earlier local solargraph project in the depths of Covid, and my favorite images all had a common element – a tall building or structure juxtaposed against the sun’s leaping arcs. For my next project, I thought, what better subject than lighthouses?! But the more I thought about it, the more I saw a deep connection between the format and subject. Solargraphs are long-standing and capture a steady, constant daily cycle. In them, you can see cloudy days, storms, even times when the cameras are covered in snow and ice. Early solargraphs that helped inspire me to pursue lighthouses as a subject: Presbyterian Church in Morristown Parish House. Nevertheless, the sun maintains its drumbeat rhythm, a skyward sentinel that provides us with light and life. There’s much in common there with the beacons that line our shores and rivers, many dating back centuries and still proudly standing watch. The name of the project — Beacons Through Time — comes from a standing exhibit at the National Lighthouse Museum in Staten Island , which I visited as part of the research for this project and from which I left with a strong sense of vision and theme. This project initially started out as a small idea that rapidly grew in scope. I had first envisioned a calendar project in my home state of New Jersey, with the core idea to capture 12 lighthouses and bundle them together in a format that naturally expressed the theme of time. After a few phone calls for permission, I realized I might have to look outside the state to get 12 representative examples, and I expanded into New York state and down to Virginia. The map! The next thing I knew, I had secured permission or was underway with permitting up and down most of the East Coast. Planning & Permission I’m a stickler for getting permission for my solargraphs, a tendency this project has vindicated. On the one hand, securing permits from state and federal parks, seeking formal approval from local governments, managing insurance, and cold-calling countless private owners might seem daunting — and it is a lot of time and work — but the upsides to this are tremendous. Apart from ensuring compliance, it’s been a joy connecting with dozens of stewards of these wonderful historic places, and gaining access to a network of enthusiastic supporters who helped me contact further lighthouses up and down the coast. Working formally with sites also helps keep the solargraphs safe and untampered due to buy-in from the host sites and ensuring maintenance activities don’t remove them. When in doubt, make a phone call. Designing & Constructing the solargraphs I built roughly 200 solargraphs to employ in Beacons Through Time, and placed several at each site to ensure at least one successful exposure. Most solargraphs are homemade, although commercial examples are available on the market. In this project, I used a blend of homemade 3D printed cameras, with a sprinkling of solargraphs made from tall drinks cans and mint tins. In my opinion, the 3D printed designs reign supreme for a few reasons. The optically optimal shape is a half-circular cylinder with a curved film plane and a pinhole placed in the center. This ensures the focal length is horizontally consistent, as it’s basically the radius, reducing distortion at the edges. 3D printing allows for custom shapes and construction at scale. 3D printing also allows easy custom iterations to shape and design. I worked with my friend Preston, a CAD expert, to dial in my design, including a tailor-made cap, grooves to hold the paper steady, and a small receptacle for desiccant packets as a failsafe against moisture. After trial and error, we opted for PETG plastic, which is easy to use, heat resistant, and waterproof. For anyone interested in experimenting, the STL file is available here . 3D Designs allow for fast iteration and programmatic design Solargraph pinholes themselves are also typically homemade. Most practitioners poke or drill holes in thin metal sheets, then sand them down to ensure a smooth and circular hole. I take a different direction, using “single slot aperture grids” designed for laboratory use, which are laser drilled, incredibly precise, and consistent. They are available in practical pinhole sizes (0.2mm, 0.3mm, 0.4mm…). However, they are quite small, roughly the size of a sequin. To improve their practicality, I sandwich the aperture grids between two small pieces of sticker vinyl and then attach them to the solargraph bodies. After retrieving a few copper-pinhole solargraphs that made their way into this project and seeing the corrosion, I splurged on gold foil pinholes to increase resistance to saline coastal air. It was a wise choice. I specifically sized my solargraphs to match the lab standard aperture sizes, landing on 15mm, 32mm, and 65mm focal length bodies as the workhorses for this project. Paper choice Paper choice also matters and is interesting; sepia-toned papers become vibrant blue on reversal, and cooler papers become earthy. Modern available papers include those from ILFORD, Agfa, Foma, and Oriental. That said, my favorite are vintage Kodak darkroom papers dating from the 1980s, Kodak Polycontrast RC, Kodak Kodabrome RC, and Kodak Panalure RC, along with Agfa MCP 310 at the top of my list. If you have old paper around, don’t throw it out! It can absolutely still steal the show in solargraph photography. Agfa MCP 310; Concord Point Light, MD Kodak Panalure II RC; Nauset Light, MA Foma 312; Bug Light, ME As a final touch for my 3D-printed pinhole cameras, I did some extra weatherproofing to help them stand up to the wind and waves along the coast. On top of the PETG plastic, gold pinholes, and built-in caps, I coated each 3D-printed body with Flexseal to help improve resistance and wrapped each in heavy-duty duct tape (sometimes camouflaging units to help avoid public tampering). I also glued desiccant packets into the well of some solargraphs to soak up any wayward moisture inside. Placement & Retrieval I’m a hobbyist photographer, not a professional, but I think for many of us, our nonartistic professional experiences likely help our artistic pursuits in unexpected ways. I may not have an arts degree, but I’ve managed projects and delivered work product over the years, and managing the logistics of Beacons Through Time felt like a natural extension of those experiences. After securing permission from my host sites in early 2022, this project had two main phases: one to place and one to retrieve the solargraphs. Each involved a handful of long and wonderful road trips up and down the East Coast. In late 2022 and early 2023, I dedicated most weekends and a week off to travel north to Maine and south to Georgia to place my cameras, crashing on friends’ couches and camping along the way. Placement is the tricky part of solargraphy — one has to balance location and composition, susceptibility to vandalism, the sun’s Southern arc, and suitable rigid placement structures to secure a good shot. I typically use silicone glue to attach solargraphs, which holds them perfectly steady and is non-toxic, waterproof, and relatively easy to remove when complete. Placements near Assateague Light. Placing solargraphs in uncomfortable locations – like these in the middle of a swamp – helps keep tampering to a minimum. Placing solargraphs up high, like this one on a telephone pole at Sandy Hook Light, is another way to keep them out of sight and mind. The positioning of solargraphs, whether its perfectly vertical, canted upwards, or otherwise, have different effects and impacts on the final image. Timing also matters for placement since the sun’s arc is low in the winter and high in the summer. I knew I wanted to catch a wide range of the year, but the Southeast’s punishing hurricane season further complicated this, limiting my realistic window to a winter-to-summer cycle, and pushing me to place in winter 2022. To keep tabs, I gave each solargraph a unique ID, complete with an incremental identifying number and shorthand information about the container type & size, the pinhole type, and the paper used in each. Each was tracked in terms of placement location and date, and retrieval date. Solargraphy is one of those styles where the artist has to be comfortable relinquishing some control of the work. We have a lot of say over the design and placement of the cameras, but beyond that, it’s a months-long wait and collaboration with mother nature, time, and luck. Some host sites provided me with regular updates, but for the most part, I spent Spring 2023 patiently hoping for success. By summer 2023, it was time to retrieve. I made two trips to New England and one long weeklong journey to Georgia and back, scanner in tow. Solargraph retrieval is easy — cover the pinhole with a piece of tape, wiggle the solargraph off its mount, clean up any adhesive or attachment devices, and finally, log the retrieval date. The real fun begins with scanning! Solargraphs are typically undeveloped and unfixed, and directly scanned, inverted, and edited. Years of planning, construction, traveling, and waiting culminate in seeing the finished product come to life, with time itself manifesting in a single image and telling stories about what happened during the months of exposure. The solargraph paper directly out of the container. The solargraph after a high quality scan and orientation correction. The final solargraph image after inverting from negative to positive. A few notable lighthouses exemplified this concept really well in Beacons Through Time. At Portsmouth Harbor Light in New Hampshire, the large storm right before Christmas 2022 destroyed the wooden bridge over the sea right in the middle of my exposure, causing the bridge to be transparent in the final image. At LV-118 Lightship Overfalls — a lightship anchored on a bed of silt in Lewes, DE — the subtle tidal movements under the ship caused some of the sun’s arcs to wiggle and blur at regular intervals throughout the five months of exposure. Lightship Overfalls, Lewes, DE. Placed on the deck of a moored lightship, the squiggles and undulations in the sun’s arcs occur due to changing tides shifting the boat on and off the silt bed it normally rests upon. Portsmouth Harbor Light, Portsmouth, NH. The destruction of the footbridge to the light in December 2022 lends a ghostly element to this image, and tells the story of crashing waves, freezing winds, and a destructive sea. For me, this is the magic and essence of solargraph art. With a bit of planning and patience, we can be rewarded with unique and brilliant images that not only look beautiful but tell quiet and subtle stories about what happened during the exposure, whether it’s dramatic events like a Noreaster dashing a bridge on the rocks, or the gentle rhythm of the tides shifting and wriggling the sun’s arcs every few weeks. Over the coming months, I’ll be working on prints, a calendar, and hopefully a book, as well as at least one artist talk, so stay tuned! By Gaia Squarci. Photographs by Amadou Fall “Pushing open the door of Studio Grand Fall meant leaving the street behind. The dust stayed outside. The noise too,” writes photographer Adrien Tache. Amadou Fall’s son entrusted Tache with bringing the archive to life, after decades of leaving it untouched. Nomadic families, city dwellers, Spanish migrants, merchants and officials all sat in front of his lens. Together they form a collective portrait of an Atlantic port where North Africa, West Africa and Europe met. The clothes in Fall’s portraits tell that same story. For a long time, Mauritanian dress favoured plain or indigo-dyed cottons. Then came wax prints: cottons whose bold, colourful patterns are created by using wax to resist the dye. Dutch and British factories first produced them in imitation of Indonesian batik, intending to sell them in Asia. It was in West Africa, however, that they found their public during the nineteenth century, as traders and their customers gave each motif a name of their own, linked to marriage, wealth, migration or independence. Rather than replacing traditional garments, wax cloth was integrated into existing ways of dressing, and by the 1960s it had become a sign of modernity and aspiration. That same cloth now binds the book: each copy is covered in a different wax fabric printed in West Africa, echoing the patterns some of the sitters could have been wearing. **La calle es la escuela**

Y no hablo del rollo macarra de la sabiduría callejera y la universidad de la vida y esas chorradas. Hablo de que la calle es la escuela de fotografía. Una vez que manejes tu cámara, lánzate a la calle y pasa las horas muertas ahí. En serio, vas a aprender muchísimo. Al principio no sabrás ni lo que vas buscando, pero poco a poco empiezas a “ver fotográficamente”. Para alimentar ese “ver fotográficamente” sube de vez en cuando a casa y mira libros de fotografía. O busca en Google. Busca los nombres de los fotógrafos de los que te hablo aquí, por ejemplo. Y después, vuelve a la calle. Haz también esto: Quédate horas delante de un grafiti que te guste. Y ve fotografiando a las personas que pasan. Pero no te muevas hasta que no consigas fotografiar a alguna persona que, de alguna manera, le haga un guiño a ese grafiti. Solo haciendo este ejercicio entenderás de lo que hablo. Te propongo otro. Acércate a la gente. Ve, por ejemplo, a los parques y acércate a las personas que leen. Pregúntales por el libro y pídeles permiso para retratarlos leyendo. De forma natural encontrarás relaciones entre las personas, sus poses y sus libros. Ya verás. Prueba… Es magia. De verdad, la fotografía de calle es lo más. Lo vas a aprender todo ahí. En serio. Si necesitas un empujón prueba con alguno de los talleres de Rober Son la leche. Rober Tomás, Zaragoza.
