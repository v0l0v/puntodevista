Cocionesgita mensual de Thierry Maindrault En las aperturas de exposiciones, debates o conferencias, esta pregunta surge con mucha frecuencia: “¿Qué criterios utilizas para clasificar una fotografía cuando dices o escribes que es correcta, buena o hermosa?” En primer lugar, es importante entender que una clasificación no es por ningún medio un juicio; es una evaluación que es tan objetiva como sea posible basada en las observaciones de hecho. Los criterios se analizan comparando con los estándares de referencia. Entonces, en lo que a mí respecta, el llamado método de tres “B” en Francia (para Bien, Bon, Beau (Bueno, bueno, Beatiful) funciona muy bien para mí, ya que evita muchos prejuicios subjetivos o incluso parciales. El trabajo que se evalúa puede ser “Correcto.”; en algunos casos, será “Bien.”; y, muy excepcionalmente, puede probar ser “Hermosa“Muchas personas están intrigadas por estas tres categorías, tanto en términos de sus definiciones como en los criterios utilizados para determinarlas. Correcto.. Una fotografía es correcta cuando mejor cumple con el propósito para el cual fue tomada. El resultado corresponde en todo respeto al tema deseado. El trabajo es limpio, bien ejecutado, y demuestra una buena maestría técnica. La habilidad del fotógrafo le ha permitido cumplir con las expectativas razonables del espectador. La representación fotográfica, con o sin editar, satisfies curiosidad, proporciona información, enriquece el conocimiento, o captura un momento de error Bien.“Si la foto es buena...”, escribió y cantó Barbara (French gran cantante). No podría haber mejor manera de describir una buena fotografía. La imagen fotográfica ya no es simplemente una descripción visual de un objeto en una situación determinada. La habilidad técnica del fotógrafo presenta un elemento intangible a través de toda la buena integración de componentes de la imagen. La forma en que el autor percibe la sensibilidad y sensualidad de su sujeto, mezclado con sus emociones personales Hermosa. Ese es el sello distintivo de una auténtica obra fotográfica: “... que esta imagen es hermosa...”. La fotografía es como cualquier otro medio de expresión: la belleza es siempre una cuestión de debate para muchas personas. Todo el mundo cree que sus propias interpretaciones son el árbitro final de la belleza. Esto no es el caso; la belleza se define a través del resultado al que se atribuye. Correcto.- un testigo sensible al Bien.—para entrar en el espacio del HermosaDentro de este mundo de belleza surgen dos categorías de creadores. Por un lado, hay el creador que, aparentemente al azar en medio de su cuerpo de trabajo, crea una imagen fotográfica particular a ese estado de notable armonía. Por otro lado, hay el experimentador que caza implacablemente por cada descubrimiento esencial para la cohesión general de lo que ya han construido en su imaginación. La belleza es un estado cualitativo; no es una cuestión de opinión. Por un momento para discutir un atributo a menudo asociado con una fotografía: “Es una bonita fotografía”. “Pretty” es un adjetivo que transmite un delicado sentido de refinamiento, gracia y cualidades de placer que hacen que encontrar una imagen fotográfica agradable en un momento específico. Este término descriptivo tiene la ventaja de ser flexible con el tiempo; está ligado a las tendencias. Los gustos y las preferencias siguen siendo una cuestión de la individualidad profundamente personal. **Experimenting with IR filters**

Hamish’s recent post of the brown, dried grass on Old Hills reminded me (very slightly!) of the surreal false colour effects that can be got with IR converted cameras and it also made me wonder what IR would do to dried-out grass. I already thought I needed more practice at using one filter I have never managed to get satisfactory results with. I have taken plenty of IR photos using a conventional 720nm filter that have produced moody monochrome images, but have found false colour more difficult. So I went out to compare the results with a group of filters. The cameras were full-spectrum conversions (ie the manufacturer’s IR/UV cut filter has been removed and not replaced with anything else, so you need to add the required filter). The filters I decided to test were the ones that give false colour: Kolari IR Chrome; ZB2 “deep blue” filter; Hoya Orange O(G) filter (around 590nm); and a Kolari Hot Mirror IR/UV cut filter (ie visible light). Because the IR Chrome is 67mm and the Deep blue and Orange are 49mm, I decided to reduce changes by taking two cameras, a Sony A7R2 with Tamron 17-28 (great for IR because it has no hot spots, and a rather older Nex 6 with Sony 18-55. Files were processed in ON1, but I used DXO PhotoLab to do the equivalent of channel swapping. The essence of work with such filters is processing to get the results you are looking for, so showing pure, unprocessed images would not be much use. I started with a shot I thought should help me see the differences clearly. The visible light filter shows the colours that were there, though I remember the trunk of the silver birch as standing out a bit more. **Video of the Week: PAMELA directed by Albert Font**

In PAMELA, Albert Font explores the theme of beauty through subtraction. Shot on 16mm film, his latest visual project moves away from the speed and excess of the digital imagination, offering instead an intimate space in which rebirth is intertwined with a renewed sense of self. At the heart of the film is the model Natalie Tischler, whose presence defines the rhythm of the images: slow movements, minimalist gestures, hands resting on her neck and back. The body does not seek an external gaze, but seems to recognise itself. Intimacy is built through small details, in which even the clothes contribute to the scene without overwhelming it. Fashion blends with the figure, accompanying its movements and transformation. Font focuses on the relationship between light, surface and material. The grain of the 16mm film emerges from the shadows, lending the images an almost tangible warmth, whilst the cold, desaturated background keeps the setting in a more detached dimension. Two distinct elements are woven into this composition: a greyhound, motionless and statuesque, evoking a classical and controlled elegance, and a red rose submerged in water, the sole bold chromatic accent and a symbol of restrained fragility. Roman Lombardi’s composition for strings follows the same logic. PAMELA does not construct a dramatic narrative, but proceeds through images, gestures and atmospheres. The result is a portrait of the female form that avoids exaggeration and draws its strength from restraint. For Font, becoming is not a spectacular transformation, but something more subtle: a different way of inhabiting one’s own body, and of looking at it.

**A Photographer Turns Up the Color in His Family Portraits**

Luis Manuel Diaz lay on his grandmother’s bed in a small town in rural Mexico, reclining beside a vase brimming with roses. The cobalt-blue room has no windows—instead, its adobe walls, lit by a single exposed lightbulb, are crowded with family portraits, Catholic religious icons, mirrors, and the relics of his grandparents’ lives. Turning his head toward a mirror embedded in a wardrobe, Diaz clicked the shutter release cable on his large-format view camera, catching the slight blur of his movement. The resulting photograph, Untitled (Self-Portrait and the Family Tree) (2024), recently presented in the Upstate Photography Biennial at the Center for Photography at Woodstock (CPW), is an arresting glimpse into the domestic life that Diaz has spent the past decade capturing in his series Canta y No Llores (Sing and Don’t Cry) (2016–present). Luis Manuel Diaz, Untitled (After Party Blues), New York, 2025 Luis Manuel Diaz, Untitled (Face Mask), New York, 2022 Diaz was born in that small town in Michoacán, Mexico, and cites his grandmother’s bedroom as his most important early influence. “I remember being five years old and just going and getting lost in the different modes of photography I saw there,” he explained during an artist talk at CPW. Photographs were also a way for his family to maintain ties across vast distances; images can traverse international borders even when their subjects cannot. For much of Diaz’s childhood, his father was based in the United States working full time in construction while the rest of the family remained in Mexico, meaning that they could only gather together on holidays. “My understanding of my father and how his face looked was heavily tied to these photographs of him,” Diaz said. “I was really interested in the performance that took place when he would make these portraits.” In 2005, when Diaz was nine years old, his family permanently reunited in the United States, moving to the suburbs of Westchester, New York. After a childhood spent swimming in local lagoons and picking fruit from trees, New York’s concrete landscape was jarring. The photographer likes to joke that as he travels north after visiting his grandparents in Mexico to his parents’ house, the color drains out of the world. Michoacán is filled with lush hues and patterns, both in nature and in its homes; the Northeastern United States, by contrast, trends pretty greige. Diaz’s photographs make the infamous sepia-tinted “Mexico filter” that US media tends to slap on to anything south of the border seem all the more comical in comparison—the imperialist expansion of the American monochrome revealed for what it is. Luis Manuel Diaz, Untitled (First Communion), New York, 2025 Luis Manuel Diaz, Untitled (Hidden Mother), New York, 2023 Not only does Diaz have an eye for color, he also takes the drama and beauty of Catholic religious painting and applies it to everyday life. If one were to describe the subject of Untitled (Hidden Mother) (2023) literally, for example, it would be banal: A mother holds up a blanket behind her daughters in a suburban New York home. Diaz’s lens, however, imbues it with cosmic significance. The unseen mother—only hinted at near the edges of the frame—seems to protect her older daughter, who lies asleep, angelic, framed by the rich red flowers of the blanket and the glow of white around her body. Likewise, Untitled (First Communion), a 2025 portrait of Diaz’s youngest sister, Stephanie, captures her sitting on the floor, shoes off, drinking Capri-Sun after her first communion. But with the gentle light on her skin, the translucent white ruffles of her dress, and her veil draped over the edge of the sofa, she seems positively lifted from a Renaissance painting. “Growing up Catholic, I was really drawn to the religious narrative tableaux that I would see everywhere,” Diaz explained. “Particularly the emphasis they put on the body—either the body in pain, or just living life.” Advertisement Advertisement More than anything, it seems, Diaz is enchanted by the body at rest. Not that he does much resting himself. After seeing his parents labor for so many years doing construction and housekeeping and the toll it took on their bodies—in 2017, his father was almost buried alive on a job site and suffered severe injuries—Diaz knows better than anyone what’s at stake in his practice. He has honored his parents’ sacrifice by working nonstop. Since getting his BFA from Parsons School of Design (2019) and MFA from Yale University (2025), he has built a portfolio that a mid-career professional—let alone a recent graduate—would be proud of. “Weirdly enough, what provides a sense of rest for me is making images, even though it’s so laborious,” Diaz said. “Through the ground glass, I get to witness my youngest sister becoming more self-aware, my mom or dad getting new gray hairs or wrinkles.
