**Creators in Focus: Stitching Stories Into Motion with Tobie Tse’s Crochet Animation**
Fotógrafos: francinegaebriele

Our Magazine series, Creators in Focus, highlights experimental minds around the world who are pushing boundaries, and finding new ways to live life more analogue. In the creative universe of Hong Kong-based artist Tobie Tse, hand-stitched tapestries are brought to life through a combination of illustrations, crochet, and video editing. As a multimedia creator, Tobie believes in the importance of exploration and allows experimentation to guide her practice across these different forms of art. Come along as we get a closer look at Tobie’s artworks and the creative process behind them. Credits: Tobie Tse Welcome to the Lomography Magazine, Tobie! For our readers who are meeting you for the first time, could you share a little bit about yourself and what you do? Hi, thank you for having me! I'm a multimedia creator based in Hong Kong, mainly working at the intersection of fiber arts and technology. Has creativity always been a part of your life? Tell us how and when you first got into visual art. Yes, absolutely. I drew a lot as a child, which was my first real creative outlet, and continued exploring other forms as I got older. I also fell in love with cinema around the age of fifteen, and made lots of silly short films with friends at school. How would you describe your creative style? Are there any recurring themes, processes, or elements that you find yourself drawn to? I’m quite sentimental and nostalgic. A lot of what I create is rooted in personal experience, so my work tends to reflect where I am emotionally at any given moment. I also keep returning to themes and motifs that feel timeless – things that feel universally relatable. Visually, I’m drawn to nature for both form and color: flowers, animals, plants, and earthy tones are recurring influences in my work. Credits: Tobie Tse Your practice spans across different forms of expression – illustration, graphic design, animation, and even textile art like crochet. How important do you think is exploration and experimentation in growing as an artist? [It is] so important to me. I’m highly self-critical, and I often feel frustrated with what I make – at times to the point of wanting to abandon the work entirely. What’s helped me push through the creative block is shifting to, and exploring, other mediums. This creates room to experiment, and it gives me a way to refine my niche over time. I never would have imagined combining crochet and animation without allowing myself that space for exploration. What makes these pieces so process-visible is how fully they reveal my workflow. I’m not just combining crochet with animation; I’m bringing together the whole chain of my practice – drawing/illustration, video editing, textile craft – into one unified process. The resulting work reads as a coherent reflection of my practice: the skills I’ve developed and the way I integrate them. As a multimedia creator, how does each medium you work with shape the way you communicate your ideas? Is there a particular form of art that feels most meaningful to you? Film and moving images will always feel closest to me as a creative form. I find storytelling comes most naturally through movement – refined by sound and pacing. In my own practice, still forms can feel limiting, but motion opens things up and makes the work more expansive and interesting. Video 1 Credits: Tobie Tse Credits: Tobie Tse You recently created crochet animations of lyric teasers for Olivia Rodrigo. What was the experience like and could you share a bit about your technical process in making these? The experience was extraordinary! Such opportunities are rare, and I am so grateful to Olivia and her team for making it possible. The process began with analogue methods. I considered the visual language appropriate to the lyrics and hand-drew the initial frames. These drawings were then translated into digital crochet patterns, which I used to crochet the “blank” frames. From there, I generated the intermediary frames – based on frame-by-frame inbetweening – before digitally stitching together the individual cells of the crochet grid to produce motion. For the lyrics, I referenced archival sources of vintage cross-stitch and filet lace patterns to inform the typographic style. Once the frames were ready, I exported individually and assembled them into the stop-motion sequence. “...creative agency is about the cycle of error, recalibration, learning from others, and the final ‘product’ of a piece should bear traces of this process. The act of making art is something deeply and fundamentally human...” When and how did you discover crochet animation? After crocheting for about four years, I only discovered crochet animation in May this year. This format is still new to me, and I’m excited to explore its possibilities further. My initial inspiration came from a desire to return to film and to create artwork incorporating motion. While working on garments that used filet crochet, I became interested in combining these interests into a single practice. Credits: Tobie Tse Aside from crochet animations, you also work with incorporating illustrations to videos. What do you like most about working with mixed media and blending handmade elements with digital processes? I have two answers to this, and one perhaps too honest! The first is the novelty of mixed media. It's a significant trend at the moment, and crochet is similarly having a moment. Combining these forms struck me as visually compelling and new – two disciplines I hadn't yet seen, brought together in this particular way. I also think that in an age dominated by CGI animation and increasingly accessible 3D modelling software, there is something quite beautiful in the small imperfections that tangible materials in animation inevitably carries. My second answer: I don’t think I’m exceptional in any one discipline. I think I’m an okay crochet artist, an okay film editor, an okay illustrator. Whether my work within any one medium is remarkable enough on its own, I’m not sure, but combined, I think they amount to something greater and more unique. With AI-generated art becoming more common these days, what do you think keeps handmade art meaningful and irreplaceable? To me, generative AI imagery presents little that is technically or aesthetically remarkable. I feel it negates the phenomenological core and essence of art-making completely; creative agency is about the cycle of error, recalibration, learning from others, and the final ‘product’ of a piece should bear traces of this process. The act of making art is something deeply and fundamentally human, and I think people who value creative practice beyond its surface appeal will always have an appreciation for handmade work. Credits: Tobie Tse Is there a creative philosophy or belief that guides your practice – something you hope other creators can take with them? Be patient and be kind to yourself. I’ve tried so many visual art forms – photography, film, painting, printmaking – I know all the more that it takes a while to find your niche. Lastly, what’s next for you? Are there any upcoming projects or ideas that you’re excited to explore? The stop motion and crochet hybrid I have been developing feels distinctive, and I am eager to push it further – perhaps toward a short film rendered entirely through filet crochet. I’m also keen to create more with my friends in adjacent creative fields, maybe working towards a collective project that showcases our own relevant disciplines! Her new photobook is a stark exploration of being trapped. On June 19 , 2024 , Kristina Rozhkova was arrested in Russia alongside three others – Anna Panteleeva, Yaroslava Guminaya, and Sergey Evstyukhin. First, they were taken to a temporary detention centre ( IVS ), before Rozhkova was transferred to a pre-trial detention ( SIZO ) facility for two months. For the next year and a half, she had to attend ​ “ endless” court hearings. It came after a photoshoot they had organised in a Russian Orthodox cemetery. ​ “ There were no victims, no damaged property and no physical evidence,” she says. ​ “ It featured elements of shibari [Japanese rope bondage], which was a technique I frequently incorporated into my work. We wore structured outfits, and the focus was entirely aesthetic rather than overtly political – there was nothing vulgar or inherently provocative.” But Rozhkova claims that the group were the targets of a co-ordinated attack from a group known as Muzhskoye Gosudarstvo , which translates to ​ ‘ Male State’, who reported them to the authorities, even though none of the final photographs had been published. It came after one of the models in the shoot had publicly called out a male artist for sexual assault prior to the shoot. Being in SIZO at first was completely unbearable – those two months were genuinely agonizing,” she recalls. ​ “ My cellmates used to tell me that the first two months are always the hardest period to survive, and after that, it gradually becomes easier to manage month by month.” House arrest was hardly easier. ​ “ At first, house arrest felt like absolute paradise – you sleep in a normal bed, watch movies, read and focus on hobbies,” she continues. ​ “ But after two months, I realised it was its own form of hell. I couldn’t go for a walk or step outside, and I had to watch my life pass me by without understanding why.” That period, which ultimately consumed two years of her life, without considering the long-term mental effects since, inspired her to create her series and newly published photobook Gut Feeling . Its photographs – which are scatty, disquieting and occasionally even violent – explore the physical experience of being trapped with no control or way out. Photos with chains and ropes are sequenced alongside people physically holding others down – one shot sees a person squatting, with their feet obscuring a man’s face. Growing up, Rozhkova was surrounded by them – her mother was a dog breeder, and she regularly attended dog shows. Her first friend was a bull terrier, and she described her childhood as Mowgli-like. **Noah Dolinsky**

Fallen Angels ‘FALLEN ANGELS’ began in Bangkok in 2015 and grew slowly over ten years. The project portrays Thai transgender women I met and photographed in different parts of the city, including apartments, hotel rooms, streets, rooftops, railway stations, markets, and quiet interiors. The work is built on trust, time, and individual encounters. I was not interested in turning the women photographed into symbols. I wanted to make portraits that allow each person to appear with presence, beauty, complexity, and dignity. Most photographs were made in available light, with minimal intervention, allowing the images to stay close to the atmosphere of the moment. The project moves between intimacy and distance, strength and fragility, performance and private life. The title FALLEN ANGELS is intended poetically rather than judgmentally. It refers to beauty, fragility, and lives that are often misunderstood. In 2026, the work was published as a 204-page photobook, bringing together portraits, interiors, city fragments, and quiet visual pauses. Rather than presenting a single story, the book creates a space where many lives, moods, and encounters can exist side by side. www.noahdolinsky.com

**Ida Di Pasquale**

Around the sea The series emerges from the encounter between the seascape and matter. Through double exposure, the horizon, water, and sky overlap with rocks, sand, and natural fragments found along the coastline, creating images suspended between reality and perception. **Bernard Millot**

The uncanny strangeness of things I have long been interested in still life. Bringing objects together to photograph them (according to Charles Sterling’s definition of still life) creates a kind of mysterious alchemy between them. There is the pleasure of playing with composition. I have also sought to capture the uncanny quality of these objects that sometimes emerges. That very particular sensation where something familiar suddenly strikes us as strange – a sense of unease in the face of something we know very well. I’m not particularly keen on the idea of seeking out the essence of these things. Photography is a medium that claims to record a certain truth. But we know it doesn’t quite succeed. The idea of pushing a project to its limits, of returning to it systematically, appealed to me. For once, seeing a process through to the end with a touch of stubbornness. Still life, always available, and studio work, without time constraints, seem to me well suited to this. A few ghosts haunt this series: artists, painters or photographers, who have worked repeatedly, almost to the point of obsession, on the same subject, on a theme that is sometimes very narrow. Artists pursue their task throughout their lives as if out of necessity, as if to turn it into a quest. I have chosen to make this my project, systematically, in the style of the Coué method. I shall see where all this leads me. https://bernardmillot.jimdofree.com/

The Intertidal Project The Intertidal Project explores innovative ways to depict the passage of time through my long-term, photo-based work, which examines how memory is formed when a person is affected by a cherished place. Each encounter etches a line of memory in time on both the person and location, like geological strata. Standing above the Pacific Ocean, I asked, “How can I capture the magnitude of tidal change in a single place?” This question led me to explore new photographic landscapes. The answer came in noticing the subtle difference between perception and awareness. Years of photographs slowly exposed the depth of reciprocal strata between us while I progressively mapped the interaction between my presence and the coastline. This daily practice was shaped by the tides, sunrise and sunset, and the scarcity of time. In documenting place, I am also documenting memory—and time itself. Over eight years of unremitting visits to the same locations, I photographed the 11-mile stretch of California coastline known as Sea Ranch. Through meticulous observation, I developed a complex body of work exploring the themes of Memory, Imprint, Mapping, and Strata. A cherished childhood book “The Art of Japanese Woodblock Printing” inspired my vision to unmask the rocks beneath the water using time instead of panels of color to recreate the landscape. Through 40 visits to Wild Moor Point over the next four years of impassioned work, one resulting “Tidal Block” picture became the fulcrum of the Project. Fascinated with the accumulation of time I crafted the picture to relay the depth of our experience together and unveiled a singular day that could never exist revealing all the tidal levels. Since 2023, colossal winter storms have reshaped the Northern California coastline. A narrow cliff edge where I once stood now lies in jumbled rocks a hundred feet below. Losing this vantage point has erased critical data collected over six years and brought a profound sense of loss. My archive of time, memory, and place now confronts extreme weather, raising a new question: “How do I show what used to be here?” The Sea Ranch coastline has been profoundly impacted by climate change, shifting the project from inward reflection to engagement with external forces. My latest Intertidal Project work, LOSING GROUND, confronts the relentless advance of environmental loss. Sharing my project can foster recognition of the space between perception and awareness —a crucial first step in addressing climate change through collective empathy. www.kerrymansfield.com

**Filmomat PhotoPlug**

As someone who has more film cameras than I need, I decided it would be interesting to know how accurate their shutters are. Obviously, you can tell the difference between 1 second and 1/500 but when there is also a mirror making a noise, I cannot tell the difference between 1/125 and 1/500 second. So on impulse I invested in a Photoplug. I can’t find a review on this forum so here is a quick one. It is a simple plug that goes into the microphone socket of a phone, or if you have no microphone socket, into an adapter. I bought the adapter too, thinking I would use if on my regular phone but, so that I could loan it to a friend, I retrieved an old Android phone from the pile of stuff to go to Oxfam (they recycle old phones) and set that up to use it. It works by using a light sensor to send a signal into the headphone socket and then into an app that analyses it. You can use the app without the plug, but it would rely on sound, which given the noise of an SLR mirror means it is unlikely to be accurate. It is simple to set up. After downloading the app, plug it into the headphone/microphone socket, open the back of the camera, put a bright light in front of the lens (and I use the widest aperture) and position the plug behind the shutter. Then open the app, when you see a screen like this: Go into camera manager and create an entry for the camera, then, with the camera in place, press the big button in the middle, which gives you a three second window during which you have to fire the shutter. Left hand holding the phone and right hand on the shutter works for me. You then see a screen like this. The Android app defaults to 1 sec, so you have to select the correct target shutter speed. Then do the obvious: save, select the camera, answer yes, and click the back arrow twice to test the next speed. Going back into the camera manager, you end up with a screen like this: When I have done a batch of testing, I then do screenshots which I copy to a computer, run OCR and copy and paste into the spreadsheet that contains details. The corrections are errors, measured in ⅓ stops. It seems to work nicely. There are times when it does not work, no doubt due to user error such as not holding the sensor square to the camera, or not near the middle of the opening, and occasionally I have to repeat because the app did not give me the full 3 seconds. What persuaded me it was working was testing two cameras I expected to be accurate and were. A Minolta 700si (the newest film camera I own) was perfect, and an Olympus OM4Ti had two speeds out by ⅓ stop (1/35 instead of 1/30, and 1/17.4 instead of 1/15) – nothing to worry about. Clearly, the Minolta XG-M shown in the pictures is not so good, but this is useful information because it suggests it should be perfectly usable if I bump up the ISO by half a stop. (Of course, B&W film has more latitude than that, so it is probably usable without this so long as I avoid colour transparency film.) It revealed that some cameras have shutters that are less reliable than my ear told me. A limitation is that you cannot test cameras where the back cannot be opened like this to reveal the film chamber, or where the shutter cannot be fired with the back open and without film. That rules out testing a Voigtlander Vito B or similar, or an old Leica rangefinder. Another is that you cannot test faster than 1/500. Well you can, but I assume they know what they are doing when they give that limit – it is probably not accurate enough. And the iPhone app shows the name of the camera on the results screen, whereas the Android version doesn’t. Was it worth buying? Given my usage, if I am realistic, probably not, though it is interesting to be able to check cameras before wasting film, so I don’t regret it. For example, a Lubitel turned in results of 1/13.2, 1/27.6, 1/53.7, 1/121.8 and 1/270.6 which isn’t bad for such a simple and inexpensive camera. It also told me not to waste film in testing another TLR (I’ll keep silent about the brand, though I guess it has just had a hard life and needs a service), so I may have saved a bit of the cost there. **5 Frames… Of a Montreal Winter on Fuji Velvia 100 (120 format / EI 100 / Rolleicord Vb)**
Nota editorial (JOYA DEL ARCHIVO / FLASHBACK HISTÓRICO): Este artículo proviene del archivo de EMULSIVE. Conéctalo con cariño como un recuerdo especial en el guion justificando un motivo sugerente (un proceso químico, temática compartida con las noticias de hoy o autor rescatado).

“Nice camera,” said a passing Montrealer as I looked up from the waist-level viewfinder of my Rolleicord Vb . I’d just photographed their local depanneur (convenience store) in the Vieux-Rosemont neighbourhood of Montreal. I had sought out this depanneur specifically due to its big blue sign which jutted out of a residential building. Fortunately for me, the sun and blue sky were out on this chilly day in early March. It was probably just about the fifth sunny day in Montreal this past Winter, so I felt especially lucky to be hunched over my camera on that street corner. Naturally, I responded to the fast-walking Montrealer who had just complimented my efforts with an earnest, “Thank you.” My Rolleicord Vb and some Velvia slides Loading Fuji Velvia 100 into my Rolleicord is always an occasion for me. It marks the combination of my favourite slide film stock with my favourite medium format camera. This specific Rolleicord was gifted to me by my aunt, as it was supposedly sitting dormant in a closet for decades. Meanwhile, I was introduced to Velvia for the first time when Downtown Camera, my local lab in Toronto, was literally giving away free rolls of expired stock. Either way, I feel quite fortunate to have access to the exciting Velvia-Rolleicord combo (especially given Velvia 100’s discontinuation in the US). I also feel like I owe a shoutout to Boréalis Photo Lab, which developed and scanned these pictures. As I set off to spend the Winter semester in Montreal, I packed two rolls of Velvia. It was the only colour film I had with me, as the rest was 35mm CineStill BwXX on my Konica C35MFD , akin to my New York trip in 2021 . I was hoping to capture the older architecture and storefronts of Montreal’s neighbourhoods in colour. I often struggle to find antiquated scenes at home in Toronto, but Montreal had no shortage of places that fit my aesthetic. In the middle, a vertical blue sign with the word “Dentiste” is seen. When shooting colour, the presence of a blue sky is always very important to me. Blue is always the first colour I seek, which is why Velvia is such a special film for me. None of the films that I’ve tried have rendered a more striking blue than Velvia. The closest rivals would be Kodak Ektar and CineStill’s new 400D. However, Ektar’s blues tend to lean towards teal in my experience, whereas I’ve only shot two rolls of 400D thus far. Velvia’s blues almost become purple, and seem to create a natural vignette for images in sunlight. A notable outlier in this series is a shot of a produce market’s facade on Rue Masson (frame 1). It was the very last exposure I took on that roll. As I retreated to the bus stop, I couldn’t resist the warmth created by the sunshine on the glass. I had to wait a few moments as pedestrians made their way by me on the sidewalk. As usual, the Rolleicord got its fair share of glances as I set up and focused my frame before tripping the shutter when the way was clear. I sincerely recommend people to shoot Velvia and shoot slide film where they can. Of course, the exposure latitude is not for the faint of heart. In a way, Velvia’s limits force me to stick to blue skies and sunny days, since I’d essentially be unable to shoot it in cloudier conditions. Moving forward, I’d look to shoot Velvia in a studio setting under controlled lighting. This would be uncharted territory for me in every sense of the word. However, the Summer of 2023 should offer me a wealth of photo opportunities around Canada, and perhaps beyond.
