**Kutxa Fundazioa/Tabakalera, San Sebastian : Sally Mann : Macula & Materia**

Nacido en 1951 en Lexington, Virginia, Sally Mannes una de las principales figuras de la fotografía contemporánea americana. Desde finales de los años 60, ha construido un cuerpo exigente de trabajo arraigado en el Sur Americano, su región nativa, que nunca ha dejado de explorar. Atentiva a la materialidad de la imagen tanto como a la memoria que lleva, el fotógrafo autodidacta adoptó la cámara de gran formato temprano. Un aparato imponente que exige una combinación de la lentitud del paciente y el rigor técnico, este proceso ofrece un paisaje distintivo. La exposición Macula &amp;amp; Materia traza un camino a través de la serie mayor que ha marcado este cuerpo de trabajo. Reúne, entre otros, A las doce. (1983-1985), Familia inmediata (1984–1994), los paisajes de Virginia (1992–1996), los paisajes de Georgia (1996) y los paisajes profundos del sur (1998), así como los cuerpos de trabajo posteriores: Caras (2004), Carne de proud (2006-2015), Delta (2016), Piedras platino (2022), y Goshen (2025). La gama de temas —niñez, familia, paisaje, memoria histórica, enfermedad, decadencia y materialidad— cuestiona todo lo que soporta, se transmite o desaparece. In A las doce., la adolescencia aparece como un punto de inflexión, marcado por la inestabilidad, la transformación corporal y la construcción de la identidad. Lejos de las representaciones convencionales de la infancia, la fotógrafa observa sus súbditos sin idealizarlos. Su método se basa en la colaboración y el mínimo estancamiento, en el que los espacios domésticos o rurales juegan un papel esencial. El entorno nunca es neutral; contribuye a la tensión de las imágenes, como si cada habitación o cada camino retenía el rastro de una experiencia. Estaba con Familia inmediata, sin embargo, que Mann ganó reconocimiento internacional, acompañado por la visibilidad mediática marcada por la polémica. Hecho durante diez años, la serie sigue la vida cotidiana de sus tres hijos en la propiedad familiar en Virginia. Cuando el libro fue publicado en 1992, ciertas imágenes suscitaron intenso debate en los Estados Unidos. Los críticos vieron en ellos una transgresión de las normas sociales que rigen la representación de la infancia. En el trabajo de Sally Mann, la historia del Sur Americano está constantemente navegando. Sus paisajes están ligados a episodios de violencia, pero el fotógrafo no busca reconstruir ni ilustrarlos. Su enfoque sugiere su persistencia a través de topografía, niebla, luz y la textura de las imágenes. Manteniendo su distancia del fotoperiodismo o la fotografía documental, su enfoque equivale a una meditación visual en las capas cargadas de la memoria pasada. La Guerra Civil, la historia de la esclavitud, y la violencia racial no son por tanto un escenario pintoresco. Forman una realidad histórica y cultural cuyos efectos todavía se sienten en el presente. Su trabajo pertenece de inmediato a una tradición fotográfica estadounidense que vincula el paisaje a la identidad nacional y a una investigación personal sobre la familia, el cuerpo y el dolor. En su núcleo, plantea la pregunta: ¿cómo puede una imagen todavía aferrarse a un tiempo que nunca deja de desaparecer? Howard Greenberg Gallery regalosBaldwin Lee: Nueva York (1975-1979), una exposición de fotografías en blanco y negro a gran escala. A la vista del 17 de septiembre al 31 de octubre de 2026, la exposición reúne fotografías que Lee hizo entre 1975 y 1979, un período formativo en su carrera, y marca la primera vez que han estado a la vista. En los años que siguieron, regresó repetidamente a Nueva York con una cámara de 4 × 5 vistas, fotografiando la ciudad a primera hora de la mañana, a menudo en invierno, cuando sus calles estaban casi vacías. Lee se acercó a Manhattan no como una colección de temas arquitectónicos, sino como un lugar formado por la presencia humana. Las figuras humanas raramente aparecen, pero su presencia sigue siendo legible en las superficies desgastadas de calles y edificios, tiendas y coches estacionados. “Mis viajes a Nueva York desde Nueva Inglaterra fueron una exploración de edificios como formas orgánicas. En lugar de acercarme al trabajo como fotografía arquitectónica, lo vi como una forma de capturar un paisaje”, dijo Lee. “Fue mi primera vez usando una cámara de gran formato. A principios de la mañana, sin nadie alrededor, sentí una intimidad abrumadora, como si estuviera caminando por mi propio jardín privado”. Los temas de Lee incluyen Wall Street al amanecer, Times Square, y las calles y lotes vacantes del Bajo Manhattan, donde el World Trade Center se encuentra en la distancia. Registra una ciudad suspendida entre el abandono y la ambición, donde los restos del pasado permanecen visibles junto a las estructuras del nuevo. Vio cincuenta años más tarde, las fotografías leídas como celebración y elegía, un registro de una ciudad eternamente borrando y rehaciéndose. En 1982, Lee se trasladó a Knoxville, Tennessee, donde estableció el programa de fotografía en la Universidad de Tennessee. Al año siguiente, comenzó una extensa serie de viajes por el Sur Americano. Entre 1983 y 1989, hizo casi 10.000 fotografías, principalmente de comunidades negras, creando el cuerpo de trabajo para el que ahora es más conocido. Las fotografías de Nueva York revelan un aspecto diferente pero relacionado de la práctica de Lee. Su cuidadosa organización del espacio arquitectónico y su atención sostenida al carácter del lugar ayudaron a establecer el lenguaje visual distintivo que llevaría a su trabajo posterior. Ellos demuestran una sensibilidad a la forma y el orden, pero también a la humanidad inscrita en el entorno construido. La visión de Lee de Nueva York es un registro de lo que la gente hizo, habitado, alterado y dejado atrás. **Kehrer Verlag : Wolfgang Zurborn : Crowds**

Kehrer Verlag publica Crowds por Wolfgang Zurborn. Durante más de cuatro décadas, el fotógrafo alemán ha observado a la gente en espacios públicos, girando su cámara hacia encuentros, gestos y coincidencias que revelan la dinámica de la vida social. En Crowds, reúne fotografías tomadas desde los años 80, creando un retrato vivo y multicapa de una era formada por la cultura de masas, el condicionamiento social y la influencia omnipresente de los medios. Los eventos políticos, deportivos, religiosos, turísticos y de entretenimiento se convierten en etapas en las que se desarrolla el comportamiento colectivo. Entre los cuerpos densamente reunidos, Zurborn captura momentos en los que su coreografía da lugar a complejas narraciones sobre los deseos y miedos de las personas. Sus fotografías no disuelven a las personas en la ornamentación de la multitud ni las aíslan como individuos desprendidos del contexto. Trabajando en la tradición de la fotografía callejera de color, Zurborn abraza la abundancia visual de la vida pública en lugar de intentar imponer una jerarquía clara sobre ella. Sus imágenes están llenas de información, invitando al ojo a moverse entre el primer plano y el fondo, entre detalles aparentemente incidentales y patrones más grandes. Lo que aparece inicialmente caótico revela gradualmente un orden visual sutil — uno que nunca resuelve completamente la imprevisibilidad y vitalidad de la escena. El proyecto tomó forma durante los bloqueos de la pandemia coronavirus, cuando las reuniones públicas de repente habían llegado a un punto muerto. Zurborn utilizó este período para regresar a su archivo, escaneando cientos de negativos analógicos y descubriendo conexiones a través de décadas de fotografías. La pausa forzada en una cultura de masas sobreestimulada le llevó a reflexionar de nuevo sobre la forma en que la gente está formada por su entorno social y los medios que los rodean, y viven. Crowds no impone una interpretación singular. Más bien, las fotografías de Zurborn transmiten un sentido expresivo del momento presente, invitándonos a mirar de cerca las complejas, contradictorias y a menudo caóticas estructuras de la sociedad contemporánea. **Editorial RM : Sebastián Thomas : Pyramid of the sun**

Editorial RM regalos Pirámide del sol por Sebastián Thomas. Pirámide del Sol explora la visión utópica de un arquitecto que, a finales de los años 70, se propuso llevar la antigua Pirámide del Sol en Teotihuacán, México, al corazón de Santiago de Chile, transformándola en un centro comercial visionario. **Braun Paxette IIM and Super Paxette I review**

The Paxette range of viewfinder cameras were produced in Germany after the Second World War by Braun in Nuremberg. There is another well-known German company (currently a wholy-owned subsidiary of Proctor and Gamble) called Braun. They became rather famous for making electric shavers and hair grooming products. This is not that company. That Braun didn’t made stills cameras, although they did make Radiograms, record players, movie cameras, slide projectors (in direct competition to the Nuremberg company), clocks and kitchen appliances, as well as electric shavers. The company that made the Paxette cameras and the Paximat range of slide projectors was started by Carl Braun as Karl Braun KG, Fabrik optischer Geräte und Metallwaren in 1915, it predated the other company by 6 years. In 1948 they changed the name of the Nuremberg company to Carl Braun Camera-Werke. Under their new name, they produced a series of Rollfilm and 35mm cameras. Prominent among these were a series of Paxette direct viewfinder cameras. The Paxette range The Paxette cameras are diminutive, featuring a wrap around, round edged body in two parts. The rear section slides off downwards and consists of the back, sides and base of the camera. A fixed spring in the back holds the hinged pressure-plate of the other section of the camera in place. The two-part body is a feature of the Paxette range The first Paxette camera appeared in 1951. It had a flat top-plate, a fixed 45mm lens and a knob wind, but it was recognisably similar to the subjects of this review. The shutter release is integral to the shutter-lens unit. Other than the shutter being set by the camera’s film advance, the set-up is remarkably simple and modular. Basic anatomy is the same on both models. The shutter release is a simple tab on the side of the lens mount, with a threaded cable release just above it (with cut-out in the buttress where it is present. The other side of the mount features a flash terminal and X-M selector together with the self-timer control. The small plunger near the winder is the rewind button. Note that the ‘Super’ at the top has a slightly higher top in comparison to the IIM – possibly to give the rangefinder more room. Variants The engraving on Paxette cameras does not help too much in identifying individual models – other than that models with a coupled rangefinder tend to have ‘Super’ in their name. Fixed lens models are generally called I, while the interchangeable lens versions are referred to as II. According to Collection-appareils , there were 14 variants of Paxette Cameras, with a further 7 ‘Super’ models. Differences include the presence of an exposure meter (some extinction, some selenium), the presence of a rangefinder (some coupled, some uncoupled), Knob or lever-assisted wind, interchangeable or fixed lenses, conventional or lever rewind, and brightline or plain viewfinder. The two models I’m looking at in this article are a Paxette IIM (interchangeable lens, uncoupled rangefinder, lever wind – hereafter the ‘IIM’) and a Super Paxette I (Pointar lens, coupled rangefinder, originally lever wind, hereafter the ‘Super’). The IIM is an earlier design, but the lever wind places both cameras around 1956-1958. Camera features One of the more obvious differences between the two cameras is the buttress surrounding the lens mount on the interchangeable-lens version. This reinforcement seems to have been added to cater for the extra torsion forces that the lens mount takes when a lens is screwed tight into the mount. The Paxette IIM. Note the reinforcing ring with buttresses to support the M39 screw thread mount. The little dial to the left of the wind-on lever controls the rangefinder: the distance is read off here and simply transferred to the lens scale. The Super Paxette I has clean lines, allowing a clearer view of the shutter speed range. Note some detail changes around the cold shoe and frame counter, which has switched to a more ‘modern’ black background. Shutter The Prontor-SVS shutter used on both cameras ranges from 1-1/300 plus B. The indicated speeds on the IIM go 5-10-25-50100-300, while the Super shows the more modern 8-15-30-60-125-300 markings. While the SVS is a mechanical shutter, it is constantly variable, so you can (for instance) set it between 100 and 300 for an intermediate (but somewhat imprecise) speed. Lenses The IIM takes a variant of the M39 screw mount. Not the version with the 28.8mm registration distance used by Leica, nor the 45.2mm registration distance version as used by Zenit on some early SLRs, but a 44mm registration distance version, peculiar to Braun. If you have any Zenit 3M 39mm lenses, it should be possible to shim them out to work on the non-coupled versions of the Braun cameras. The Paxette IIM lens mount Both of these cameras feature 45mm f/2.8 lenses. My IIM wears a Cassarit from Steinheil of Munich, while the Super, has its ‘fixed’ Roeschlein-Kreuznach Pointar. Other standards included Kataplast, Casser and Staeble-Kata 45mm lenses. The lens on my ‘Super’ is not supposed to come off, but the stub on this body that is supposed to stop the lens unscrewing (at 5 O’clock in this picture, between the tabs that hinge the dark plate), has broken off. As the helicoid of the lens screws towards its infinity setting, the back of the lens pushes the plate (notice the ‘scuff’), and the movement is read by a plunger at the 11 O’clock position. There were a fair number of lens choices for Braun’s version of M39. Interchangeable lenses seem to have been available from 35 to 135mm from a whole range of suppliers, including Carl Zeiss, Schneider and Wray. I’ve not got access to any extra lenses. Lenses tend to have multiple blades (12 is quite common) and stop down to around f/16. Viewfinder The Viewfinders of both of these cameras are very similar. The ability of the IIM to take wide-angle and telephoto lenses is one thing, but the viewfinder gives no assistance whatsoever. I can only think that it must have been assumed that you would use a supplementary viewfinder. After a recent run of using cameras with poor viewfinders (Lomo 120, Konica 28W), I’ve now hit rock bottom. The Paxette viewfinder, is as bad as a viewfinder could get without deliberately pointing in the wrong direction or attempting to poke your eye out. The only way is up. The viewfinder is small, not particularly bright, rather difficult for spectacle wearers. It has no bright lines and shows more than the standard 45mm lenses will project onto the film. Braun may have made their cameras uglier with their later high-top bright-frame versions, but I would see it as entirely justified if it improved the viewfinder. Top is as seen through the Super Paxette viewfinder. Below is the actual negative superimposed. It is more usual (and useful) if the negative captures slightly more than you expect from the viewfinder image. Lever wind The lever wind works by ratcheting one disk over another. The lower disk needs to turn 360 degrees to advance the film and cock the shutter. The film winds and the shutter cocks with 1 and 2/3 strokes of the winder (although you can use multiple short strokes). Film counter Both cameras have a manually set film counter (counting down) which is built into the cold shoe. It is neat and seems reliable. Changes to the film gate Although the sliding back seems to have been a constant through the history of the Paxette range, there are detail changes to the hinged pressure plate and film transport between these two models. The earlier design from the IIM uses a single large sprocket driving the top row of sprockets and features a pressure plate that hinges from the top, while the Super has a more conventional transport pulling both sets of perforations of 35mm film and hinges the pressure plate at the bottom. It is also notable that the later design features a prominent serial number – something absent from earlier cameras. There has been a redesign of the film transport – the old hinged-from-the-top single-sprocket transport has been swapped out for a dual-sprocket drive with the pressure plate hinged at the bottom… The change to film transport seems to have made a difference to negative spacing. Frame spacing with both cameras is variable, but is noticeably wider with the Paxette IIM. Negative strips: The IIM at the top, with the ‘Super’ below Issues The rewind of the Paxettes is not intuitive. With the rewind knob down in what might be thought of as its normal working position, it doesn’t engage with the forks that fit into the 135 cassette. This means you don’t get confirmation of the film transport by seeing the knob turn as you wind. To rewind, you need to pull the rewind knob up about 1cm, which makes it engage with the forks. Once the knob is in the raised position , you can then press the rewind plunger on the top-plate and rewind the film. It is just a quirk, but it can be unsettling if you don’t know about it (or if you don’t have access to a manual) my first films were unloaded in a dark bag. My Super seems to have had the lever-assist tab of its winder cut off at some point – effectively converting it to knob-wind (the tab assist is a bit faster, but the knob-wind is OK), Prior to running a film through the Super for this review, I remember taking it to Sheffield around the time I was writing my first Minolta SRT 101 review back in the summer of 2020. At that time the Paxette had a good go at shredding the perforations on the film I loaded. I think I put the remainder of the roll through another camera. As I had lots else going on at the time, I just put the camera aside until recently. This time I didn’t have any issues with film transport. What I did have a problem with was shutter timings. I tend to steer away from the slower speeds with old leaf shutters as the lack of ‘exercise’ of those speeds tends to make them a little ‘unpredictable’; however the faster speeds are generally OK. The Prontor-SVS shutter used in the Paxettes only goes up to 1/300, and as the summer of 2026 when this article is being prepared, has been quite bright, I was using it a fair amount. When the film came out of the tank I could see quite quickly that the majority of the shots were overexposed, although the shot I took on a tripod with the speed set to either 1/125 or 1/60 to show viewfinder coverage was far better exposed. It could be a duff shutter, or it could just be that the shutter needed more exercising at its fastest speeds. Anyhow, that is my excuse for the scarcity and quality of sample Super Paxette shots in this article. The Paxette IIM shutter seems to operate fine. While the rangefinder in the Super was quite good (about the only positive thing I can say about the viewfinder), the rangefinder patch in the IIM is very hard to see. I suspect that someone has previously cleaned up the rangefinder parts in the Super, but that the IIM is still dealing with 70 years of accumulated grime. Normally, I would take the top-plate off and clean everything up, but one of the pages I found on the internet had the nearest I’ve ever seen to a ‘Here be dragons’ warning regarding the lever wind mechanism used on the Paxette cameras. Basically, it warned that getting the springs and pawls back together was challenging . As the winder would need to come off to remove the top-plate and access the rangefinder, I decided that improving the visibility of the uncoupled rangefinder was not worth the risk. It did maybe explain why my ‘Super’ had ended up with a simplified knob-wind though.. Fellow travellers I’m convinced that there must have been some co-operation between Braun and Aeronautical General Instruments’ AGILux camera subsidiary. The IIM is even more like the Agimatic than I noted the Super Paxette was. It has to be said that British firms did have a go at playing fast and loose with German camera patents during and after the war (the AGIFlex and MicroCord being examples), but the similarities between the Paxette and Agimatic/ Agima cameras just seem too sustained (and two-way) not to have been some form of co-operation. Both companies were of similar sizes and for much of the 1950s until export controls came off in the UK, they were not in direct competition. I’ve not found anything about co-operation or patent challenges, but this degree of convergent development stretches credibility a bit. **An Archive of Us**

News - September 15, 2026 by Marina Vitaglione Somewhere in the middle of assembling The Sound of Dirt and Beauty , Jesse Glazzard found a photograph of himself at seven years old, arm outstretched, taking his own picture. He hadn’t remembered it existed. “So I guess it’s always kind of been there, which is kind of crazy,” he says, when asked when self-portraiture began. The discovery was one of many that surfaced while searching through ten years of negatives for the earliest photograph he had made of his friend Ella – the first person he began documenting, and in many ways the starting point of the book. When he finally found the images, he was undone by them. “I was just crying,” he says, “because I was reliving so many things.” The book took a year to assemble, but the lives and friendships inside it had been unfolding for a decade. There is something of that unfolding in the photographs themselves – a sense of time accumulating gently, without announcing itself. Glazzard was introduced to photography by his father, in a darkroom set up in his grandad’s pantry and on walks along a canal where he learned to notice light, composition and the shape of things. “You can take a good photo on a shit phone,” his father would tell him. It wasn’t until years later, when a tutor at Salford questioned his admiration for the British fashion photographer Tim Walker and introduced him instead to Nan Goldin and Richard Kern, that his understanding of what a photograph could be began to loosen. “I don’t know why I’d assumed photography had to be this staged thing,” he says. “When she showed me that work, it changed my perspective.” The Sound of Dirt and Beauty© Jesse Glazzard The Sound of Dirt and Beauty© Jesse Glazzard That looseness gradually became a way of working. The early studio portraits now seem almost tender in their naivety, Glazzard says – “even though it was just people coming over to have a cup of tea” – before the photographs moved out of formal setups and into bedrooms, kitchens and the unposed middle of things. The camera became less an instrument for arranging a picture than a way of staying close to a person. “I think now it feels like I’m trying to get as close as possible to the person, without feeling like the camera’s there.” That closeness is built slowly. Glazzard often meets people at parties or through friends, spending time with them before making a photograph. Some sitters take years before he feels ready to photograph them – an ease that seems less constructed than sensed. “You know there’s going to be a connection,” he says, “because they’re already not hiding – they’re willing to be seen.” The photographs carry the traces of that patience: people caught between poses, laughing, waiting, looking away; domestic spaces becoming records of the relationships that formed within them. “I’m trying to build an archive of us,” Glazzard says, a phrase that describes the book’s deepest impulse – not simply to preserve what people looked like, but to hold onto the experience of being together. The Sound of Dirt and Beauty© Jesse Glazzard The Sound of Dirt and Beauty© Jesse Glazzard The Sound of Dirt and Beauty© Jesse Glazzard His self-portraits carry a quieter urgency. Growing up searching history books for people who looked like him and finding little to recognise, he came to photography as a way of writing himself into the picture. “I wanted to hold the power over my own image,” he says. Near the end of the book, the photograph of seven-year-old Glazzard returns with a new resonance. The child holding the camera could not have known what that gesture would become – a way of looking, of staying close to people, of making a record of the lives unfolding around him. What began almost accidentally has become an archive, not of a single life but of the people who have passed through it. The Sound of Dirt and Beauty (SPBH Editions, 2026) by Jesse Glazzard is published by MACK and available at 25£. She is a photo editor at The Wall Street Journal’s London bureau, where she collaborates with photographers and photojournalists on international assignments, and she regularly writes on photography. Her personal photography practice focuses on environmental crises, weaving together poetic and documentary approaches to reveal the invisible realities of the Anthropocene. Bakin printed these and other portraits, together with interiors and details hinting at lingering moments, on discontinued photographic paper from the 1960s to the 2000s. **The Brazenness of Bangkok’s Street Youth**

News - September 14, 2026 by Guénola Pellen The photography of Chardchakaj Waikawee, born in Bangkok in 1980, resists easy classification. It belongs neither to social documentary, with its cool rigor, nor to art portraiture, with its aestheticizing restraint. It is something else, something harder to name, and therefore more precious. His work has traveled internationally for more than fifteen years. “Youth”, the exhibition devoted to him by London’s Graces Mews, brings together a body of analog photographs taken between 2008 and 2020. The title borrows the Thai phrase wai run , a term that reaches far beyond its literal translation to describe an inner disposition, an energy of being that neither age nor poverty can extinguish. Waikawee’s subjects include motorcycle-taxi drivers, street vendors, and more broadly the young people who survive on the margins of Bangkok’s economy. “Bangkok is chaos,” he says. “Heaven and hell exist on the same street.” One enters the exhibition through a filmed portrait of the photographer, his voice continuing to murmur through the galleries like an undertow. This sonic device anchors the images in a meditation on memory, time and value: sober words for photographs that are anything but. For Waikawee’s eye plays as much as it bears witness. One need only look at the boy sipping Coca-Cola from a plastic bag, staring straight into the lens with quiet defiance, to be convinced of it. Or the punk whose extravagant pink mohawk bursts through the open sunroof of a champagne-colored Mercedes. Untitled (Woman in Green Dress) © Chardchakaj Waikawee. Courtesy of Chardchakaj Waikawee and Graces Mews Untitled (Boy with Coca-Cola Bag) © Chardchakaj Waikawee. Courtesy of Chardchakaj Waikawee and Graces Mews Untitled (Mohawk and Mercedes) © Chardchakaj Waikawee. Courtesy of Chardchakaj Waikawee and Graces Mews What strikes one, technically, is the deliberate insolence of the process. Waikawee works with expired film, repaired cameras, hand-printing techniques. Cross-processing produces unpredictable chromatic shifts — lemon yellow, scorched orange, acid magenta — which the photographer treats as so many traces of time’s passage upon the material itself. The scratches, the dust, the chemical marks are not accidents but the visible memory of the making, a reminder that every image is also a physical object. Full of life, these portraits are anything but miserabilist. “Each image reflects a relationship before it becomes a photograph,” Waikawee notes. He foregrounds the personality, the friendship, the singular sense of style of his subjects, who wear their appearance as armor against the social and economic invisibility the city imposes upon them. Take the laborers crammed into the back of a truck, riding home at night in a tableau at once baroque and entirely ordinary. Or the boy whose back, tattooed with sacred Sak Yant script, is covered in inscriptions down to the waistband of his jeans. One will long remember, too, the doll-like face of the young woman, her eyes lined in black, holding the camera’s gaze without flinching. So many ways, for this sidelined but defiant youth, to compose, with whatever means are to hand, a presence in the world. Untitled (Workers Going Home) © Chardchakaj Waikawee. ### British Journal of Photography (1854)

**Expanded photography: the work of Forensic Architecture and Giles Price**

On 17 October 2023, an explosion took place in the courtyard of al-Ahli Hospital in Gaza City. Forensic Architecture created a digital reconstruction of the blast by tracing lines from the crater to the locations of shrapnel markings on buildings around the courtyard in order to better understand how the incident unfolded. From When it Stopped Being a War: The Situated Testimony of Dr Ghassan Abu-Sittah, Forensic Architecture, 2024. What would it mean to see the world – long-term and predominantly – from the perspective not of the victors but the survivors? Forensic Architecture and Giles Price consider their efforts towards a psychological transformation of eyesight and images of war A rocket strikes a hospital. Hundreds die. A spokesperson for the Israeli military appears on the mainstream media of two further states, the BBC in the UK and CNN in the US, holding up an image of a flash in the sky, a still pulled from a video. He attributes this bright light, this explosion, to a misfiring rocket launched by Palestinian Islamic Jihad. But really, he says, we do not need to go extensively into these details. Going into detail is the hallmark of the image-based evidentiary practice of Forensic Architecture (FA), the research centre based at Goldsmiths, University of London and with affiliates in cities around the world. Its approach to working with the photographic image – in tandem with technologies such as mapping, spatial analysis and photogrammetry – has made manifest aspects of documentary photography that might seem to unburden it of its troubled status as proof; its performativity, its constructedness, its inherent uncertainty. Yet instead, FA’s practice offers the documentary image as a potent vector for uncovering facts, not as representation but by offering an explanation. --> Markings left by the blast on a tree in the central courtyard of al-Ahli Hospital in Gaza City. Forensic Architecture, 2024. “I wanted to mirror the surveilling and targeting point of view, but with the hi-res and clarity to see the destruction caused by it. At ground level, the rubble was comprised of homes and schools” – Giles Price In relation to the pulled image, the Israeli military spokesman invoked public trust in seeing and believing, as he superimposed his own narrative on the still. But FA geolocated, stabilised and positioned the original footage, alongside another video of the same event, within their existing 3D model of Gaza, to show the explosion pictured is in fact an Israeli interceptor. Everything turns on the detail. Detailism, as Piyel Haldar writes, is a technique of visual dissociation, which entails a stepping back from what can clearly be seen, to pay attention to something considered otherwise irrelevant. It was used by art historian Giovanni Morelli as a method to identify fake paintings by the tiniest, least significant feature, and acknowledged by Freud for its similarity to the process of psychoanalysis. While this method can be applied to the individual photograph – it is, after all, closely affiliated to Walter Benjamin’s concept of the optical unconscious – its potency is amplified when many photographs of an event or a location can be found, analysed and measured. Since the advent of Google Earth, the photograph’s relationship to mapping has taken centre stage; in fact, the former has replaced the latter. Maps are now photographs, though their histories have always been entwined. The long-standing art of cartography was wholly reshaped by the photographic, which enabled new ways to create spatial and geographic representations. Following the vision of Nadar, who pictured Paris in 1858 from a hot air balloon tethered to the Arc de Triomphe, American photographer JW Black flew a balloon above Boston Common two years later, to picture his city in Boston, as the Eagle and the Wild Goose See it. Photographic technologies combined with map-making to extend ways of seeing the world, and were quickly utilised to military ends, creating a form of visual power and cultural dominance. The union of airplanes and photography – “these two globalising mediums” to quote Allan Sekula – helped rationalise the practice of warfare; an ongoing process. In his 1936 essay From Pigment to Light, photographer László Moholy-Nagy referred presciently to the meeting of photography and cartography as an “advance in technique [that] almost amounts to a psychological transformation of our eyesight”. On 04 August 2011, Mark Duggan was shot and killed by an anonymised Metropolitan Police officer in Tottenham, north London. The Met claimed that officers were attempting to arrest Duggan on suspicion of possession of an illegal firearm, one which they alleged he then threw over a nearby fence after exiting the vehicle. Biomechanics experts told Forensic Architecture that to travel to where it was found, the gun would have to have been thrown at 6.7 metres per second, at an angle of around 31–40 degrees. An independent pathologist deemed it “very unlikely” that Duggan would have been able to throw the gun that distance after being shot. From The Killing of Mark Duggan, Forensic Architecture, 2020. Forensic Architecture’s rendering of the moment Duggan steps out of a minicab, surrounded by police officers. Forensic Architecture, 2020. --> This transformation quite literally brought another dimension to the act of seeing, especially when used in conjunction with photogrammetry, used as early as 1851 for topographic surveying by French military officer Aimé Laussedat. The practice evolved due to the efforts of Albrecht Meydenbauer, a government surveyor, who created the first 360-degree photographic record of Berlin in 1868. Compared to an earlier, pin-sharp camera lucida-created painting of Berlin, it was a visual failure; blurry, imperfect. But its aim, resonant with FA founder Eyal Weizman’s 150 years later, was to offer more than a visual record; it evidenced visual order. Naming this order, Weizman says, “The ‘politics of verticality’ entails the re-visioning of existing cartographic techniques. It requires an MC Escher-like representation of space, a territorial hologram in which political acts of manipulation and multiplication of the territory transform a two-dimensional surface into a three-dimensional volume.” It is this understanding that has shaped Weizman’s decades-long investigation into the violence enacted upon Palestinians by the State of Israel, the land of his birth. He has defined the multiple spatial practices designed to control every facet of life in the Occupied Territories, including militarising the airspace, politicising access to water, and – via archaeological excavation – the narrative of the ancient past. At the same time, the complex patchwork of roads, bridges, tunnels, checkpoints and borders create, from its struggle to ‘separate the inseparable’, a hollow land, a phrase that would become the title of one of his, and FA’s, foundational texts. It was via mapping techniques that Weizman was able to demonstrate this thesis, and through the continued use of mapping, geolocation and photogrammetry, as well as via open-source data, images and 3D modelling software, that the practice of counter-forensics was established. Its philosophical underpinning was born of Edward Said’s insistence on counter-cartography as an act of resistance, and his call for new images and imaginings in the “struggle over geography”. The nature of counter-forensics makes it a useful vehicle to investigate state violence, both internationally and in the UK. The FA investigation into the killing of Mark Duggan by an anonymised Metropolitan Police officer in north London in 2011 served as evidence to support his family’s claim for damages against the police force, following an earlier inquest’s verdict of ‘lawful killing’. FA used contemporary photographs made public by the inquest, as well as site surveys, to create a 3D model, using photogrammetry techniques. Statements by police officers present at the scene created the next evidentiary layer, forming the foundation of a complex display highlighting the contradictions between their accounts. FA’s use of testimony to expand the visual has become cardinal to its methodology. Forensic Architecture reconstructs the perspective and range of vision of officer V53, who fired the fatal shot as Duggan stepped out of the minicab. V53 claimed to have seen a gun in Duggan’s hand, yet had no memory of seeing him throw it. If Duggan had made a large, sweeping motion with his right hand, could V53 have missed it? Forensic Architecture, 2020. --> In relation to the Duggan case, as an evidentiary work, the model, or ‘assemblage’, was able to show the precise position of Duggan’s and the officers’ bodies, and locations, at the time the shots were fired. The analysis focused on the alleged throwing of the gun said to be in Duggan’s possession, which was found almost seven metres away from where he was shot. The counter-investigation reveals various gaps in video evidence, noting that of the seven video files that comprised ‘Witness B footage’, only six were referred to by one of the specialist image analysts employed by the inquest. A further gap in the footage occurred at a moment that was heavily scrutinised by the court, yet was left unmentioned. This gap reverberates with the evidentiary echo of the withheld Frame 313 from Abraham Zapruder’s home-movie footage of President JF Kennedy’s assassination in 1963, which he sold to Life magazine, requesting that one exceptionally gory frame never be shown. Film-maker Errol Morris calls this frame ‘the forensic heart’ of the 26-second film. Life complied with Zapruder’s wish, and the frame that might contain the crucial documentary evidence to determine the position of the assassin was not seen for 12 years. Such omissions, intended or otherwise, form another important aspect of FA’s modus operandi. Uncertainty can be the impetus for investigation and its character. The meticulous written investigation reports produced by FA often acknowledge the presence of uncertainty, though aesthetically, its visual outcomes do not dwell in this register. There is a tension between the precise, authoritative, sonorous voices of the narrators of its completed presentations coupled with the clinically clear imagery it employs, and the concept of accepting the shadow of a doubt. The question of uncertainty floats across the public spaces in which the work of FA appears: museums, art galleries, courts of law. Each space affords different audience consideration of the material on display. The Killing of Mark Duggan investigation became a featured display within the wider exhibition War Inna Babylon: The Community’s Struggle for Truth and Rights at the ICA in London in 2021, where the vibrant history of Black activist organisations took centre stage, as well as testimonies from families whose loved ones had died in police custody. The inherent contradictions between varied forms of bearing witness, from the juridical to the artistic, were given breathing space in the public forum. The exhibition exposed the porous nature of the border between fact and proof, showing the much-needed potential for an expanded legal imagination. Though the legal appeal predating FA’s research was unsuccessful, every element of the ICA show felt like a step towards justice. Forensic Architecture collaborated with forensic archaeologists from the University of Staffordshire to conduct a non-invasive survey using ground-penetrating radar in Shark Island, Namibia, in order to corroborate the existence of graves at this location. From German Colonial Genocide in Namibia: Shark Island, Forensic Architecture, 2024. Colonial photographs crop indigenous peoples out of the wider territory of their ancestral homeland, fragmenting their life worlds. Forensic Architecture set out to return these images to their context, by reconstructing the contemporary landscape of Waterberg, Namibia, in a game engine. Image source: Koloniales Bildarchiv, Universitätsbibliothek Frankfurt/Main. From Restituting Evidence: Genocide and Reparations in German Colonial Namibia, Forensic Architecture, 2022. --> Recent FA investigations show the potential for a reading of images that shifts from the archival and art historical to the juridical, in its work on the German colonial genocide in Namibia. Working with both historical photographs and maps from this period, researcher-in-charge Agata Nguyen Chuong describes how the violence of the colonial archive was revealed, in a foreshadowing of the regime’s genocidal ways of seeing. “Landscape photography, framing a Lebensraum for German settlers – and often disseminated as postcards – or cartographic annotations on maps, betrayed genocidal intentions of erasure and land dispossession before they materialised on the ground. In this context, the notion of ‘photographic restitution’ – returning images to their environmental and cultural contexts – became central to our method.” Comparing contemporary and archival images enabled a close reading to understand, for example, a transformation in grass growth that, combined with other details of land degradation and dispossession, evidences a crime we might soon be able to name as ecocide; so often genocide’s bedfellow. What happens to the land happens to people too. An 1897 map shows the extent of settler farms and how a proposal to establish native reserves would contain Indigenous people and restrict their movement. The Namibian people, via German, then British, then South African regimes, suffer from the far-reaching consequences of such division today. Reading the contours of historical maps in conjunction with testimony from the elders of Otjimbingwe demonstrates how German colonial expansion was driven by the availability of water and grazing lands. Photographs from 1897, when combined with inherited testimony, enabled modelling of vegetation and trees, placing them back into the once richly biodiverse and fertile landscape. What had been wilfully misunderstood as a barren landscape, or a void – that erroneous lynchpin of colonial logic – is proven to be full of life. Says Nguyen Chuong: “In this investigation, inherited testimonies were vital – as an act of resistance against the completely erased settlement and a way of life. Through the research, oral histories enabled the images to yield truths that colonial depictions had tried to obscure. Inherited testimonies add resolution to the subtle visual cues in the photographs, transforming our reconstructed landscape into a lived environment. Through these methods we attempted to re-situate this fraught material within the ontologies colonial violence had sought to erase.” Great Mosque of al-Nuri, Mosul, Iraq. From the series Phantom Pro © Giles Price. --> FA’s research into Israel’s actions towards the Palestinian people charts an inescapably similar trajectory: strategic destruction of agricultural crops, the poisoning of wells and the destruction of olive trees – some of which are older than the State of Israel and are a central symbol of Palestinian identity, as well a source of income. Making these invisible processes of control and destruction visible evidences specific practices and policies which are replicated across centuries and geographies to the same end. Rather than as a caption, or an accompaniment to a portrait – important though these modes surely are – testimony is used here to expand the possibility of the image. This has consequences for the humanist model of the ‘concerned’ documentarian, whose empathetic imagery might lose its charge or at least its efficacy. The sharing of lived experience repositions its narrator from a potentially vulnerable role, as someone to whom something (terrible) happened, into the source of vital knowledge, thus creating an expanded way of seeing. Inherited testimony, a precise remembering of transgenerational knowledge in indigenous worldviews, also offers a challenge to the lesser status of testimony in law. Another intriguing aspect, especially when taken into consideration with the mnemonic qualities of photographs, is how these visual assemblages are created from memory, often traumatised memory. It is important to note that the process for engaging with these memories has been developed with Goldsmiths’ Forensic Psychology department, to avoid potential retraumatisation. Yet, at the same time, the unspoken acknowledgement of seeing the world through the eyes of those traumatised by colonial or state violence is in equal parts tender, shocking and profound. What would it mean to see the world, long-term and predominantly, from the perspective not of the victors but the survivors? What might be drawn from this kind of work is a significant shift in the role of the contemporary documentary photographer in relation to the concept of visual evidence. But what is the role of the individual practitioner in relation to this distinctively collective way of working? Further, what is the role of the conflict photographer in particular, already significantly changed in relation to technologies of war, since the ‘shock and awe’ tactics employed by the US in Iraq and the rise of asymmetric warfare. The image Azersjkhan Neighbourhood uses what Price calls “the expressive tactic of leaving visible the computational processes and photogrammetry software artefacts” within the image. On first sight, this image evokes spacecraft, hovering above the territory. It is hard to escape the thought that this is the position of invading armies of the future, surveilling for an occupation yet to come; continuing the rise and fall of empires across the centuries, phantoms of the air that cannot be put to rest. In uniting place, sight and voice, the previously disembodied art of mapping, which has so often served to divide and conquer, is imbued with a new potency. These imperfect assemblages reveal a shift from a photography that describes, or represents, to one that helps to explain, clarify, imagine and, sometimes, to prove.
