The exhibition “Japonismes” by photographer Alain Combier will be on display for two months in Paris, first at the Galerie de Bazillac, then at the Bibliothèque St. Simon A native of southern France, Alain Combier has always been interested in photography. Equipped with his first film camera, he traveled across the plains and mountains of his native region, drawing inspiration from the light of the Mediterranean landscapes and the rugged beauty of the Pyrenees. Very early on, he joined a local photographers’ association. His early black-and-white works were featured in his first Paris exhibition, titled “Alma Mater,” in 2025. His engineering studies and a career in industry took him, for more than forty years, from Paris to Montreal, then to Madrid and Tokyo. But throughout all those years, he never lost his passion for photography. Each of his trips and stays abroad provided an opportunity to enrich his portfolios, with a particular fondness for landscapes. Now based in Paris and with more free time, he continues to deepen the passion he’s had since his youth. This new exhibition allows him to present works drawn from his collection of photographs taken in Japan, a country where he lived from 2016 to 2019 and for which he has developed a genuine affection. His photographic work focuses on atmospheres rather than events. His perspective is marked by serenity and contemplation, attentive to details and moods rather than the spectacular. Through natural light, minimalist compositions, and fleeting moments, Alain Combier creates images that reflect the authenticity and poetry of the Land of the Rising Sun. **Jeremy Dennis : Photography as Memory and Reflection on Native Presence by Marie Audier D’Alessandris – Part 3**

Over the first two parts of our conversation, Jeremy Dennisintroduced the research-driven foundation of his practice through On This Site, before exploring Nothing Happened Here and the ways photography can address memory, visibility, and the lasting impact of history. Throughout those conversations, one idea returned consistently: creating empathy by encouraging viewers to reconsider their relationship to the places they inhabit. In this final installment, Dennis discusses one of his most ambitious bodies of work, Rise. We explore how he uses staged and digitally constructed imagery, references drawn from art history and popular culture, and carefully crafted symbolism to examine Indigenous presence, sovereignty, representation, and the stories that continue to shape the American landscape. The conversation concludes with a broader reflection on photography as a vehicle for education and, ultimately, for empathy. Let’s speak about Rise. Several images show Indigenous figures emerging into affluent or seemingly tranquil contemporary spaces. What kinds of power dynamics, anxieties, or role reversals are you asking viewers to confront in those scenes? The Rise Project is one of my favorites. I started it in 2018, and as you mentioned, it’s sometimes in affluent places and sometimes in nature. Whether it’s a self-portrait in the project or if it’s someone volunteering as a model, even if it’s not the Hamptons, it’s supposed to be framed that way. So it’s been photographed everywhere from Italy, residency called Civitella Ranieri Foundation, another place called Eyes on Main Street Residency in North Carolina, down to Florida, you have the southwest a lot of people volunteered as well at Santa Fe Art Institute, and it’s all about this idea of that confrontation or maybe the discomfort of acknowledging Native presence and I think it’s a lot of pop culture in the series as well. I’m excited about the Rise project because I’ve been doing it for seven years at this point, I would say, hard to believe. It started with pop culture, especially an article by Noam Chomsky, and most randomly, movies. And it’s about your fears, both in these fictional narratives and movies and in real life. This idea of an invading force that we have to make an enemy out of, and usually it’s a racial group, or it’s another nationality, and what’s revealed is that they’re the group that we’re actually oppressing. When we think about Native history…not only Native history, but the creation of America, and I’m thinking currently about the Declaration and how we’re described as “Indian savages,” a lot of that violence that is labeled on Native people comes from simply defending our land. The Rise project comes out of that simple premise of, “Why are we feared simply for defending our land?” But in the 21st century, the fear, in my opinion, stems from acknowledging our sovereignty, governance, and treaty rights. Things that we should be entitled to, but don’t have access to. So, the project really looks at as many different possibilities in terms of where do the Native people exist, where are they imagined, what scenarios could they be in? And it spans everything, from the foundation of the house. It could be your garden. It could be the spaces that you don’t look at in cities or in nature. And sometimes it looks like images of ghosts. Other times it looks like there’s a confrontation, but it draws on that popular imagination. It’s usually about fear. And sometimes it’s about, “What’s that noise behind me?” It’s usually a deer or a bird, or it could be a Native person. A big part of the takeaway, or the message, is that Native people are everywhere. You just don’t see, recognize, or acknowledge us. And sometimes, that’s an artist. I’m sitting in on panels and conferences, and art and culture spaces are always saying, “I want to work with Native people, but don’t know where they are”. It’s a lot of that, just trying to make the point that, on the contrary, you can find us everywhere. Going back to the Rise Project, the very first images were all about non-Native people, like being at a window and seeing Native people coming back to claim their ancestral territory. And it centers on a lot of that discomfort: who belongs, who’s trespassing? Part of it is because of where we live, here in the Hamptons, where real estate and housing are such an expensive commodity and investment. For Shinnecock people, we just have a very small sliver of our ancestral land, and we’re making it work. But with climate change and sea-level rise, we live on marshland, so much of it is threatened. One of the places we’re trying to regain is called the Shinnecock Hills. It’s just across the street. It’s very elevated, and it’s also where many of our sacred sites are, since they’re on hilltops. So we’re trying to navigate how to get that land back. When we first got federal recognition in 2010, after 30 years of petitioning to the federal government, we mostly did that whole struggle, that expensive struggle, to get that land back, but a lot of courts say that we can’t get it back because we’ve waited too long. It’s an ironic series of events! But the Rise project is, strangely enough, like a long circle of things that I’m trying to bring to people’s attention in terms of that urgency. And so it’s a lot of people volunteering their time, their image, and sometimes the props they’re bringing together. Another major contribution to all these images is that they often allow for their domestic space, their home, to be a backdrop. Maybe it’s a gesture of apology. It’s seeing the work that needs to be done, and for people who aren’t in government or who aren’t landowners in the Hamptons, it’s what they can do to help. The other part of that symbolism is the fact that landback is about accessing land that’s been privatized, so even if at the end of the day, the image is terrible or unusable, just that gesture of going to someone’s house and taking photos, and it gets almost like a performance art or an archive of that. And as I mentioned before, Nothing Happened Here is a very memorable series, and it’s all people who aren’t models, professional models, or anything. So it’s something that they have never done before. It sticks with their mind, and then they reverberate that story or that image. So it comes back to your connection again at the end of the day. Can you tell us about the role of manipulation in your work? There’s a lot of digital editing and staged scenarios, which are central to your process. How do you respond to viewers who question the truth of manipulated images, especially when you are dealing with histories of erasure and denial? A big question as well. I’ll go back to another series I didn’t mention, called Stories, which are creation stories about where Native people come from. I focused mostly on the Northeast. And there are stories for everything, from where animals come from, and why the landscape is the way it is. And it is sort of mythology and legend, so trying to depict that image is almost impossible, just taking a snapshot without editing. What I ended up doing early on was kind of a paper collage, but instead it was, of course, digital. I would take a powwow of dancers, cut them out of the powwow arena, reconfigure them into an epic scene, and tell that story. To me, that became the only way I could possibly make those images. Part of the justification is that later in my art career, I realized many Native artists have been depicting those stories, whether it’s through beadwork or painting, but they are just not being shown, especially in New York. I wanted to fill that gap by using any means necessary to create these images and bring to life those stories that have never been realized in an image. That carries over to the Rise Project too, because much of it deals with reckoning with the history of disruption, culture, and colonization for the Shinnecock people in 1640 when we first made contact with English colonists. So much of that time period is just European masters creating narrative painting scenes, and I think a lot about what Native art would look like if we hadn’t been disrupted during that time of trade and goods being exchanged. A lot of the work looks back at the past and pretends it’s part of the overarching narrative of art history. I look at photography the same way that a painter looks at a painting. I have an image in my head, before I even pick up the camera, and then I try to create that using different props and self-portraiture. If it demands a single volunteer and me 10 times, then I’m happy to have that process go out. In Rise, many self-portraits are duplicated. And I do that illusion because when I was an undergrad, the artist and photographer Anthony Goicolea was doing those childhood self-portraits. He would be at summer camp or a Boy Scout, and I thought that was such a powerful gesture. And in my project, I started doing that because in the 17th century, when the English had strongholds in the villages, they began making rules for us. One of the early rules was that you couldn’t have more than two or three Native men meet in private on their own, or else they would be punished and sent away because they were accused of creating a conspiracy of war. So, images that show multiple Natives, especially men, are rarer. I decided early on that many people expect Native people in Buckskin regalia to have bows and arrows and other weapons. There are only a couple of exceptions where that’s the case. I wanted to create that image, relating back to horror movies, where our mere presence is the scariest thing. So, for many, the fact that we’re still here is a scary realization. It might lead to them no longer being able to access their house, or having to move, or having to sit with sad stories of how they got their wealth, all these things. Sometimes I do give weapons or tools to the non-Native people in the images, but I keep thinking back at the idea that we’re unstoppable and hopefully we’ll always be here. With the state of the world and the violence over land, I think that’s always in question. But it all comes back to this idea of connection, pop culture, scary movies, land back. Representation is one of those things that I think about in the project all the time because it is myself: it’s like a Native dressed as a Native, or sometimes it’s like an Indian playing an Indian. It is playing with the idea of the Boston Tea Party, how the colonists dressed as Native people in a similar way. But I’m actually Native, so I don’t know what that translates into. One of the things that people will notice is if they’ve been to a Native American powwow, each regalia that’s being worn is so unique, but when you look at these images with multiple Native figures, they do look copy and paste, and I think for some people that I know, they’ll see the joke in it, and then for other people, they’re like, is that the same person or is it just maybe a primitive person? They never really got individualized. So it’s a little bit of nuance here. There’s always so much depth behind every single image. It’s impressive. Do you want to tell us a bit about your new work in Rise, and what’s coming, and your new projects as well? I just got back from two residencies, one in Italy, it’s called Civitella Ranieri Foundation. I was there for a month in early fall. Then I went right to Santa Fe, New Mexico, for the Santa Fe Art Institute Residency. I was mostly creating work by sort of commission and invitation to respond to the 250th anniversary of both the Declaration of Independence and the Founding, or the Independence of the U.S. At first, I was really questioning why they would invite us, since Shinnecock was pretty much neutral. We’re such a small nation. I think Long Island was under British control, so we didn’t really do anything. So hard to find any letters at all or any information about our involvement. I was sitting there wondering, what do I even do? But then I started thinking about it. The simple premise was a nation being built on top of a nation. That was one of the big key changes in how to see it. The other was the Treaty of Paris, which ended the war in the early 1780s. The British signed away everything east of the Mississippi to the 13 colonies, so the Shinnecock Nation is under that umbrella along with dozens of other tribal nations, if not hundreds. I think about how the founding of America and our presence is inconvenient, just because it puts into question the founding of the nation and those early agreements that the British had with us, and how, as I’m learning about it, we tend to think of Americans and the British as totally different nations, but it was just the subjects of the king. So it was like this infighting, brothers fighting brothers, as it was described. For some reason, because of that separation, a lot of the agreements with Native people were just completely ignored or removed. And then the other was how Native people fought on both sides of the war. When it comes to celebrating the 4th of July, it’s very easy for us to feel discomfort and not patriotic when we see the American flag. And then there are so many cannons and muskets. A lot of those were also weaponized against Native people. So that’s also something we don’t really feel pride in. The other funny thing is that since 2016, Shinnecock has been invited to have a float in the parade. Usually we have regalia, we’re just waving, and we have the drum, but now we’re using it as a protest vehicle. I don’t know if they’re still doing it, but currently, I think we’re protesting the beach access and that we still have to pay to go to the beach. There’s this snake in this propaganda poster, but instead of just being on a blank background, it’s inside a Native wigwam, our traditional housing. We were just told to die pretty much. It’s that discomfort of how it was that people fought and risked their lives for something glorious, but I think the glory really wasn’t there. It was more about the land, which was so valuable. That was the big part of it. It pulls from pop culture again and reframes it. One of the things I hope people who will see the new work won’t see is just that we’re complaining, or maybe it’s history that’s so old, 250 years, that’s no longer relevant. I think when you have this commemoration every 10 or 50 years – I did a little bit of research on other Native artists who were invited to do this, there’s not that much artwork, as it’s really hard to think about why I would incorporate the American flag into my portfolio. So when I did the project, of course, I started with the declaration, and it does seem like I didn’t pay attention in history class, because a couple of months ago I realized there were like 27 grievances; I didn’t even know that. I went through all 27, some of them are very similar, but a lot of the irony is that the way tribal nations were treated was exactly the same way that they were blaming Britain. Like the British enacting a government in the American colonies, which was done to Native people, or imposing fines and taxes that were also imposed on Native people. All these different things that happened to us. As a conclusion, because there’s so much to be said about your work, and it almost feels like your images, similarly to the way you’ve done the recognition of the different sites and how it led into learning about the history of the location, why it’s important, and it opened your eyes. We’ve spoken together about using photography to examine both personal identity and your community, collective identity. Which misunderstandings or stereotypes about Indigenous identity do you most hope your work will challenge? I think the conclusion that all Native people and tribal communities always come up with is that we’re still here. That saying that has been going on for new generations is this idea of no matter where you are in the country, Native people are in your backyard, your neighbors, coworkers, things like that. We’re Still Here is actually one of the titles of a local historian’s book. The author’s name is John Strong. I think the book was published in the 1980s, and it is also a survey of different tribal leaders, health center staff, political leaders, and people running small businesses. It is a great overview of about 100 people from the Unkechaug, who are in Mastic, and the Shinnecock people here in Southampton. Those are the two out of 13 tribal communities here on Long Island that still have a land base. You can just go drive up to them and say hello, support their businesses, and meet people. When it comes to the education part, one of the other realizations I had early on in my work, especially when I was working on creation stories, is that even the source material for that work had to be rewritten and filtered through a Native lens. You would see a lot of stories that are titled or mention things like angels, wizards, red men, or other derogatory terms. A Native person may have told the story, but then it was a missionary or someone who was like an anthropologist writing it down or remembering it and then rephrasing it. I have done many artist talks and heard people’s feedback and questions. I think people really do need to be guided when it comes to the interpretation of Native art. So, every single time I create an image, and hopefully on display, each image always has a text or explanation or artist statement. And I try to use the best terminology, to capitalize the right words, and to create as much context as possible, because you can’t really trust the viewer who is non-Native to understand a culture they never really engage with. We all grew up with the same public school education system, and in my mid-20s, I had to sit down for years to relearn much of it. Many people don’t have that time, so it’s just a quick way to give people a glimpse or a new message they might take away. Sometimes you do face people who have such a cemented view of Native people. Sometimes they have met one Native person 10 or 20 years ago, and that’s their only impression, and they want to retain that nostalgia and not even look at the text or read it. I’ve seen that too. A lot of the work I do, whether you see it in a museum or a gallery, usually has so much more that goes into it than the one or two paragraphs. If there was one last word, one emotion you want to leave us with, what would it be? That’s a tough one. Empathy. I’ll leave it at that. Marie Audier D’Alessandris

**Heide Museum of Modern Art : Brassaï : Secret Paris**

“I invent nothing. I imagine everything. I have sought only to express reality, for nothing is more surreal” – Brassaï. Walk with me through a foggy Parisian night. Pause. Watch the mist fall in gentle swirls wrapping the city in gossamer folds. Feel the damp upon your skin. Wander the streets in the liminal space between dusk and dawn. Meander along the banks of the River Seine lulled by the slapping of water on the shore, the flutter of falling leaves. Linger between dream and reality. Play in the light and shade. Look in ways you have not seen before. Wonder at the extraordinary of the quotidian. Pay homage to lives now long passed. The echo of a memory, revealing itself anew. One of the most alluring aspects of the photograph is its capacity to transport the viewer to another world, to take us beyond what we know or to remind us of what we have forgotten. Nearly a century after Brassaï’s Secret Paris photographs were taken, they continue to fascinate. Compelling, rich with narrative and exuding the humanist qualities that underpinned this artist’s work, Brassaï’s photographs are undoubtedly some of the most arresting pictures of all time. Entering the Brassaï’s Secret Paris exhibition at the Heide Museum of Modern Art (Melbourne) is to arrive at another time and place. Brassaï’s deep, emotional connection to the images he created, first in his mind and then on glass plates and as prints, are enthralling and profound. Each represents a moment, a life suspended in time, which for me encapsulates the enduring value of the medium. Yet these photographs transcend the notion of documentation, conveying a level of intimacy that is possible because of Brassaï’s ability to reach beyond the superficial and take us with him into his world. To say I love Brassaï’s Secret Paris is to underplay the connection I feel to the ‘luminous years’ in which this polymath found his way to photography allowing generations to experience one of the world’s greatest cities in all its guises through his unique way of looking. To know Brassaï’s photography is to understand the difference between seeing and looking, and to meet in that place where intellect and imagination collide with reality. Brassaï’s photography forces you to give yourself up to what’s happening in the image and unprompted, let it do its magic. I have long admired Brassaï’s work, read his letters to his parents during the early years when he was struggling in Paris and spent time with his images. The Heide show is the first time I have stood amongst the vintage prints that Brassaï made in his “laboratoire” (darkroom) and it is a spine-tingling moment. In 1899 Gyula Halász was born in the small Hungarian Transylvania town of Brassó. After studying fine arts in Budapest and Berlin, and serving in the military, in 1924 at the age of 25, he moved to Paris where he spent the rest of his life. It was at this time he adopted the name Brassaï. An artist who used a range of materials to convey his ideas, from writing and sculpture to photography, Brassaï is best known for the latter, a medium he embraced to express his insatiable curiosity about the human condition. However, being a polymath comes with its own preoccupations and Brassaï longed for artistic freedom, writing that he would have liked to model himself in “the tradition of the seventeenth-century humanists, that is, those great figures of the Renaissance who were granted the right to develop, in harmony, all their talents in life and in the arts including thinking.” Brassaï lamented that “specialization, one of the first requirements – and calamities – of our modern era, has always been odious to me.” Nevertheless, for a good part of his adult life, Brassaï chose photography as his means of artistic expression, later returning to writing and sculpture. Where he suspends the notion of “specialization” is in his subject matter. Through his photographs we can see that Brassaï was captivated with life and its array of forms. At the time Brassaï came to photography, Paris was a thrumming hub of artistic experimentation. Foreign artists congregated in low rent arrondissements, to spend their days nursing cups of black coffee in cafes, debating and exploring new ideas, theories, artistic methods and the existential questions of the time. This is a period romanticised by history, when the truth is that many struggled to survive, Brassaï included. The letters he wrote to his parents during this time evidence that trying to live a life true to oneself was tough. As was the case with many aspiring artists (and remains true), Brassaï earned his living turning his hand to all manner of tasks, from reporting on the Olympic games to being a ghost writer, and caricaturist. From the moment he picked up a borrowed camera in 1929, he fell in love with the medium claiming he “saw photography as a way to uncover and record the world that surrounded me, the city in which I live, as comprehensively as possible.” Early on he took pictures to accompany the articles he wrote for Hungarian and German magazines, journalism being his main source of income. But he quickly tired of photography’s “practical consideration.” Writing in the preface to Brassaï: Letters to my Parents, he notes “As soon as I learned to use the camera, I lost interest in having my pictures published as illustrations for commissioned articles. From the moment I realized that the camera was capable of capturing the beauty of the Parisian night (that beauty with which I had fallen passionately in love during my bohemian adventures), I pursued photography solely for my own enjoyment.” A sentiment that resonates with this author. In those early years, Brassaï spent his nights “wandering about the city from Montparnasse to Montmartre…inspired to become a photographer by my desire to translate all the things that enchanted me in the nocturnal Paris I was experiencing.” Often these night tours were made alone, although on occasion the writer Henry Miller joined him, Miller labelling Brassaï “the eye of Paris.” Many of the photographs that feature in this exhibition were published in Brassaï’s first monograph Paris de Nuit in 1933, a turning point in his photographic career. In the gallery are the vintage prints handmade by Brassaï as well as life-sized panels suspended from the ceiling, weighted down with chain and stone. The staggered hanging of these panels allows for a full immersion into the streets of Paris, the way Brassaï saw them. Moving back to adjust my gaze so I can see the panels align, allows an extraordinary view of the steps on which the little white dog stood, and to see the lights of Paris reflect in the River Seine through the arch of the Carrousel Bridge. To immerse in Brassaï’s Paris is a joy. These large panels also reveal what Lesley Harding, the artistic director at Heide and a curator of the show, describes as “the artist’s hand,” elements that are imperceptible in the original prints. With images at such scale, we can see “where he’s marked up the negatives…the literal hand of the artist is present.” Seeing these marks on the prints gives further insight into Brassaï’s practice, his attention to detail, and are evidence of the meticulous work he did in his “laboratoire” exemplifying his intention to tell a story beyond documentation. It is fascinating to trace the handwork of Brassaï in the blown- up prints and then to look at the originals to see how the work he did in the darkroom presents in the final print. “I like to turn a new page in my life with every day” Brassaï’ wrote, his innate curiosity evident in the breadth of subjects he photographed, from the fabulous La Môme Bijou au Bar de la Lune, Montmartre(c. 1932), one of my favourite portraits, to the Little White Dog, on the steps of Montmartre (1932). Architecture also drew his eye. The photographs of the gargoyles of Notre Dame keeping watch over Paris at night are haunting. Then there are the street walkers and brothel workers, the backstage antics at the Folies Bergère, lovers in cafes and children sailing boats on the Seine. Wherever he turned his gaze, Brassaï found moments that he crafted into compelling and enduring human narratives. His love of humanity was fundamental to his artistic practice, says Philippe Ribeyrolles, his nephew and godson, who was in Melbourne for the opening of Brassaï ’s Secret Paris at Heide. Philippe grew up around Brassaï the youngster spending time in his uncle’s “laboratoire.” Brassaï had no children of his own and welcomed his nephew into his world. I ask if Brassaï’s photography shifted over time. No, explains Philippe, it was not the subject matter that changed, but rather the medium, Brassaï returning to his love of writing and sculpture in his later years. “Touch became an important part of his practice too,” says Philippe. “Feel, don’t look.” In Brassaï’s photography you can see his interest in sculpture also. In his series Transmutations, a collection of nudes, he explores form, lines, and contours, the sculptural aesthetics obvious in these abstract compositions. As we chat at Heide, Philippe explains that Brassaï did not direct or instruct those he photographed but allowed the scene to unfold, both in his mind and in front of him. When he felt the time was right, then he pressed the shutter, a point at which the subject had forgotten Brassaï was there to take a picture. Despite the size of the cameras at that time, Brassaï ’s engagement with his subjects, his genuine interest in their stories, allowed any preoccupation with posing to fall away. It was at this moment he made his picture. Brassaï ’s work is without guile or judgement, says Philippe. “My uncle photographed people and situations not only as he saw them, but as he emotionally perceived them.” Brassaï was a lover of humankind, someone who was interested in others’ stories, who felt deeply and used photography as a means of exploring his own emotionality. “He was a humanist,” says Philippe, “and an intellectual” deeply interested in the history of art, someone who read constantly and was inspired by the works of Proust and Goethe amongst others. Brassaï’s knowledge, and appreciation for art is reflected in the portrait of Dali and Gala he made in 1933 at Villa Seurat, in Paris. Philippe tells that Brassaï knew that Dali loved Velázquez, so he drew on the composition of Las Meninas, painted in 1656, to frame his portrait of the pair. In Las Meninas the artist can be seen in a mirror in the background standing at his easel, and in the photograph taken of Dali and Gala, Brassaï is reflected in the mirror with his camera, an intentional structuring of the scene. Mirrors appear frequently in Brassaï’s work, acting not only in a reflective capacity, but as a means of challenging perspective, both the artist’s and viewers. The mirror becomes a vehicle through which Brassaï shares what is in his imagination, his mind’s eye. For instance, in Philippe’s favourite piece, the image of the Folies Bergère backstage taken in 1932, a large mirror dominates in its obscurity. It is perhaps one of the most complex images, as it requires the viewer to pause for a long time and contemplate exactly what they are looking at, such is the intricacy of the scene, the angle from which it is shot (it looks like it was taken from the fly floor above the stage), and the enormous mirror that reflects a tableau at odds with reality. I can see why the image intrigues Philippe, but he tells that when he was first given the print as a child for Christmas, at the age of around eight, he was less than enthused. “A picture, great. Not a game?” he laughs. The exhibition features several portraits of famous artists – Dali, Matisse, Maar, Miller, Giacometti – all of whom Brassaï became acquainted. However, portraits of Picasso dominate in this exalted group, and provide rare, and intimate insights into this artist’s life. The pair forged a lifelong friendship which began when they met during a photography commission Brassaï received from the groundbreaking Surrealist magazine Minotaure for its inaugural edition in 1932. Brassaï photographed Picasso in his studio along with various artworks, the latter at the time one of the world’s most revered artists, the former in the nascent years of his career. Over decades Brassaï photographed Picasso and in 1964 he published Conversations with Picasso an extraordinary book that documents his many wide-ranging discussions with the artist. The grouping of Brassaï’s images of Picasso allows a closer reading of the famous artist’s life and practices and his relationship with the photographer. The exhibition also features viewing apertures that line the walls in a separate room affording a different type of engagement with the pictures. Sitting on a small stool, one can view an image without distraction from the periphery, which acts to slow down the act of seeing so that you can really look at what is depicted without external visual noise. Brassaï comes to the Heide Museum of Modern Art following the success of recent headline shows, notably Lee Miller and the double bill, Man Ray and Max Dupain. Brassaï seemed a logical next step as Lesley Harding explains. Bringing Brassaï back to Melbourne after more than 40 years is influenced by the fact that there is “a real appetite” amongst audiences for modernist photography and there are new generations to introduce to his work. The familiarity of the landmarks in the City of Light, many of which still exist today, is also a drawcard for audiences. In 1953, Jean Gallien wrote in Photo-Monde that Brassaï ’s photography paralleled the emotional power of literature such was his capacity to tell a compelling story, one that resonated and stayed in the audience’s mind. “Humanism in photography is both an intellectual as well as an emotional endeavour,” wrote Bill Jay in The Romantic Machine in 1978. These qualities are clearly present in Brassaï Secret Paris the most expansive survey of his photography shown in Australia. **The Eclipse Was Real, But Did We See it? – A One Shot Story**

Where were you when the eclipse happened? On 12 August 2026, at 7 p.m., I was on the bridge at Finchley Central Station. The sun was low over London, and people had stopped to look at it. They stood on the high bridge, facing the sun, waiting for the eclipse to begin and then for the light to change, many of them holding their phones towards the sky. The evening was very hot; you can almost feel the heat of the day through the photograph. I had not prepared my camera to photograph or video the eclipse itself, and I did not want to trust my eyes to a pair of cheap eclipse glasses. Instead, I found another way of looking at the event and raised my camera towards the watchers. A station is normally about movement: arriving, departing, catching the train, getting somewhere. And then suddenly people stop. The bridge became an observatory. They were not tourists who had come specifically for an astronomical event. Even some of the station staff, whose usual task was to keep people moving, stopped to watch. For a few minutes, the familiar progression from daylight to evening was interrupted. The sun was still there, but the light became dimmer, and then bright again. There was something instinctive about the scene. These thoroughly modern people, with their smartphones raised towards the sky, were doing something much older: turning towards the sun when something unusual happened to it. We rarely look directly at the sun, we just see what its light reveals. Perhaps we, human beings, are often better at recognising light than seeing where it comes from. When the peak of the eclipse had passed, and I walked towards the platform, I wondered exactly what I had photographed. The people on the bridge were recording the eclipse. I was recording them. The eclipse itself was outside the frame. So were the images and videos people had captured on their phones. What remained were the people, the phones in their hands, the sunlight on their bodies, and the sense that something was happening just beyond the picture. They saw the eclipse through their screens; I was looking at them through my Panasonic Lumix G9 and Olympus Zuiko 12–40 mm f/2.8. I was not outside the phenomenon I was photographing; I was part of it. So, where were you when the eclipse happened? For about 20 minutes, I was on a bridge at Finchley Central, in a summer of heatwaves and no rain in London, under Amber Weather Alerts, watching people watch the sun. Perhaps the photograph can tell us one thing with certainty: we were there, but did we see it? The photograph: Finchley Central Station, London, 12 August 2026, during the eclipse.
