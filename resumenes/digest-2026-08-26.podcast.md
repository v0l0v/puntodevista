Fotógrafo: Björn Persson

All images © Björn Persson, courtesy of Rizzoli, shared with permission Björn Persson's New Book Highlights the World's Incredible and Endangered Wildlife August 25, 2026 Books Nature Photography Kate Mothes Share Pin Email Copy Bookmark From the Tanzanian grasslands around Mount Kilimanjaro to the ever-evolving icy realm of Antarctica and beyond, Swedish photographer Björn Persson captures the majesty of the natural world and its wild denizens. His bold, tonal style of portraiture taps into the drama of running giraffes and a stoic American bison in profile, but he also homes in on family-centric moments as a lioness plays with her cubs, for instance, or a coalition of cheetahs perch calmly atop a knoll. Persson's emotive photographs meander between intimate, tender vignettes to striking and powerful demonstrations of brute strength and presence. In many cases, the animals look directly at the viewer, as if we're being stalked. Others look away, scanning the grasses for prey or for other members of their cohort. Dozens of Persson's photos fill the pages of The Earth We Share: Extraordinary Photography of Our Vanishing Species, forthcoming from Rizzoli. The volume also includes a preface by the late Dr. Jane Goodall , who completed the writing shortly before she died last year. Indeed, Persson's goal is to encourage care, spurring conservation efforts and portraying wildlife through a lens of hope and possibility rather than discouragement. With a reverent eye, Persson focuses specifically on endangered creatures, which face dwindling populations due to poaching, habitat loss, and the effects of the climate crisis. He believes that by portraying animals as sentient beings, he can inspire people to acknowledge the intrinsic beauty and worth of these extraordinary creatures, says a statement. The book is an extension of this vision, with the aim of inspiring not only awe but action. The Earth We Share is slated for release on September 8. **The Eclipse Was Real, But Did We See it? – A One Shot Story**

Where were you when the eclipse happened? On 12 August 2026, at 7 p.m., I was on the bridge at Finchley Central Station. The sun was low over London, and people had stopped to look at it. They stood on the high bridge, facing the sun, waiting for the eclipse to begin and then for the light to change, many of them holding their phones towards the sky. The evening was very hot; you can almost feel the heat of the day through the photograph. I had not prepared my camera to photograph or video the eclipse itself, and I did not want to trust my eyes to a pair of cheap eclipse glasses. Instead, I found another way of looking at the event and raised my camera towards the watchers. A station is normally about movement: arriving, departing, catching the train, getting somewhere. And then suddenly people stop. The bridge became an observatory. They were not tourists who had come specifically for an astronomical event. Even some of the station staff, whose usual task was to keep people moving, stopped to watch. For a few minutes, the familiar progression from daylight to evening was interrupted. The sun was still there, but the light became dimmer, and then bright again. There was something instinctive about the scene. These thoroughly modern people, with their smartphones raised towards the sky, were doing something much older: turning towards the sun when something unusual happened to it. We rarely look directly at the sun, we just see what its light reveals. Perhaps we, human beings, are often better at recognising light than seeing where it comes from. When the peak of the eclipse had passed, and I walked towards the platform, I wondered exactly what I had photographed. The people on the bridge were recording the eclipse. I was recording them. The eclipse itself was outside the frame. So were the images and videos people had captured on their phones. What remained were the people, the phones in their hands, the sunlight on their bodies, and the sense that something was happening just beyond the picture. They saw the eclipse through their screens; I was looking at them through my Panasonic Lumix G9 and Olympus Zuiko 12–40 mm f/2.8. I was not outside the phenomenon I was photographing; I was part of it. So, where were you when the eclipse happened? For about 20 minutes, I was on a bridge at Finchley Central, in a summer of heatwaves and no rain in London, under Amber Weather Alerts, watching people watch the sun. Perhaps the photograph can tell us one thing with certainty: we were there, but did we see it? The photograph: Finchley Central Station, London, 12 August 2026, during the eclipse. **Kodak No 2 Bulls-eye, model D  – Amazing Results with an 1898 Camera**

A recent comment from a 35mmc reader about my Leica 0 included “I look forward to more postings from you with other ancient cameras!” I thought it was a fun idea and decided to start with my 1898 Kodak No 2, model D. That should be ancient enough. If you look this camera up, it is easily confused with the similar but later Kodak #2 Brownie Model D camera that uses 120 film. The Bulls-eye No. 2 camera, introduced in 1892 as the first roll film camera with a red window was produced through 1913. The “ Historic Camera ” site stated it was constructed of wood covered in seal grain leather and furnished with nickel hardware. The site’s lens information is only that it is a meniscus achromatic lens with fixed focus and a set of three stops. Some sources indicate the widest aperture is f/16. The Eastman rotary shutter is a single speed that is approximately 1/25 second. Image size on 101 film was 3 ½ X 3 ½ inches but using 120 film, the image size becomes 2 ½ X 3 ½ inches. The 120 roll yields seven pictures, instead of the original eight. I bought this Kodak Bullseye camera from the granddaughter of the original owner. It had been stored in the attic of a funeral home for many years. The included film roll was ruined but the camera is still in perfect mechanical condition so I got 3D printed spool adaptors to use 120 film to replace the unavailable 101 format film. I calculated how many turns to take on the winding knob after loading and between frames as I had to tape over the red window to keep it light tight with the smaller width film. The viewfinder was completely useless with a totally fogged mirror so I used Adhesive Acrylic Craft Mirror material that I cut to size with scissors and stuck over the useless original mirror. Now it is bright and clear. I dug around a lot and concluded that this camera was likely built in 1898. I remembered that an abandoned church in nearby Griffin, Georgia was also completed that year. I had shot it before with various cameras and decided it would be appropriate to shoot it with my Kodak No. 2, Model D Bullseye box camera. The following church photos were shot on Ilford Pan F 50 film. I took this camera on other outings using different films and hope you will enjoy some of these. Catlabs Pro 320 film (fumbled the loading and got some light leaks that added to the charm of the next two) Kodak Tri-X 400 film. The meniscus lens gives characteristic softness to the frame edges seen in the trees above and below. Holga 400 film (Fomapan 400) Kodak Gold 200. Extreme color abounds with these toy tractors and the ancient, uncoated lens does a great job. Here is a fine one to end this story. I hope to dig around and bring out another oldie soon.
