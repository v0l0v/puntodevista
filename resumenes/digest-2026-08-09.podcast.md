**In Depth: A Grand Tour of Travel Photography**
Fotógrafos: angelinatacinelli

You remember that feeling of the last day before summer vacation, sitting in your last class of the day, the entire student body buzzing? When half of your peers hadn't bothered to show up, and the other half count down the minutes, no seconds, until the clock strikes summer – that moment you've been working towards all year? Now, let’s go back a few hundred years, to a world where apparently only men can get an education, everyone smelled a lot worse, and paintings were the only way to see the world around you. The grandiose Grand Tour was taken by young men (perhaps the sixth Alexander in their family) across Europe, as a way to experience the whole wide world (a.k.a. homogeneous European countries), and live the dolce vita lifestyle. For this historical piece, we will go into the early history of travel photography, from inspirations in painting to the use of portable cameras. From sipping on a glass of vin in France to “discovering” Greek ruins, we will traverse through space and time, with, of course, a little Lomographic flair. You know the phrase what happens on vacation, stays on vacation? We will see how true that is. Left: Portrait of a Young Man. Pompeo Batoni, ca. 1760–65. Oil on canvas. The Metropolitan Museum of Art, Object Number 52.63.1 ## Setting the Scene Imagine yourself as an English chap. The year is 1780. Day after day, you sit in an English boarding school, or in your oversized estate with a private tutor, learning about philosophy, science, and maybe a bit of Economics, as Adam Smith has just published The Wealth of Nations a few years prior. Bored by the fact that your biggest problem is that you have to take over the estate once your father is no longer walking the sweet green grass of this Earth, you decide to set off on a journey so unbelievable, you will brag about it for years to come. Yes, while any other person in the working class had to work all month to earn a fraction of what you spend in a day, you decide to jet off on an all-expenses-paid trip across the English Channel, through France, Italy, and with the final destination of Greece. The journey excites and delights you, and the prospect of being free from the chains of aristocratic life is truly tantalizing. This is the Grand Tour, the beginning of travel as we know it today. Image: Italian Picture Dealers Humbugging My Lord Anglaise. Thomas Rowlandson, May 30, 1812. Hand-colored etching. The Metropolitan Museum of Art, Object Number 59.533.1221 ## Painting on the Road Until this point, the only way to see the world was through paintings. Artists such as Canaletto and Bellotto created masterful landscapes of cities, such as Dresden, Vienna, and Venice. Some of these paintings make their way back into England, as King George III had begun amassing a large collection of them. Photography did not exist, so you could either patronize a painting of a city that lives in your dreams, or see it for yourself. Most of those who embarked on this Grand Tour were young, aristocratic lads between the ages of 18 and 25. Free from the borders of their hometowns, and, I guess, stuffy society, it was the first time they could let loose and drink all the wine they wanted. Think Colin Bridgerton, from the Bridgerton series on Netflix. What they journey to find is something beyond the paintings, beyond the whispers of the languages that others have experienced. They want to find a new version of themselves, have lots of sex without consequences, and find a way to capture it. Paul Getty Museum, Los Angeles, Object Number 95.GG.24 ## Across the English Channel We Go The first stop for many was France after a voyage across the English Channel, into a port city such as Calais or Le Havre. Once there, the men hired a French-speaking guide and secured a private carriage to Paris. There, they spent months experiencing the city and mingling with French girls. British tourists often complained about 18th-century French food, the lack of comfort during their inn stays, and the extremely pleasant sewage that dotted the streets of Paris. However, for any young aristocrat hoping to go into government or diplomacy, it was a necessary step in refining their tastes and learning the language of romance and unpronounced letters, French. The Metropolitan Museum of Art, Object Number 1988.162 ## An Italian Passegiatta Next up on this grand escapade for most of our partiers, oh wait, I mean serious, distinguished travelers, was Italy. Along with their trusty carriage driver and steed who most certainly deserved a break, they would spend months, and sometimes years, exploring cities such as Venice, Rome, and Florence. They entered Italy via the Alps, and crossed the Brenner or Mont Cenis passes. From walking through the Roman ruins, to admiring Renaissance art, these itineraries formed the very early cornerstones of what drives travel today. They commissioned portraits by local artists, such as Pompeo Batoni, and collected vedute, or cityscape paintings, as souvenirs. ## Galivanting on a Greek Adventure Many Grand Tours took the most adventurous turn in Greece, where travelers explored rugged terrain to see legendary sites such as Mycenae, Epidaurus, and the Olympic stadium in Olympia. Of course, their feet also stepped onto the Acropolis and the Parthenon, as they were inspired by the thousands of years of democracy and philosophy around them. During this era of an I saw it first culture in a pre-photography world, the cost of these trips was a small price to pay to study the works of Michelangelo or sit and sketch the ancient ruins in Greece. This was the earliest form of traveling for leisure, and the only thing missing? A way to capture it almost instantly. Left: Santa Lucia, Naples. Calvert Richard Jones, 1845–46. The Metropolitan Museum of Art, Object Number 2005.100.562 ## Early Travel Photography Forms of documentation changed when photography hit the scene a few decades later in 1839. No, instead of commissioning portraits and supporting local artists, now, these early travelers could capture their own awakenings with a camera. Two photography processes were announced in 1839 that completely changed travel (and the world) as we knew it: the daguerreotype and the photogenic drawing. In the beginning, photography was not the most practical; the photographs took a while, and the cameras were large and not very portable. Professional bookshops and printers set up shops in major European cities that created photographic souvenirs and postcards. Later processes like albumen printing, while producing much better results, still demanded a fresh supply of eggs at all times for the coating. They were like a travelers’ own set of infinity stones, and many arranged them into collections for further study. The industrial revolution, an era when travel was made easier through railroads and steamships, was also the time photography was developed commercially. The railways made travel more accessible for everyone, and new ways of capturing the world through travel photography with portable cameras were abound. Images: Postcard Early 20th Century Public Domain, Wikimedia Commons ,  Postcard of Piran early 20th cent Public Domain, Wikimedia Commons Photographic souvenirs were a practical alternative if the English Brads and Chads couldn’t manage the equipment themselves. They were ready-made photos of key locations that influenced, and still do today, which locations were “important” enough to share. With new inventions, they could create their own photographs, and portable cameras such as the Kodak No. 1 and Folding Pocket Kodak changed the way these aristocratic fellows saw the world. Image: Kodak Folding Pocket N1 Public Domain, Wikimedia Commons ## A Note on Coloniality All of this being said, many European aristocrats left these places messier than they found them. While many back home would respect their accounts as gentlemens’ guides to ancient history, in many ways, the Grand Tour served as an extension of imperial domination and Eurocentricism. Without getting too much into it, it is important to note that much of what we learn from this era is based on romanticized, and often appropriated, narratives. Photography is a means to re-interpret historical archives. By re-examining travel and colonial photographic archives, we can critically view this time and re-center narratives that have been erased. As always, photography is a powerful artistic tool that can change how we think about history. ## An Outro to this Escapade I encourage you to go out into the world inspired by the passion to discover and learn about those who walk in different shoes.
