**The Places We Call Home: Ala Kheir on the Sudan Family Archives**
Fotógrafos: lynne57

Home is as much a feeling as a place, and one photograph can bring back memories that may often feel far away. Working with collections of family photography from his home country of Sudan, photographer Ala Kheir began the Sudan Family Archives project, which seeks to protect and preserve home, heritage, and humanity for present and future generations. Family photography archives have been key to connecting with Sudan's visual culture and history, as well as forging new relationships and legacies in uncertain times. Ala spoke to Lomography Magazine about the value of these everyday visuals and how family knowledge lays the foundation for collective memory. © From the archive of Elsharif Aboud Hi, Ala, welcome to Lomography Magazine! Could you please introduce yourself to our readers and tell us how your journey with photography began? My name is Ala Kheir. I am a Sudanese photographer, filmmaker, and educator. I have been using photography as a way to tell stories about people, identity, and the places we call home. Alongside my personal work, I founded The Other Vision (TOV), a platform that mentors emerging Sudanese photographers and supports visual storytelling across Sudan and the diaspora. My journey with photography began during my final year at university. I was studying engineering, but to be honest, I had grown a little bored with it and wanted to explore something completely different. My roommate had a camera, so I started borrowing it and taking photographs. At the time, my interest was purely technical. I simply wanted to learn how to make a beautiful image. I spent countless hours practicing, experimenting, and teaching myself through online tutorials and photography forums. What started as simple curiosity soon became a passion, and that was the beginning of a 21-year journey driven by a constant desire to learn. Photography has taken me to places I never imagined, introduced me to incredible people, and completely changed the direction of my life. Even today, I am still learning something new every time I pick up a camera. © From the archive of Elsharif Aboud We were pleased to learn that the Lomography Community has been a source of inspiration for you. What has been inspiring and motivating your personal photography work recently? The Lomography Community has always been inspiring because it celebrates curiosity and personal vision rather than perfection. It reminds us that photography is about exploring, experimenting, and enjoying the process, and I think that's something every photographer should hold on to. Recently, my biggest source of inspiration has been much closer to home. Over the past few years, Sudan has gone through a devastating war that displaced millions of people, including myself. As a photographer, I found myself documenting not only the experiences of others but also my own journey through displacement. That experience changed the way I think about photography. “...what keeps me motivated is still the belief that photography can help us better understand one another, and in that might be the solution to most issues that humanity is facing now.” Today, I am less interested in creating a technically perfect image and more interested in making photographs that carry emotion, memory, and a sense of humanity. And what keeps me motivated is still the belief that photography can help us better understand one another, and in that might be the solution to most issues that humanity is facing now. © From the archive of Elsharif Aboud Your project, Sudan Family Archives, explores collections from three different Sudanese families throughout the 20th century. How did you first come across these photographers’ material and how did the archival project itself take shape? Part of learning photography is learning about the history of photography. Naturally, I became curious about the history of photography in Sudan. But when I started looking, almost everything I found was either colonial photography or official documentation. There was very little that reflected how ordinary Sudanese people lived their everyday lives. I came across the work of Abbas Habiballah online, and I already knew of Jadallah Jubara as a pioneering Sudanese filmmaker and photographer. Their work made me realize that there had to be many more untold stories waiting to be discovered. Through Elsharif Aboud's son, I was introduced to his father's incredible collection of photographs. Seeing those images was a turning point. They showed a Sudan that felt intimate, joyful, and deeply human, far removed from the formal or colonial images I had been finding. From that moment, I became obsessed with searching for more family archives and meeting the people who had preserved them. One archive led to another, and before long, the project had taken on a life of its own. © From the archive of Elsharif Aboud What began as personal research into the history of Sudanese photography gradually evolved into Sudan Family Archives, a project that is still behind the curtains, aiming to celebrate the everyday lives of Sudanese families and preserve an important part of our visual heritage. “Working with archives is also a way of challenging how Sudan has often been represented, and I think those photographs are just as important as the information found in history books.” You have been described as an ‘archival activist’. What does this mean in your own words, and why do you think engaging with archival or historical photography is a valuable task in the present day? I have never really thought of myself as an “archival activist.” For me, it is less about activism and more about making sure these photographs are seen, appreciated, and preserved before they disappear. Working with archives is also a way of challenging how Sudan has often been represented, and I think those photographs are just as important as the information found in history books. © From the archive of Elsharif Aboud The project focuses strongly on family, and the importance of the family in preserving national history. What role do relationships play in your archival process, from research and preservation, to exhibition and public response? Culturally, Sudanese people are very family-oriented, and it is quite common for old family photographs to be kept at a grandparent's home. In many ways, the family archive already exists. It's where generations gather every weekend, and sometimes revisit old albums. As Sudanese, we often don't realize how valuable these collections are because they have always been part of our everyday lives. It is only when we sit down and look through those albums that we begin to appreciate the importance of family, the memories we have shared, and how quickly time passes. One of the most rewarding parts of exhibiting the project is seeing how people respond. Visitors often recognize places, clothing, hairstyles, or traditions that remind them of their own families. Even people who have never been to Sudan connect with the universal experience of looking through family photographs. The project begins with Sudanese families, but it often sparks conversations about memory, belonging, and the role photographs play in preserving our personal and collective histories. © From the archive of Elsharif Aboud While working with these family collections, were there any parts of the experience that challenged or surprised you? One of the biggest challenges was earning the trust of families. It is not easy to ask people to share something as personal as their family history with the public. These photographs carry intimate memories, so from the beginning I understood that the project had to move at the family's pace. In many cases, our first step was simply to digitize the photographs and preserve them, then have ongoing conversations with the families about what, if anything, they felt comfortable sharing publicly. What I never expected was how dramatically the project would change after the war began. Many of the family archives we had hoped to digitize were lost or became inaccessible as homes were damaged, destroyed, or abandoned during the fighting in Khartoum. The scale of that cultural loss has been heartbreaking. At the same time, it has reinforced why this work matters. These archives are part of Sudan's collective memory. Today, preserving them feels even more urgent than when I first started the project. My greatest hope is that one day we can return to Khartoum, reconnect with these families, and continue this important work of preserving Sudan's visual history before more of it disappears. “Very often, the conversations that happen around the photographs are just as valuable as the images themselves.” Do you have a favorite photo from the Sudan Family Archives project, and why? It is such a unique photograph. It was taken in the 1970s, and all of the women in the picture are grandmothers today. I love how inviting the image feels. It draws you in and makes you wonder about their lives and their friendships, and wonder where they are now. © Abbas's relatives in Alobied during a family holiday, from the Abbas Family Archive Lastly, what advice would you give to our readers who are interested in engaging with photography history and archives themselves? My advice would be to start close to home. Ask your parents, grandparents, relatives, or neighbors if they have old photo albums or boxes of negatives stored away. You might be surprised by what you find. And spend time looking beyond the photographs themselves. Ask who took the picture, who is in it, where it was taken, and what the story behind it is. Very often, the conversations that happen around the photographs are just as valuable as the images themselves. Thank you, Ala, for sharing your incredible project with us! **New Town, Old School: Photographing Northstowe before the town became itself**

Northstowe is the largest new town being built in the UK since Milton Keynes. It has been my home for the last eight years. It is being developed on the former RAF Oakington airfield, between Cambridge and the Fen edge. As it approaches the tenth anniversary of the first residents arriving, it is still very much a town in the process of becoming a town. Streets, houses and public spaces are appearing, but there are large areas where the previous landscape remains visible. Old boundaries cut across new developments. Empty spaces sit alongside finished houses. Construction sites give way to fields, and military remnants sit beside the infrastructure of a new community. We tend to think of towns as finished things. You arrive somewhere and it is simply there. Streets have names, buildings have histories, people have routines, groups are established and the landscape has acquired a familiar appearance. Northstowe hasn’t reached that point yet. As an early resident and amateur photographer, I started photographing it because I was fascinated by the process happening around me. I’m not photographing new houses simply as architectural objects. What interests me is everything around them: the materials waiting to be used, the spaces waiting to acquire a purpose, the evidence of things that have happened during the building process, the interim states and temporary structures that may disappear as quickly as they arrived. It is a landscape of the not quite yet. And perhaps inevitably, a landscape of the already gone. I’m photographing it with a camera and process that belong, in photographic terms at least, to another age. New town, old camera I decided to work with my Pentax ME Super, limiting myself to a 50mm lens, shooting black and white film and developing it by hand in Caffenol. Why? One of my modern digital cameras could make perfect sense for this project. I could walk around and photograph everything as pixel perfect high resolution images, checking them immediately and returning whenever something changed. But that isn’t the point. Using the Pentax, a small, straightforward 35mm SLR from an earlier generation, forces me to slow down and consider what is in front of the lens. For me, it is a camera for looking. I’m not trying to make sweeping architectural photographs or dramatic aerial views of the development. I’m interested in what is at eye level. The camera and 50mm lens combination keeps me at human scale. And then there’s film.. For the most part I’m using Fomapan. Not a particularly expensive or exotic film. I like that. But the other part of the process is what happens after I’ve taken the photographs: I develop the film in Caffenol: coffee, washing soda and vitamin C. It’s an almost absurdly analogue process for photographing a town designed to represent the future. Why Caffenol? I didn’t choose Caffenol because I thought it would somehow make the photographs more “vintage”. If anything, the attraction is the uncertainty. Caffenol isn’t as predictable as simply pouring a commercial developer into a tank and following the instructions. The results have their own character, and there are inevitably small variations with each roll developed. That seems fitting for Northstowe. The town itself is a work in progress. A view that exists today may disappear behind a new building in a few months. The negatives have their own imperfections and variations too. The photographic process becomes part of the project rather than simply a way of recording it Looking slowly There’s also something deliberately slow about the process.I have an SLR and a roll of film.Thirty-six opportunities at a time.That limitation changes the way I look. I walk. I stop. I look again. Sometimes I take the photograph. Sometimes I don’t. Then I go home and develop the film with ingredients that are more likely to be found in a kitchen than a darkroom. By the time I scan the negatives, the landscape may already have changed. I like that. It creates a separation between making the photograph and seeing the photograph. It also means that I’m not constantly trying to perfect the image while I’m standing in front of the subject. The photograph has already happened. All I can do is wait and see what I’ve got. A town becoming history There is a slightly paradoxical aspect to photographing somewhere new. At first, it feels as though you’re photographing the future. But the moment you press the shutter, you’re also photographing the past. That is particularly noticeable at Northstowe. A photograph made today might show a road that won’t look the same next year. A pile of pipes will be buried. A pile of bricks will become a home. A building compound will become a park, a school or a community centre. Although, as I write this, we’re still waiting for a shop! The photograph freezes a moment in a process that doesn’t stop. That is what has gradually become the main interest of the project for me. I’m less interested in producing a comprehensive record of Northstowe than in collecting fragments and reflections on the journey. Others will take perfect digital images showing the finished town. What I want to preserve are some of the things that happen before that. The awkwardness. The gaps. The leftovers. The temporary arrangements. The places where the old landscape hasn’t quite given way to the new one. And so it has become not simply a documentary exercise, but also a meditation on the tensions, challenges and promise of building a new community over time. The not quite yet The pipes are a good example of what draws me to the project. They are infrastructure waiting to become infrastructure. At some point these pipes will disappear beneath the ground, carrying water, services or drainage. Once the town is complete, most people will walk over them without ever knowing they are there. For now, though, they are visible. They are part of the between. A new town also produces strange contradictions. A park may look finished, with grass, benches and newly planted trees, but a fence can still say KEEP OUT. The landscape has been designed for people, but people aren’t quite allowed to use it yet. The invitation to play is there, but so is the prohibition. That tension between invitation and exclusion occurs repeatedly in Northstowe. The town is being made for future residents, but for much of its construction those future residents are effectively looking at it from the outside. There is also something strangely human about looking at a collection of foundations. At this stage they are simply concrete, piles of bricks and timber. But each one represents a future home. And each future home represents something much less abstract: people, families, arguments, dinners, birthdays, children growing up, dogs, gardens, neighbours – lives yet to be lived. None of that is visible in the photograph. But it’s there in the potential of the foundations. This is perhaps what makes photographing a new town different from photographing an established one. In an older town, you can look at a house and imagine the history contained within it. Here, you can sometimes only imagine the history that hasn’t happened yet. The buildings and streets are the raw ingredients of future lives. Not everything in the landscape is about the future. Some things are evidence of what has already happened. A COVID-19 “Keep your distance” sign, left among construction barriers and materials, is a small reminder that the town hasn’t been built in isolation from the wider world. The sign was temporary. The circumstances were temporary. But they have now become part of Northstowe’s history. It is another example of the way the past accumulates even while the town is still being built. And sometimes even the future becomes history faster than expected. The House by Urban Splash sales centre was intended to promote a different approach to housebuilding, based around modular construction. It was a very contemporary idea for a very contemporary new town. But the company subsequently went into administration, and the sales centre became another temporary object in the landscape. I find this particularly interesting. Something designed to represent the future became part of the past while the town around it was still being built. Old school The “old school” in the title isn’t meant simply as a reference to film photography. It’s about the relationship between the way the town is being created and the way I’m choosing to photograph it. Northstowe is planned, engineered and designed for the future. I’m walking around it with a 35mm SLR. The town is being built with modern machinery, computer modelling and contemporary construction techniques. I’m developing my photographs in coffee. That contrast makes me smile, but I also think it says something more serious about photography. New technology doesn’t necessarily make us better observers. Sometimes removing technology from the process makes us pay more attention. The Pentax doesn’t show me an instant image.The film doesn’t give me unlimited exposures. Caffenol doesn’t give me perfectly repeatable results. All of those limitations force me to slow down and reflect. Before the town became itself Many of the images will simply become records of things that changed or disappeared. Perhaps others will acquire meaning because they show something that became important along the way. That’s one of the reasons I want to continue making the photographs. This isn’t a project with a neat ending. I keep coming back to it in fits and starts. As long as Northstowe is changing, the project can continue. It’s a slow, slightly unpredictable process. But so is creating a new community. Eventually the finished town will become ordinary. People will stop noticing that it is new. The temporary landscape will vanish, and the community will mature. And that’s the point at which these photographs will become something else. They will no longer be photographs of a town being built. They will be photographs of what was there before the town became itself. New Town, Old School is an ongoing personal photographic project by Peter Cope. **A Matter of Taste: what remains after eating**

Eating is one of the most common acts of daily life. However, when viewed in the context of its surroundings, it takes on broader meanings: work, privilege, survival, memory or a sense of belonging. It is against this backdrop that the fifth edition of the Yeast Photo Festival, entitled A Matter of Taste, takes place in Galatina from 11 September to 8 November. The event focuses not so much on the image of food itself as on the systems that make its existence possible. The selected photographs avoid the refinement typical of contemporary food photography and instead show peppers pierced on skewers, broad beans scattered on a blanket, freshly caught fish, handwritten recipes or a frying pan on the hob. Food is restored to its material nature, as something to be gathered, prepared, preserved and passed down. In an age when photographing meals has become a habitual gesture, the exhibition offers a different approach. Whilst digital images tend to transform food into an object of desire, here it becomes a means of observing what usually remains outside the frame. In the project Daughters of the Sea', Forough Alaei documents the fisherwomen of the Iranian island of Hengam, where their work is shaped by legal and social constraints, in a context that is at risk of disappearing. In The Last Generation', Bob Miller portrays American farming families at a time of transition, when the passing of land from one generation to the next can no longer be taken for granted. The theme of transmission emerges in several works. In Pier Costantini’s photographic diary, his father Guerino’s recipes form a domestic archive. In Marco Zorzanello’s La Panarda', an Abruzzese feast of over fifty courses becomes a communal occasion in which no one is excluded. Even the simplest images suggest that a culture is preserved through repeated gestures. A Matter of Taste addresses the theme of food without presenting it as an object of desire. In this context, ‘taste’ concerns who is able to take part in the meal, who prepares it, and what stories make their way onto the plate – as well as what might be lost.
