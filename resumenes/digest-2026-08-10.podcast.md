All images courtesy of the National Gallery of Art, shared with permission Remember Me' Highlights Black Americans' Self-Expression in Early Photography August 10, 2026 History Photography Kate Mothes Share Pin Email Copy Bookmark In our era of screens and proliferous imagery, it strains the imagination to comprehend a time before photography. Two centuries ago, an inventor named Nicéphore Niépce took the very first picture of his courtyard in France by fitting a pewter plate into a camera obscura. A few years later, a professional scene painter named Louis-Jacques-Mandé Daguerre conceived of the aptly named daguerreotype , in which the image could be permanently fixed. He and Niépce both sold their technologies to the French government, which then published a how-to guide and facilitated the global spread of the process. In 1939, the first daguerrotypes were created in the U.S. As they say, the rest is history. Except, we often only see a part of the whole story. Because the technology was expensive, and therefore having your photograph taken (let alone owning the equipment) was costly, the visual record of the 19th century in this medium is heavily skewed toward affluent, white individuals and families who could afford it. Due to the long exposure time required to capture the images, we also don't see candid shots, as carefully posed scenes were required for clarity and fidelity. Joseph D. Marsters, Portrait of Martha Ann Patty' Atavis and Anna Whitridge (1848), daguerreotype, image (visible): 3 9/16 x 2 9/16 inches Photography was invented decades before the Civil War and the end of slavery in the U.S., yet so rarely do we glimpse real faces that illuminate the complexities of race, freedom, and agency before, during, and after the Civil War, says the National Gallery of Art , which presents Remember Me: Picturing African Americans in Early Photography later this year. The exhibition brings forward dozens of images taken of and by Black Americans, showcasing a wide variety of lived experiences in one place. Fashion, family, and social status are on full display in a range of gelatin silver prints, daguerrotypes, tintypes, and ambrotypes , some of which are colorized. Because many of the earliest formats were very fragile, they were often displayed in ornate folding cases. Several of these will be included with their images, plus cabinet cards and portraits by pioneering Black artists like James Van Der Zee , who ran a prominent studio in Harlem in the early 20th century. The exhibition will highlight around 70 pieces in the museum's collection that center self-expression and the entrepreneurial spirit of African American individuals. Remember Me is slated to open on November 7 and will continue through March 14 in Washington, D.C. Learn more and plan your visit on the museum's website . **A Day With Brian Hippern and the Lomo'Instant Automat Glass**
Fotógrafos: chloefuller

Seattle-based collage artist and illustrator Brian Hippern creates vibrant, hand-cut compositions inspired by nature, found materials, and the quiet moments of everyday life. Working with colored pencil drawings, vintage paper, handwritten letters, and carefully collected ephemera, his intuitive process balances thoughtful composition with spontaneity, allowing each piece to evolve organically. For this edition of A Day With, we asked Brian to document a weekend in his creative life, from neighborhood coffee stops and treasure hunting for collage materials to long hours at the craft table bringing new works to life. Armed with the Lomo'Instant Automat Glass, he captured the people, places, and rituals that shape his artistic practice. Photo by Brian Hippern ,  Shot on the Lomo'Instant Automat Glass > Hello! Lomography asked me to document my creative process for the weekend using their instant camera. It went a little something like this: > A rare weekend off in my life starts with a trip to get some coffee with my wife. We’re spoiled in Seattle to be surrounded by a seemingly endless amount of quality coffee shops. Today we went to Red Arrow Coffee, a neighborhood favorite with a plant covered terrace situation that hides away from the main street. It’s like a little oasis. Photo by Brian Hippern ,  Shot on the Lomo'Instant Automat Glass > After coffee, I hit up the local art store, Push/Pull, for some extra paper. I go through a lot of paper making collage, so having an art store a few blocks away has been a massive benefit. I’m frequently after black mixed media paper, which usually functions as the base layer of all my collages. That stuff is like gold to me. I’m always on the hunt for black mixed media paper. Photo by Brian Hippern ,  Shot on the Lomo'Instant Automat Glass > A quick trip across the street to the vintage books store, Ballard Books, was made to sift through their incredible selection for some collage source material. I have my own rules about sourcing, and try to focus on material that seems rejected, and offer it new life. Here, I found some handwritten letters from the early 1930s to use. I love used book store, estate sales and vintage shops for collecting collage material and especially love handwritten notes or letters. > That is always a white whale for me: a letter written with good handwriting in black ink on old, good quality paper. Pure gold. I found an author’s prose writing rough drafts in a Life magazine from the 1960s at an estate sale once, that gave me a ton of mileage for collages. Something about a handwritten note carries some sort of mystic power with it. Not always, but I try to notice the ones that do. Do I sound crazy yet? Photo by Brian Hippern ,  Shot on the Lomo'Instant Automat Glass > After the book store, it was time to get to work. My collages lately have been made up of my own colored pencil drawings, cut and arranged with layers of paper and accent notes of found collage material. So, the first step: draw something. It was a nice day, so I took my dog outside to the backyard picnic table with my sketchbook and pencils, and got to drawing. > Lately, I’m focused on organic shapes, inspired by plant growth, the PNW, the yin and yang and playing with dimension. I try not to think too far ahead at this stage, and just freestyle. I know the general gist of what I’ll draw, but I let the rest just flow. Sometimes, I get too in my own head about making the perfect thing, when the best thing to do is just to listen to some music, and get lost in the drawing without having destination in mind. Photo by Brian Hippern ,  Shot on the Lomo'Instant Automat Glass > The next day, I took to the craft table to cut up my drawings and start working on collaging. I have a huge amount of scrap paper in various colors. I like using various types of paper, it gives the collage some dimension, maybe not visible through a screen, but certainly in person. My secret weapon is washi paper, but I need to keep the brand a secret. I don’t want to gatekeep, but this brand of washi paper is perfect and always sold out. Sorry! Photo by Brian Hippern ,  Shot on the Lomo'Instant Automat Glass > Assembling the collages can take a while. There’s a little more planning involved than the drawing, but I try to keep things loose, and never be stubborn. These things are all about composition, so exploring different orientations or options that you didn’t think would work can sometimes unlock better avenues to go down. You have to know that you don’t know everything, you know? Photo by Brian Hippern ,  Shot on the Lomo'Instant Automat Glass > I have a few showings coming up, so this month is really go-time in terms of production. I need to make a lot of work, especially because a lot of the time these collages aren’t great. I just make a bunch, try to work out the creative muscle, and trust that if I make enough, they’ll be some good ones from time to time. Photo by Brian Hippern ,  Shot on the Lomo'Instant Automat Glass > Thank you to Lomography for giving me the opportunity to document my creative process, and to you, if you’re reading this, go buy a camera, silly! ### Huck Magazine

**In photos: Electric nights in ’70s Japan**

Tokyo–Koza — Keizō Kitajima’s new photobook explores the explosion of party culture of Tokyo and Okinawa during an era of rapid change in the country, as well as its stranger turns. Photography emerged at the vanguard in Japan as the 1968–69 university protests signified the rise of the New Left amid a global revolution against Western imperialism. Liberated from the myth of “objectivity” and false hierarchies of form, a small group of Japanese artists and critics including Daidō Moriyama and Nakahira Takuma came together to create Provoke, a photography magazine that embraced the raw, radical, and expressionistic possibilities of the medium. Although Provoke only ran three issues, its legacy would inspire generations of photographers from around the globe, beginning with native son, Keizō Kitajima. “I began photography in the mid-1970s, around the time when the political fervour symbolised by the May 1968 Paris Uprising was beginning to subside,” he says. “At that time, photography was considered one of the most cutting-edge and powerful means of expression in Japan, and I was deeply impressed by it.” Born in 1954 in Suzaka, Nagano Prefecture, Kitajima first discovered the work of Nobuyoshi Araki and Daidō Moriyama as a teen, going on to study with the latter in 1975 at WORK SHOP Photography School, founded by members of Provoke after the magazine folded. The two established a lifelong collaboration that took root the following year with the creation of CAMP, an artist-run gallery, darkroom, and meeting space in the Shinjuku district of Tokyo. With Moriyama’s support, Kitajima began exploring Koza, the historical name for Okinawa City and second largest city on the island following Tokyo. “Okinawa has numerous US military bases, and Koza is a nightlife district catering to American soldiers,” Kitajima says. “When I frequented Koza, it was shortly after the Vietnam War, and the town was bustling with many returning soldiers. People from all over Southeast Asia gathered in Koza to do business with the American soldiers. Many of them, including the soldiers, carried painful experiences in their hearts. I heard a thousand sad stories and a hundred happy stories from them.” With the recent publication of Keizo Kitajima: Tokyo–Koza (Little Big Man Books), the artist returns to the start of his career, retracing a fascinating chapter of Japanese photography and history. The lush, oversize paperback brings together Kitajima’s early works, Photographs of Okinawa (1975–1979) and Tokyo 1979, to create an electric portrait of Japanese nightlife during the second half of the decade. In 1979, Kitajima mounted a monthly series of exhibitions at CAMP showcasing black and white photographs made around the gallery in Shinjuku Ni-chome, a former red light district, accompanied by booklets titled Photo Express: Tokyo. “Japan was undergoing rapid urbanisation, and I was fascinated by the image and energy of the city,” he says. “The city seemed like a chaotic entity where light and darkness coexisted. Wandering through the labyrinthine alleys of Tokyo, I sought an encounter with a truth that transcended good and evil.” Kitajima remembers the experience as a whirlwind, excitedly photographing people who frequented the area, delighting when they would come to exhibition openings when their images were hung. “I attempted to compress the act of shooting, production, and exhibition into a single act, presenting it with maximum volume,” he says. Kitajima transformed CAMP into a darkroom by covering the walls with rolls of photographic paper, projecting the negatives onto them, developing them with a sponge, rinsing them in a bucket of water, and opening the show the very next day in that state. It is only now, half a century after these two series were made, that they can be seen together as Kitajima always dreamed. “For me, there are no past photographs. All the photographs I take, including street photography, are in the present tense and reflect my current position,” he says. “I believe it’s important to repeatedly revisit the same photographs, and I continue to re-examine the photographs I’ve taken, reinterpreting them in new contexts. My photographs are always beyond me.”Keizo Kitajima: Tokyo–Koza is published by Little Big Man Books.Miss Rosen is a freelance arts and photography writer, follow her on X.Buy your copy of Huck 83 here.Enjoyed this article?
