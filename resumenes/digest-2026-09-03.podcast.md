**The Questionnaire : Sandrine Arons by Carole Schmitz**

Sandrine Arons : What the image does not show For Sandrine Arons, the true subject of photography begins precisely where the visible ends. Her images do not document reality: they put it to the test. They question what we think we see, what we have forgotten to look at, and what an image sometimes reveals only in retrospect. For her, photography is less about capturing than exploring. A face, a body, a landscape, an object become entry points into a more interior reality, shaped by memory, sensations, absences and fragments of identity. This approach is anything but accidental. Sandrine Arons’ gaze has been shaped at the intersection of several territories photography, literature, psychology and painting and this permeability runs through her entire practice. Magritte, Caravaggio and Frida Kahlo stand alongside Sally Mann, Sophie Calle, Dorothea Lange and Richard Avedon in a pantheon that already says much about the way she conceives the image: not as a transparent window onto the world, but as a space of displacement, uncertainty and interpretation. In her series, reality splits and doubles. Frontiers emerged from her travels and her multicultural experience, without initially being conceived as a project about identity. Only later did she recognize a recurring motif within her own images: that of borders, both geographical and psychological, and their inability to remain completely impermeable. With Multiplicity, the process is reversed. The concept comes before the image: several “selves” — woman, mother, wife, daughter must coexist within a single photographic space. Not as a digital construction, but as a physical manifestation of this inner coexistence. This desire to make the invisible visible finds its most intimate expression in her relationship to memory. The portrait she took of her father after his death becomes, for her, a threshold image, suspended between a before and an after. As for the photographs she made over nearly seven years of her mother, who was living with a degenerative disease, they raise an altogether more vertiginous question: can disappearance be photographed without betraying it? These images, still kept outside the exhibition space, bear witness to a practice willing to look where looking becomes difficult. Her attachment to black and white stems from the same imperative. Far from being an aesthetic form of nostalgia, it allows her to strip away whatever might distract the eye. To remove color in order to reach a form of essence. To silence the setting so that we can listen to what the image says otherwise. “What is real?” she asks. What was experienced? What we remember? Or what photography reveals to us when we look at it again? Perhaps it is within this gap that Sandrine Arons’ territory lies. Between the image and what it sets in motion. Between reality and its memory. Between what we look at and what, suddenly, looks back at us. Her photography does not necessarily seek to provide answers. It prefers to leave a crack. And it is through that crack that something gets through. **J. Paul Getty Museum : Every Minute Is History : Five Views of Los Angeles**

The J. Paul Getty Museumpresents “Every Minute Is History: Five Views of Los Angeles”. Featuring works by five photographers – Laura Aguilar, Reynaldo Rivera, George Rodriguez, Patssi Valdez, and Ricardo Valverde – this exhibition explores how these artists expanded the understanding of the built environment and cultural landscape of Los Angeles, along with the representations of Latino/a communities in the city during the second half of the 20th century. “Through their unique visions, these photographers have challenged conventional narratives and broadened the ways Latino/a lives are represented,” said Timothy Potts, Maria Hummer-Tuttle and Robert Tuttle Director of the J. Paul Getty Museum. “Their work documents histories that have often been overlooked, while also exploring deeply personal themes of identity, belonging, and creative expression.” Emerging during and after the Chicano Liberation Movement of the 1960s, the artists in the exhibition demonstrate the breadth of photographic practices that celebrated Latino/a and Chicano/a identities by blending documentary, conceptual, and experimental approaches to create enduring records of Los Angeles’s cultural and physical landscapes. George Rodriguez (American, born 1937) documented both celebrity culture and the collective political struggles of Mexican Americans during the Chicano Movement. His photographs capture pivotal moments in Los Angeles history, including the Sunset Strip Riots, the United Farm Workers movement, and subsequent periods of social unrest, creating a powerful visual record of civic activism and cultural change. Ricardo Valverde (American, 1946–1998) used photography to chronicle his family, friends, and the East Los Angeles community in which he lived. While working for the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power, he photographed daily life throughout the city. In the final decade of his career, Valverde experimented with photographic materials, revisiting older negatives and altering prints with pigment and incised marks to create richly textured and increasingly abstract images. Laura Aguilar (American, 1959–2018) became renowned for her compassionate portraits of queer communities and for her vulnerable self-portraits. After studying at East Los Angeles College, Aguilar created intimate photographs that explored identity, visibility, and belonging, often positioning her own body within the landscapes of the American Southwest. Reynaldo Rivera (American, born 1964 in Mexico) documented the vibrant underground music, nightlife, and drag performance scenes of Los Angeles during the 1980s and 1990s. **The Rencontres d’Arles 2026 : What Our Images Say About Us**

Once again this year, Arles was transformed into a vast city devoted to photography. For the 57th edition of the Rencontres d’Arles, I moved through the exhibitions with the sense that, more than the images themselves, it was our way of looking that was being put to the test. Using analogue photography, the artist photographs images taken from streaming videos featuring cam girls, post-pornography and feminist pornography. She isolates fragments of bodies and gestures lost within the digital flow, transforming them until they acquire an almost spectral appearance. Through photographs, objects and fragments of memory, she constructs an intimate narrative around a place that has become legendary. Presented at the Chapelle Saint-Martin du Méjan, the exhibition brings together images taken in cities as varied as New York, Paris, Tokyo, Moscow and Mumbai. Within this former chapel, the contrast between the architecture and the chromatic richness of his photographs makes the experience particularly immersive. The exhibition brings together photographic archives relating to electricity, industry and, above all, the prevention of workplace accidents. Behind monumental images of electrical infrastructure, workers gradually emerge, along with technical gestures and the invisible dangers associated with their jobs. I found the project particularly interesting because it demonstrates how an apparently documentary photograph can also become a tool for memory and accountability. Things that now seem entirely ordinary to us switching a light on, operating a machine depend on labour and risks that we rarely see. Among the projects on view, Andi Gáldi Vinkó’s Sorry I Gave Birth I Disappeared But Now I’m Back (2016–2022) particularly resonated with me. I appreciated the way Gáldi Vinkó refuses to present motherhood as either an entirely joyful or an entirely difficult experience. These apparently ordinary scenes become alternative images, revealing everything that photographs can leave outside the frame. I found the project particularly interesting because it demonstrates that an old photograph is never entirely fixed: it can be revisited, displaced and made to tell a different story. What I ultimately take away from this edition of the Rencontres d’Arles is this succession of shifts in perspective. Looking at a body, an animal, a worker, a mother or an old family picture is never neutral. More than anything, the photographers I encountered this year reminded me that a photograph does not simply show us something: it also reveals the way we choose to look at it. Tímea Urbantsok

**Archives : In Memoriam : Jean Marquis**

Archives – September 3, 2019 Monday, September 2nd, 2019. It’s back to school. Parents accompany their little ones for the big jump in life. It was nice this Monday at Rambouillet. Jean Marquis, 93 years old, also left surrounded by all his family. He leaves us a magnificent body of work. Françoise Denoyelle Jean Marquis: From the stage to the darkroom Jean Marquis had chosen the theater, the limelight, the free life of a performer, and the vertigo of words. In the games of love and chance, he threw his lot with Susie Fischer: a whirlwind romance. Aged twenty-three, in 1949, the young man had left the commune of Armentière, Lille, and the world of Pavillon Bleu on the banks of the Deûle, and headed for Paris. He continued to work in theater as a stage assistant. There followed his marriage with Susie, Robert Capa’s cousin, and his introduction to the legendary reporter, the heart and soul of Magnum photos agency founded and run by young photographers. Susie was part of the group and focused on marketing the images. Jean took some photos of stage sets and was in charge of stage lighting, but there is a big step to devoting himself to photography full time… But the idea is there. Capa, always ready with recommendations and proofs of friendship, got Marquis hired as an assistant to Pierre Gassmann, a former photographer working as a printer for his friends. The developing trays of his Pictorial Service held some of the best of contemporary photography. Jean Marquis crossed over from word to image: a revelation! In the spring of 1953, his contract with Gassmann had expired: it was time to plunge into deep waters. His first photo stories: the Foire du Trône, a Sidney Bechet concert, an SNCF train strike… In order to join Magnum, he needed a more in depth story. He returned to the banks of the Deûle, to his native north country. He was flooded by images of the past: the family business liquidated in 1936; the takeover of the Pavillon Bleu, a riverside inn where his radical-socialist father exhorted bargemen amid canals, slag heaps, factories, and mining communities that lived the harsh and solidary life of workers, so close and so distant in their silent dignity. In the fall, Jean Marquis was fully engaged with this corner of France, the country just barely emerging from the war. He was always hard at work, his sleeves rolled up. This was his first big assignment. The main lines of his work and the vocabulary of his style began to crystalize: his sensibility to the most trivial situations, to ordinary people, and unimportant sites; his spot-on framing, his sense of composition and balanced contrasts; his mastery of light, shadows, and darker blacks. Magnum was a whole new way of life. With Ernst Haas, Inge Morath, Kryn Taconis, and Marc Riboud, Jean Marquis joined a very exclusive club of photographic masters. The camera brought its own gratifications and kept him as spellbound as theater had. Marquis honed his skills, and sized up his abilities and the territories open to professional growth, against the clash of different sensibilities, varied aspirations to excellence, and struggles for influence among the most brilliant artists. Capa would dispatch him to film sets; he also traveled across Hungary, the home of the Fischer brothers; and took full advantage of the opportunities offered. Capa’s accidental death in 1954 altered the power balance within the agency. Susie severed her ties with Magnum. Jean Marquis followed suit three years later. While he was giving up an exceptional community, his connections with the domestic and international press remained intact. The New York Times Magazine, Time-Life, L’Express, Science et vie … continued to supply fresh opportunities. Susie took a post at the Parisian office of Time-Life. An independent photographer and master of his own production process, Marquis was nevertheless subject to the demands of the editors. Fashion, celebrity portraits, film sets, the modernizing infrastructure, national and international news stories piled up in his archives. Edward Steichen, the director of MoMA’s Department of Photography, selected the photograph Rue du Petit Musc for the exhibition The Family of Men. A few notable news stories gained Marquis a leading audience. Commenting on his reportage on the working-class environment with the journalist Béatrix Beck, “Caïn qu’as tu fait de ton frère?” [Cain, what have you done to your brother?”] (L’Express, December 14–16, 1955), Albert Camus wrote: “I defy anyone to read these pages without shame and without protest.” Reporting on assignment and reporting out of conviction, in line with the social aspirations of downtrodden men and women. In 1963, the publisher Robert Laffont, building on the success of Léon Zitrone’s book La vie d’un cheval de courses, illustrated by Marquis, invited him to collaborate on Louis Aragon’s book, Il ne m’est Paris que d’Elsa. The poet expressed some reluctance: “I don’t want anyone adding potted flowers to the foot of my poems.” There followed encounters, walks, and monologs: Aragon crisscrossing the Paris “of fake windmills pretending to spin in Montmartre,” exploring the recesses of memory, from the Hôtel Istria to the Rue de Varenne. Marquis engaged with the text, capturing in his images the rustling presence of Elsa: in between words and shadows, and lights. “Day breaks by the Fontaine des Innocents.” Not a single potted flower. The photographs are like signs of farewell suspended between departures and returns, like arms wide opened between the two banks of the Seine, and like time winding around the bridges. Marquis’s gaze is still distant, imbued with empathy, trained on the dying world of rural France and lost amid the Corrèze countryside. Accompanied by the journalist Lucien Espinasse, Marquis visited the region on multiple occasions between 1965 an 1967. He was keen to bear witness to the living conditions, to the disappearing skills and crafts of a countryside subsisting in age-long harmony. He knew how to frame the splendor of the landscapes shaped by prolonged human labor. Far from being anecdotal, the photographs uphold unadorned humanism. Michel Tournier analyzed its sources in his TV program, La Chambre Noire, on July 3, 1967. At ninety three years old, Jean Marquis still worked on his archives, had exhibitions, annotated his images, and accepted to be interviewed. He emanated self assurance for a job well done, questioning the verdicts of History. Susie is still there, sparkling and full of life, the temple-keeper. His work, intertwined with the upheavals of the Trente Glorieuses (i.e. 1945–75), speaks of conflicts, struggles, and grief that marked the era in which abundance, luxury, and opulence were only one side of the story. **Street Photography is Tough**

Street photography is tough. The way I see it, there are at least two approaches by which to achieve success. One requires stealth, quick reaction time, finely tuned powers of observation and anticipation. It requires an intimate comfort with your camera of choice. The other method is to engage with your subjects. Play with them. Get in close. Get to know them and gain their trust and then ask or feel confident that it’s OK to take a photograph. This is a much more conspicuous and honest approach. I recently read the excellent article published right here on 35mmc by Scott Ferguson – Photochemical Graffiti – a play on the similarly titled Led Zeppelin album. In his article, Scott describes how in his earlier days in this genre, he was too shy, lacked confidence and felt that he was taking too much of the subjects time. Judging from what he photographed on the day of his street adventure, he’s obviously come a long way. Check out his article. Me on the other hand…… I struggle with street photography. Mightily. I employ the stealth method. I’m a coward. And that unfortunately makes the whole enterprise more difficult. Last month there was an outdoor music festival in my hometown of Hamilton, Ontario. **Born Chaotic**

A film needs to be born twice before it shines. The first birth is the exposure. When a shutter is fired, a certain amount of light, bounced from physical objects, passes through glass and tiny openings, engraving an image onto a blank film. That’s the first birth we all know about. But the second, which I would argue against common sense, is probably far more meaningful. It doesn’t happen until the film is developed, scanned, and finally presented in front of you, days, weeks, or even months later. This is what many people call the disadvantage of shooting film: you never really know what you’ve got until the film is already born as what it is. Before pressing that little button called a shutter, you can put all your imagination and effort into what you expect the photograph to become. But once it’s done, you either accept it or discard it. There is no second chance. But aside from simply accepting or discarding a film, sometimes there is another possibility, at least for me: to discover a new kind of meaning in something that has already been born. More often than not, this new interpretation is completely different from what I had in mind when I pressed the shutter. A wrong exposure, an awkward composition, or even a pointless subject can become unexpectedly moody. Some frames I shot almost carelessly carry far greater emotional weight than an entire roll of well controlled photographs. That is what I call the second birth. I have found the beauty of the second birth throughout my work over the past two years. Some of my rolls were precisely planned and executed. I wouldn’t say none of those results had ever excited me. But I gradually realized that whether a film ultimately moves me has very little to do with what it was about or how it was shot. Not just technical aspects, sometimes even the original idea doesn’t matter that much. During the second birth, every frame is judged equally by the same emotional sensor. Once the emotional sensor took over, I began wondering how I had arrived at this point. I still remember the excitement of getting my first SLR. I experimented with every piece of equipment I could afford and every technique I could imagine or learn from the Internet. Within just two or three years, I filtered out most of them, settled on a few focal lengths, and formed my own technical and aesthetic discipline. Then? It’s boring. I wanted to repeat the old experiments that once excited me, but I would stop myself: I’ve already ruled those approaches out. Why go back? … Until I found the second birth. And I realized all those disciplines in photography and storytelling only belonged to the first birth. During the second, I no longer cared about descriptiveness, one of the fundamental elements of photography. I cared about how it feels when I look at it. Digital rarely gives this privilege. A frame that doesn’t perfectly reflect the original idea is often killed on the memory card seconds after its first birth. I would look for a second shot, not a second birth. And that’s why I took my Minolta Weathermatic Dual 35, an underwater toy camera, into the operating room to document Anastasia’s first second to her first seven days in this world. My journalist instinct still told me to anticipate every critical moment, plan how the story would unfold, and predict the exposure and composition. I knew there wouldn’t be a second chance for such a documentary. But, heck, why fear an imperfect first birth, when I knew another one was still waiting? **UNIVERSE CONTAINED**

Space is often mistaken for emptiness. As if silence were the absence of life, rather than evidence of everything it has absorbed. But space remembers. It stretches around memory, desire, grief, instinct. It holds traces of movement long after bodies disappear from it. A woman moves through space differently. She does not simply occupy it; she alters its emotional temperature. Presence becomes something expansive, difficult to contain within fixed borders. She carries softness beside severity, restraint beside hunger, solitude beside sensuality. Never singular, never fixed, she exists as an accumulation: of past selves, imagined selves, inherited selves, hidden selves. Entire interior worlds coexisting at once. In emptiness, these layers become visible. Without distraction, space begins to echo the architecture of the self. Corners feel like memory. Light becomes almost tactile. Silence sharpens perception. What first appears vacant slowly reveals itself to be dense with emotional residue. There is a particular power in flourishing without excess. In discovering that abundance is not always material, but internal. That beauty can emerge not from decoration or fullness, but from tension, atmosphere, and the ability to remain expansive even within limitation. Perhaps this is why emptiness can feel strangely transformative. It leaves room for contradiction to surface. For fragility and confidence to exist together. For stillness to reveal movement underneath it. A woman becomes not one identity, but many unfolding simultaneously—shifting between presence and absence, exposure and protection, intimacy and distance.

There are a few moments in a man’s life that are truly capable of changing him in the depths of his being. Whether through pain or peace, sadness or joy. **Las fotos no hechas: Vietnam**

Con aquel tiempo resultaba más tedioso manejar la cámara manual con la que había decidido afrontar aquel viaje, que ya terminaba. Para la mayoría de las tomas solo hacía falta un poco de paciencia, pero aquel día necesitaría un manejo más preciso, rápido y fluido. Especialmente para la foto que no pude hacer: un retrato. Despachamos la distancia desde Hanói durmiendo en el tren nocturno, en un trayecto de ocho horas, tal y como suelen hacer tanto locales como turistas. Una vez allí, notamos el frío enseguida y corrimos al hotel esquivando a guías y puestos callejeros. Nos cambiamos de ropa para afrontar el clima implacable.
