**Reimagining Films on Film: The Devil Wears Prada**
Fotógrafos: francinegaebriele, holgernitschke, ayaneike, redlissa, danielog, cosima, moisfoto, julientixier

Last year, the Lomography Magazine team came together for a special Writer’s Notes piece called Reimagining Films on Film – a reflective essay based on films that deeply resonate with us. I remember it as a lovely opportunity to reflect on our personal experiences during the holiday season while also celebrating the ways movies have influenced our perspectives. With that in mind, I wanted to pick up where we left off and continue with another film reimagined through analogue photography – another 2000s gem that shaped a lot of what I dreamt of back then and what I strive for now. Recently, I had the chance to watch the long-awaited sequel to The Devil Wears Prada. Seeing the story continue in the modern age prompted me to reflect on what the original meant to me growing up and how I resonate with what the sequel advocates for. The last time I shot with the Lomo LC-A 120, almost half of my roll came out blank and I accidentally overexposed some shots, leaving me with only a few useable results. Determined not to repeat those mistakes, I brought along the Lomo MC-A as a backup. It was also my first time using this 35 mm camera for a conceptual photoshoot, so I was excited to see how it would perform. I used two rolls of Lomography Color Negative 120 ISO 800 and a roll of LomoChrome Classicolor 35 mm ISO 200. Credits: @francinegaebriele ,  Shot with Lomo LC-A 120 and Lomography Color Negative 120 ISO 800 Since fashion is such an important part of The Devil Wears Prada, I wanted the styling to feel intentional. Hanji put together her own look for the shoot, and she did an amazing job picking pieces that matched my vision. She wore a structured blazer cinched with a belt, then paired it with a short skirt and knee-high boots. The oversized sunglasses were also a great touch as this frame style was one of the fashion staples that defined that era. I initially wanted her to wear something red to add a pop of color to the photos and pay homage to the movie’s color palette, but I eventually realized that her pretty red hair was already enough of a statement – and it happened to be a somewhat similar shade to Emily Charlton’s hair in the movies, too! We brought along a magazine and a cup of coffee to complete the look, and then we were ready to go. Credits: @francinegaebriele ,  Shot with Lomo LC-A 120 and Lomography Color Negative 120 ISO 800 One of the reasons I chose this film for this article was because its recent sequel brought me back to a childhood fascination with a very specific kind of movie character: the ambitious young woman making her way through a big city, often with a career in journalism – Andy Sachs (The Devil Wears Prada), Jenna Rink (13 Going on 30), Andie Anderson (How to Lose a Guy in 10 Days), etc. At the time, writing-related jobs felt like the coolest career to me because for some reason, a lot of movies depicted it as so. I grew to admire the worlds that these characters inhabited and it inevitably sparked my interest in pursuing a similar path. As a child, I knew very little about what actually went into these careers. I was too captivated by the romanticized version of a working adult’s life that I never anticipated the pressure, the competition, the sacrifices, and the uncertainties in the off screen world. It’s a bitter truth that I think most of us come to realize at some point when we age. This might especially be true for artists who once had nothing but the burning desire to create only to eventually be greeted with the reality that sometimes passion alone isn't enough. Perhaps that’s also why the sequel felt so timely to me. It allowed me to revisit a world I once dreamed about and see it through the perspective of someone who now understands a little more about what it actually takes to be part of it. Credits: @francinegaebriele ,  Shot with Lomo MC-A and LomoChrome Classicolor 35 mm ISO 200 The sequel’s premise revolves around the decline of traditional print media, the rise of digital content, and the pressure to adapt to an industry that no longer looks quite the same as it did in the 2000s. Admittedly, the movie did not come close to the charm and legacy of the first, but it still felt like a fitting statement from a story so deeply rooted in creative work. I found myself relating to the anxiety it evokes throughout its entire run time – the fear of being left behind and of wondering what place your work will have in the future. It is truly unsettling to watch an industry you care about become a victim of corporate greed, especially when it comes at the expense of years of human creativity that built it all in the first place. As someone who has always valued the art and passion I saw portrayed on screen, it was a heartbreaking thought. Credits: @francinegaebriele ,  Shot with Lomo LC-A 120 and Lomography Color Negative 120 ISO 800 During the shoot, I basically had Hanji walk around and pose in an area that I felt could capture a little bit of that New York atmosphere from the film. I also intentionally incorporated motion into some of the shots to reflect the fast-paced energy of the city and the hectic mood of working in journalism. I wanted the photos to feel like a walk on the way to the office or an errand run in the middle of the work day. It was a fun shoot, though I’m honestly still getting the hang of shooting conceptual portraits on film. I had a clear vision of how I wanted the photos to look, but I still felt nervous of whether or not I was achieving what I had so deliberately imagined prior. Looking back at the results, I realized my biggest issue was that I didn’t make the most of it. Since I was too worried about not getting the shots I wanted, I found myself taking several repeats instead of experimenting more with different directions. As a result, I think the final set could have benefited from a little more variation. Nonetheless, it was a cool experience and another step towards learning to fully trust my instincts when shooting on film. **The Questionnaire : Antonin Katre by Carole Schmitz**

Antonin Katre : Ruins As Material For more than thirty years, Antonin Katre has explored industrial wastelands, abandoned buildings and suspended architectures between past and future. Emerging from the Paris graffiti scene of the 1990s, he gradually shifted his gaze from the wall to space, and from space to images. For him, photography is never merely documentary: it becomes the raw material for a practice where painting, silkscreen, architecture, installation and light converge. His images are less concerned with spectacular ruins than with what they reveal: time layers, worn surfaces, material accidents, traces of a human activity that has now disappeared. Concrete, metal, glass, peeling paint and industrial marks form a visual grammar that sometimes brings his photographs close to a form of contemporary archaeology. But where the archaeologist seeks to reconstruct, Katre accepts the void. He does not fill in absences; he puts them under tension. This attention to what is no longer there leads to a central question in his work: what can an image still produce once its subject has disappeared? Photography becomes both an act of preservation and of transformation. It fixes an architecture condemned to disappear while giving it a second existence, independent of its physical reality. This is where his graffiti background takes on its full meaning. The urban gesture has not disappeared as it moves from the wall to the photograph; it has simply changed territory. The photographic surface becomes a wall in its own right, a space for intervention. Katre adds paint, material, transparency, texture or light, shifting the image towards the realm of object. The photograph is no longer simply what remains of the place; it becomes the site of a new intervention. This movement from the photographic to art is probably one of the most distinctive aspects of his practice. Katre does not seek to preserve the original image reverently. He disrupts it. He superimposes other temporalities onto it. The industrial architecture, already marked by traces of its history, receives a new layer of memory: that of the artist’s gesture. His use of black and white is part of this construction. Far from being a nostalgic choice, it acts as a tool of reduction. By removing part of the chromatic information, Katre brings out structures, lines, volumes and, above all, textures. Yet this stripping-down paradoxically prepares the return of colour: when colour enters his works, it is no longer descriptive. It becomes event, intrusion, energy. There is therefore a constant dialogue in Katre’s work between architectural order and free gesture. On one side, industrial geometries, perspectives and repetitive structures; on the other, graffiti, paint, light and accident. Two visual systems that might appear antagonistic but which, in his work, feed one another. Architecture imposes a structure on the eye; the artistic gesture disrupts it. His photography exists precisely in this in-between space. It is neither entirely documentary nor truly staged. It begins with reality but refuses to stop there. What seems to interest him is less what a place was than what it can still become within the image. His recent experiments with ephemeral light installations in abandoned spaces logically extend this approach. Introducing light into abandoned places almost reverses the process of decay: where time has gradually withdrawn human presence, the artist reintroduces a presence of his own—but a temporary one. He does not restore. He reactivates. This distinction is fundamental. Katre does not seek to save industrial wastelands from disappearance. Instead, he works with that disappearance as if it were an artistic material. His works therefore speak less of nostalgia than of transformation. They record a world that is fading while simultaneously inventing the conditions for its survival. Ultimately, photographing a ruin for Antonin Katre means photographing a possible future: that of a place that no longer quite exists, but which, within the image, can begin to produce meaning again. The industrial wasteland becomes a laboratory where memory and invention, architecture and graffiti, disappearance and creation meet. **FOTO ARSENAL WIEN : Zanele Muholi. We Are Here**

FOTO ARSENAL WIEN presents the exhibition Zanele Muholi. We Are Here. The direct, focused gaze is confident and uncompromising yet vulnerable and powerful a tension that is also reflected in the way the clothing is styled. In this self-portrait, photographer Zanele Muholi wears a bow tie, which is a symbol of Western-influenced men’s clothing, and an Ingwe T-shirt, which has traditional male connotations in the Zulu communities of South Africa. Worn by Muholi, a nonbinary person, in this picture, it raises questions about the currency of gender roles, stereotypes, and traditions. Phaphama, the title of the photograph, means “Wake up!” in IsiZulu and seems like an invitation to look more closely. Who is depicted in pictures? Whose stories are told and saved in archives? And who remains invisible? Questions such as these are central to the oeuvre of Zanele Muholi, one of the most important voices in contemporary photography. Following presentations of Zanele Muholi’s works at Tate Modern London, Gropius Bau Berlin, and San Francisco Museum of Modern Art, FOTO ARSENAL WIEN is pleased to present Zanele Muholi. We Are Here, the photographer’s first solo exhibition in Austria. The comprehensive show brings together more than 120 photographs, as well as video works and archival material from two decades, providing an intensive insight into the complex oeuvre. Zanele Muholi (born in 1972 in Umlazi, South Africa) confidently uses the term visual activist rather than artist to refer to themself. For more than twenty years, they have dedicated themself to making Black lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, and intersex people visible in South Africa and all around the world. The works are the products of a deep bond and are created through an intensive exchange with the people and communities portrayed; the works are to be understood as a contribution to a visual history that was long ignored or repressed. Although South Africa was the first country in the world to guarantee the equality of people regardless of their sexual orientation in its constitution, discrimination, exclusion, and severe violence are still everyday factors in the life of queer people there. In the words of curator Marit Lena Herrmann (FOTO ARSENAL WIEN), “Embracing the community has shaped Muholi’s work for over two decades: It is about revealing inequality and simultaneously about change. Muholi’s images tell positive visual stories and show the power of togetherness and the healing that comes from this sense of community.” Zanele Muholi. We Are Here consists of six sections that bring together key series from different phases. In the series Being (2006), Muholi focuses on intimate lesbian relationships. The photographs depict love, trust, and everyday life, creating images that reflect belonging and self-evident facts. Zanele Muholi observes, “Despite the fact that, in contrast to most other African states, our South African bill of rights guarantees us legal protections against homophobia, there are still no loving, intimate photographs of Black lesbians. As a visual artist, one is always confronted with the politics of representation. I have the choice to portray my community in a manner that will turn us once again into a commodity to be consumed by the outside world, or to create a body of meaning that is welcomed by us as a community of queer Black women. I choose the latter path, because it is through capturing the visual pleasures and erotica of my community that our being comes into focus, into community and national consciousness.” Another focus of the exhibition is the ongoing project Faces and Phases (2006–). The archive, which now contains several hundred black-and-white portraits, is a collaborative project that brings together Black LGBTQIA+ people. Personal testimonies supplement the vivid archive of memory, presence, and community. Concurrently with the exhibition, the book Faces and Phases will be published by MACK, London (2026). A rich program of events, tours, and education accompanying the exhibition invites visitors to take subjects such as queer Black visibility, memory, and social involvement one step further. The focus of Muholi’s oeuvre is always communicating with the local community and creating spaces for encounters and exchange. The accompanying program, which consists of discussions, workshops, tours, a conference, and book presentations, depends on various collaborations with institutions such as the Queer Analog Darkroom Collective, MEZEKERE, the Queer Museum Vienna, DER GREIF, and the Austrian Film Museum. Zanele Muholi is the 2026 recipient of the internationally acclaimed Hasselblad Award. The award includes an exhibition in Gothenburg, which opens in October 2026. **Daniel Cooney Fine Art : The World of Charles Henri Ford & Pavel Tchelitchew**

Daniel Cooney Fine Art : : The World of Charles Henri Ford & Pavel Tchelitchew Daniel Cooney Fine Art presents The World of Charles Henri Ford & Pavel Tchelitchew, an exhibition of photographs by Charles Henri Ford and works on paper by Pavel Tchelitchew, curated by photographer Allen Frame. The exhibition offers an intimate portrait of an enduring artistic partnership and the extraordinary cultural world they inhabited in Paris, New York, and Rome, from the 1930s through the 1950s. Charles Henri Ford (1908-2002) was a writer and artist whose creative life encompassed poetry, fiction, photography, collage, film, publishing, and drawing. Pavel Tchelitchew (1898-1957) was an admired and influential figure in the European and American avant-garde. Born in Russia, he fled the Revolution with his family in 1918 and studied art in Kiev before working in Berlin as a stage and costume designer. He moved to Paris in the mid-1920s, where he created sets and costumes for Diaghilev’s Ballet Russe and made figurative paintings that combined elements of Surrealism and Neo-Romanticism. Ford and Tchelitchew were each other’s muses. During the period of their relationship from 1933 to Tchelitchew’s death in 1957, Ford photographed Tchelitchew extensively and Tchelitchew painted Charles. They were a remarkable duo and were both, in turn, photographed by their notable artist friends, including George Platt Lynes, Cecil Beaton, and Carl Van Vechten, whose photographs are included in the exhibition. Ford came to Paris in 1931, via Mississippi and New York, at first pursuing a budding relationship with the writer Djuna Barnes, 16 years older. Charles was 23, with blonde hair, wide-set, almond-shaped blue eyes, and a Southern accent—his family leased and managed small hotels in the Deep South. Both were bi, but presumed to be gay, and they had an intense and unconventional relationship. As Charles writes in an unpublished autofiction manuscript derived from his diaries of the 1930s, “She’d been to bed with enough men to know that there wasn’t all that much novelty to be found in the next one. But a boy? Beauty and a boy seemed no less poetic or mythological than beauty and a girl—let’s just say that a boy, at this point or period in her life, was more—mysterious, mystery being as much the absence of something as the presence of something.” Ford was a precocious poet and had already published eight issues of a literary review called Blues, A Magazine of New Rhythms, which he had produced in Mississippi before leaving for New York in 1930. He became close friends there with the writer Parker Tyler, whose poems he had already published in Blues (along with other writers such as Gertrude Stein, Ezra Pound, Sherwood Anderson, and Paul Bowles). When he returned to Mississippi after six months, he proposed to Tyler that they co-write a novel about their bohemian experience in New York’s Greenwich Village. Charles outlined its episodes, and they wrote it over the course of the next year before Ford returned to New York briefly en route to Paris. He became infatuated with Barnes, who was headed to Paris herself, and she offered to put him up in a garret apartment two flights up from her own. Barnes already knew Paris well; she’d been a significant part of the 1920s ex-pat scene, working as a journalist and publishing several books. Peggy Guggenheim had agreed to subsidize her with a monthly allowance. Plunged instantly into the literary scene, Ford, with his wit and good looks, was well received. Tchelitchew, spying him at an art opening, was smitten, but Ford was still with Barnes, and Tchelitchew was in a relationship himself with the American pianist Allen Tanner, living together with Tchelitchew’s demanding sister. With an invitation from a Cuban artist to join her in Italy, Charles decided to leave Paris. “Living with Djuna,” he writes in his manuscript, “had not been conducive to the production of poems. Her presence and formidable literary powers were too overwhelming. How could a small poem compete with her magnificent output, an outpouring, a flood. Where did it all come from, that inimitable style, that fiery vocabulary, piercing the unexpected like a divine huntress—Athena [Djuna] the incomparable, the incontestably supreme. Which meant there was no question of Higdon’s [Charles’], little boy from Mississippi, competing with her. The only way to compete with her was to get away—and start writing another novel. …Go away and write a great book! Poetry didn’t seem to be important to anyone, so there was no incentive to produce it. Everyone wrote prose. Prose, prose, prose. Joyce, Stein, Hemingway.” Tchelitchew gave Ford a going away party in an abandoned apartment house in Montparnasse, “an unoccupied building, four floors without electricity, on the rue Delambre…between the Dome and the Blvd Edgar-Quinet. The only expense would be the numerous candles, to illuminate the bare rooms on each floor (two large rooms to a floor.) … [and] jazz records from Victrolas…It would be word-of-mouth alert—and the word got around. One thousand guests, invited and uninvited, showed up…[including] Julien Levy, Lee Miller, and Kiki.” Ford departed for Florence to join his Cuban friend in her villa, then went with her to Rome and Madrid, and finally, to Tangier, where he persuaded Barnes to join him. They resumed their affair, and he typed up her novel Nightwood, which portrayed her previous relationship with an American artist named Thelma Wood. Now discovering that she was pregnant, and refusing to say whether Ford or another lover was the father, Barnes broke off their relationship and returned to Paris. In the meantime, Ford and Tyler’s novel, The Young and Evil, had been published in France (banned in the U.S. and England), and the publisher sent him money that he used to go back to Paris himself. He telegrammed Gertrude Stein to see whether he could visit her and Alice B. Toklas along the way at their country house in Bilignin, and she wired back, “Yes, if you are alone.” (She did not like Djuna Barnes.) Charles’ hair was as short as Stein’s, and one evening as she sat with her profile to him, writing in a notebook, he said, “You look handsome in that light.” “Yes,” she replied, “we are both very handsome.” Arriving in Paris, Ford connected again with Tchelitchew, who put him up in a room nearby where Charles spent months writing the stream-of-consciousness novel, Life of a Child, about his Mississippi childhood, told from the point of view of a child going from six to ten. Finally, he decided that the only way to pry Tchelitchew away from his sister and Allen Tanner was to leave him and return to the U.S. He felt sure that Tchelitchew would follow—he had already been offered a show at Julien Levy Gallery in New York. In fact, Pavel did follow him, and his painting of Charles as Huckleberry Finn wearing a straw hat was included in the show. In 1940, Charles founded the art magazine View, designed and associate edited by Parker Tyler, “intended to replace, in America, the European vanguard publications that folded as World War II approached: transition and Minotaure of Paris, and the London Bulletin, the British surrealist journal for which Ford had been the American editor (and Tyler a New York correspondent.)” View became one of the most significant American art magazines ever, with special issues devoted to Tchelitchew and Yves Tanguy, Duchamp, and Max Ernst, and including art and writing by Man Ray, Magritte, Joseph Cornell, William Carlos Williams, Wallace Stevens, Paul Bowles, Mina Loy, André Breton, Henry Miller, Borges, Sartre, Genet, and Camus. Tchelitchew was given a retrospective at MoMA in 1942, his visionary painting Hide and Seek acquired by MoMA that same year, but by the late 1940s he felt pushed out by the rise of Abstract Expressionism. The war now over, he and Ford moved to Rome, where Tchelitchew died in 1957. Ford published 17 books of poetry and exhibited photographs, drawings, paintings, collages, poem posters, and sculptures. He remained deeply connected to the art worlds of successive generations, introducing Andy Warhol to his first film camera and making his own experimental underground film, Johnny Minotaur, in Crete, in 1971. He moved to Kathmandu in 1972, where he met Indra Tamang, who subsequently collaborated with him, in Paris, Crete, and New York, remaining a close friend and invaluable assistant until Ford’s death in 2002. Tamang’s memoir, My Curious Years with Charles Henri Ford: The Autobiography of Indra B. Tamang, written with Romy Ashby, was published in 2024. Thanks to Indra Tamang for making this material available. Which bodies are normally granted visibility and under what conditions? **Archives : Peggy Anderson : The Morning Dip**

Archives – September 21, 2020 The small coastal village of Torekov, Sweden, is known for both its local pier,«Morgonbryggan», and for the associated daily ritual of a morning dip in the sea. Locals and summer guests are avid disciples of this sacred ritual which can be both solitary and social. There are unwritten codes of behavior related with this activity, including the most visually obvious, the selection of one’s bathrobe. Through the slow and repetitive process of working with a large format film camera, the photographerPeggy Anderson has gained insight into this community, her native country and her own place within it. From the text by Lyle Rexer: “The characteristic regalia of the morning dip appeared at once modest and distinctive, a kind of low-level signifier that seemed to say something about the social class, personality, and aesthetics of the bather. The bathrobe represented social and historical data in a way similar to, say, the industrial structures cataloged by the German photographers Bernd and Hilla Becher, who took a page from Sander’s typological book. Not to belabor the point, but the bathrobes offered an initial point of comparison to specify same and different, type and individual. The bathrobes were also an entry point for the project, since Anderson herself was an avid participant in the ritual. And over time, as people in Torekov got to know Anderson and agreed to be photographed, and especially as she herself expanded the pool of subjects to include bathers from the nearby local campgrounds, the project began to suggest generalizations about Swedes:This is what Swedes do in the summer; this is where lines of class are crossed; this is how they dress and present themselves—a matter of attitude. But that’s not exactly the way things went, or rather, that’s not the most important thing that we see. It has something to do with the camera. Anderson purposely chose to work with a 4×5 large format film camera. It is a large, slow, and somewhat cumbersome instrument that to contemporary eyes looks as much like an antediluvian remnant as the bones of a T-Rex. Yet it is beloved by many photographers not just for the detail it offers but even more for the process it entails. Shooting with a conspicuous machine, on a tripod, with each frame individually considered—not least of all because the film is so expensive—changes the way people in front of the camera behave. For one thing, they tend to be less suspicious and more curious. They are not being surveilled but attended to, and that is a very different experience. Such a camera makes photography important again by making the process of being photographed more intentional, even formal, closer to having a portrait painted. The pace slows down. Something significant is taking place, something not in a hurry”. Peggy Anderson is a photographer based in New York and Sweden but currently living in Paris. She graduated from The International Center of Photography (ICP) in 2013. Since then she has been pursuing various photography projects. **ORWO TF-12D – Controlling Contrast**

I love technical films. Films that aren’t meant for cameras, or if they were, weren’t meant for normal photography. High contrast copy films, sound recording films, Microfilms, direct duplication films. All of these films can occasionally be found online, and i am happy to try them all. Don’t get me wrong, I love conventional film, too! So why do I experiment with film that’s not meant to be put in a normal camera? Because they’re unique! While I have definitely bought a few technical films that for one reason or another aren’t super useful for normal photography, If you’re willing to experiment, and put some time into testing them, they can produce wonderful images that are just a little bit different than the film you can buy from places like B&H. While I have bought a lot of technical films, including stuff like Imaglink HQ microfilm, Kodak 2468 and 2422 direct dupe films, and even Imagesetting film that can see IR light , in this article I will focus on a film that I suspect many people might have used, but few have seen the full potential of, TF-12D. What is TF-12D? TF-12D is an obscure stock. There is a very little information about it online, at least by that name, in regards to using it for photography. There is one article claiming that TF-12d is sold by BFG in the Netherlands, but based on the examples shown there, I would be hesitant to say that it is TF-12d (more likely Aviphot 80, another one of my absolute favorite film stocks). While examples of photos might be a few and far between, there is a decent amount of information about the film’s intended use. This film is not meant for recording pictures, but for recording sound! Well, kind of. It doesn’t actually record sounds we hear, but a representation of sound that can be read by movie projectors. Picture showing several audio formats on 35 mm film, the two jagged lines on the right are the audio tracks made from sound film like TF-12D. Image from Wikipedia Commons. If you have ever used any sort of audio editing program, or maybe even a video editing program that lets you see the audio tracks, you are very familiar with what is usually put on this film. The sound is represented by a line of varying width, the wider the line, the louder the sound. I am no audio engineer, but from what I can tell, the modern equivalent is an “audio waveform”. you might be asking the question that I was initially asking when I learned about sound recording film: Why do you need a specific film to do this? Wouldn’t any normal film work? And the answer is, technically yes. But, practically no. There are a couple of traits that are fairly unique to this specific type of film, that still photography film just doesn’t have. Sound film needs to have an extremely fine grain and very high resolution, capable of capturing even the smallest detail. All of those very tiny details in the waveform need to be recorded almost perfectly. If they aren’t, the sound won’t be very good. While some low speed films like Pan-F might be capable of capturing such details, the next trait Is arguably more important, and also what makes it a very hard film to use in a normal camera. When an audio track is recorded onto film, it’s passed over a light in a machine that has a slot that can be made wider or narrower to record the waveform. It needs to record exactly where the light strikes the film and nothing else, And the density of where the light strikes needs to be extremely high. Thus, sound film needs to be some of the most contrasty film possible. TF-12d is normally extremely contrasty. Shot at EI 50 and developed in HC-110 B. So, now the question becomes why would you want to use a film like this? The answer for most people is, it’s a very unique look that you just can’t get with other films. For a while Washi was selling this film as Washi-S to photographers for that exact purpose, as a high-contrast, experimental film. FPP still sells the Kodak equivalent, 5278, as Sonic 25 as well. I bought it from Astrum, where I get most of my films, as “Foto-32”. And I do actually think that it looks interesting like this too! But, I’ll be the first to admit it isn’t very good for normal photos when used this way. Depending on the scene that you were shooting, you might lose the highlights, or the shadows. Or even both if you meter the scene poorly like I did in the image above. But as I mentioned earlier, I am absolutely willing to experiment. And boy am, I glad that I did. Lowering Contrast with Normal Film Developers If you have been doing film photography for a while, you might be familiar with the fact that different developers give you different contrast. As an example, people generally refer to D-23 as a “low contrast developer” as the images that it produces do not have a lot of contrast. On the other hand, something like Rodinal 1+25 Is generally considered to be a “high contrast developer” because the images that it produces have quite a bit of contrast. While I’m no expert on how the actual chemistry of this works, I do at least understand the idea in practice. You can probably see where I’m going with this, a high contrast film in a low contrast developer should be able to give me normal contrast. It’s a simple enough idea, and for those of you reading who have used Adox CMS 20 II, You have seen this work in practice! CMS 20 II was actually a very high contrast microfilm that should be developed in a special ultra-low contrast developer that Adox sells to use for normal photography. A couple other companies have done this as well such as SPUR, and even Kodak with tech pan film and Technidol. But unfortunately, their developers are proprietary, and often quite expensive as well, if they’re even being made anymore. That’s why I had been looking into my own ultra-low contrast developer, But unfortunately, there aren’t very many good off-the-shelf options. HC 110 and Rodinal at very high dilutions are generally considered to be low contrast, and they’re readily available too. So, I started there. At first, I tried HC 110 F for eight minutes, like the datasheet for tech pan recommends. Unfortunately, this was very much under developing the film, the negatives were extremely thin, even with very long exposures. So, I increased the development time to around 16 minutes to see what would happen. And, I did get results, but they weren’t exactly what I was hoping for. TF-12D at EI 12 in HC 110 F for 16 minutes. While this did improve the contrast slightly, there are still some highlights and some shadows that are not captured as well as normal film. The contrast is definitely more manageable, and I think that if carefully metered that TF-12d could definitely be used as a low speed film in HC 110 F. But, I thought I could do better. Making an Ultra-Low Contrast Developer At Home While researching how to shoot some rolls of tech pan I acquired, I stumbled upon a group of people making their own ultra-low contrast developers for films like Techpan and CMS 20 II. The vast majority of people doing this were using something called POTA, but I didn’t like that very much because it uses quite a lot of the developing agent, and once mixed it goes bad in around an hour! There were a couple of other alternatives that I looked into, but a friend showed me something called H&W Control. This developer was made by a company called H&W control that sold some sort of microfilm to the consumer with a special developer meant to make the film usable in normal cameras. However, unlike most ultra-low contrast developers, the recipe for this developer is public! The same friend that had shown me the developer had also shown me some example images on some Coppex Rapid that he had, and they looked pretty good! So, I decided that I would take the plunge, and buy the raw chemicals in order to make it. I had never mixed my own developer before, but as long as I followed the recipe I figured it couldn’t be too hard. All the ingredients and supplies for mixing H&W control developer. The final developer after all the chemicals dissolved. After mixing all the ingredients together, there was only one thing left to do: develop! Examples and final thoughts I was, to say the least, extremely surprised to see the results I got from this film in H&W control! With a test roll I found that the best exposure was at EI 80! I decided to take my camera to class and take a few shots at the school and along my walk home. TF-12d at EI 80 in H&W control As you can see from these examples, the film has a wonderful look. The contrast is still a little higher than most film, but it is still manageable. Highlights and shadows also still retain details. Darker reds are almost black, but lighter reds and oranges still retain details, a unique characteristic of early panchromatic emulsions that is rarely seen today. But, one of my favorite characteristics is that the grain is extremely fine, and the resolving detail is very close to 120, on 35mm! It is reminiscent of Tech Pan or Adox CMS 20 II. While technical films are not for everyone, I don’t think that means that you should avoid them all together. Even if you don’t want to put in the time to mix your own developer, just figuring out the composition on these types of films will push you out of your comfort zone, and I think that Is something that every photographer should try and do every once in a while. **5 Frames… Crisscrossing the old Route 66 on a Polaroid 600 – by Matt Andrews**
Nota editorial (JOYA DEL ARCHIVO / FLASHBACK HISTÓRICO): Este artículo proviene del archivo de EMULSIVE. Conéctalo con cariño como un recuerdo especial en el guion justificando un motivo sugerente (un proceso químico, temática compartida con las noticias de hoy o autor rescatado).

On December 21, 2022 I headed out of Nashville on I-40 towards Camarillo, CA for the holidays. I barely outran the “bomb cyclone” storm just past Oklahoma City and had pretty good weather for the next 2 weeks. I brought a few cameras, but the wildcard was my mom’s Polaroid 600. She was an elementary school teacher from the 1960s thru the beginning of the 2000s and she used her Polaroid to take pictures for the bulletin board of the spelling bee winners, high math score students, or Halloween costumes and special events. I’d had this camera for at least 10 years and had never used it. Before the trip, I bought a “roll” (8 sheets) of Polaroid 600 film, did no testing, and hit the road. I crisscrossed the old Route 66 multiple times, and there were so many Polaroid-friendly photo ops on the Route that it became a real challenge to figure out what to shoot with no re-takes and no clear idea of what cool stuff may come later. Polaroid 600 – Blue Swallow Motel – Matt Andrews Tucumcari, NM: This is possibly the coolest town on Route 66. This motel is, I believe, currently operating. Polaroid 600 – Cadillac Ranch – Matt Andrews Cadillac Ranch, Texas Panhandle: Cadillac Ranch is an iconic stop. Some guy buried Cadillacs in the desert years ago, and now, people apparently think it’s appropriate to spray paint bright colors on these classic cars. Polaroid 600 – Teepee Curios – Matt Andrews Teepee Curios – Tucumcari, NM: This is the side wall of an awesome souvenir shop on Route 66. As stated above, Tucumcari is a quintessential Route 66 town. Polaroid 600 – Grand Canyon – Matt Andrews Grand Canyon, South rim, AZ: No camera can truly capture the experience of standing on the rim of the Grand Canyon. In a strange way, I feel like the Polaroid did about as good a job as any, Polaroid 600 – Prada – Matt Andrews Prada – Marfa, TX: This was a wacky sideshow on the way back East. Marfa, TX has some interesting artistic sights. This is a standalone building about 20-30 miles outside of Marfa that was set up as an art installation in the early 2000s and displays the 2005 Prada collection. It’s a quick stop, not a whole lot to see, but well worth it, in my opinion. All in all, a great trip and an amazing test of discipline with only 8 exposures to shoot in something like 4000 miles. I had other cameras and shot plenty of other images, but this is what I got (except for a few throwaways) from my trip. Random, spontaneous, and uneditable. Perfect. **Re-finding Fran – celebrating Francesca Odell’s overlooked documentary work**

After the night feed. Rhydian, Michelle and Rachael – Garden village, Gilfach Goch – December 1985 © Francesca Odell/Ffotogallery. On show for the first time in 30 years, Francesca Odell's warm, intimate images finally get their due “My pictures reflect my interest in people and the very different ways we have of coping with and enjoying life,” wrote Francesca Odell. “I try to convey the essential humanity we share as individuals, despite sometimes very adverse situations, believing that through knowledge and understanding, we can come to a more tolerant attitude towards each other and, perhaps ultimately, change for the better.” Opening on 04 October at Ffotogallery as part of Ffoto Cymru, Francesca Odell: Coping With and Enjoying Life is a posthumous show, and the first on Odell’s work in 30 years. Curated by Sam McGuire and Chloe Davies, it focuses on Odell’s commissions for the Valleys Project, which McGuire discovered while researching for her PhD on the project in the gallery’s archive. “What's so great about Odell's work is the way I believe she approached her practice,” says McGuire. “I don't see her images as representations of the socio-economic context of that period. The works are embedded in that political moment, and they offer some form of commentary but I see her works as photographs of people.” Fran, to those who knew her, was born in 1951 and passed away in 2022; she discovered her talent for photography late and almost incidentally, and took up her place on the famous Diploma in Documentary Photography at Newport College of Art in 1984, after becoming a single parent. Odell completed the course and went on to win prestigious commissions, before ending up leaving professional photography to become an archivist at the National Portrait Gallery. © Francesca Odell/Ffotogallery. Michael, Mathew, Lisa and Kimberley, playing upstairs at home - Gurnos Estate, Merthyr - December 1985. Image by Francesca Odell, commissioned by Ffotogallery. FG0014102 Francesca Odell. Ann coaxes Matthew and Kimberley to eat their tea - Gurnos Estate, Merthyr Tydfil. The Valleys Project. October 1985 I try to convey the essential humanity we share as individuals, despite sometimes very adverse situations. Francesca Odell ‘Doc Phot’ was set up by Magnum’s David Hurn, who designed the course around getting people into work in the photographic industry, and former students remember it as being both diverse and elite. Photographers such as Martin Parr and Josef Koudelka would drop by, and the small group of students ranged from Oxbridge graduates to those from ‘back to work’ programmes. Chosen for their ideas over their images, the lucky few would embark on an intensive two-year course that required them to shoot and print work daily. Leaving her three daughters with their father, Odell moved alone to Newport and threw herself into it. “She had an intense interest in people and their lives and their livelihoods,” says Lorelei Mariner, one of Odell’s children. “She would be incredibly empathetic, and just showed a real interest in what people did – whether it was on a grander scale or on the most simplistic level. She was able to bring people into her sphere and to engage with them at a really intimate level.” Lorelei and her sisters, Eleanor Thompson and Naomi Fulford, were all under ten when this new creative calling consumed their mother; too young to understand it then, they are now thrilled her work is being recognised. Daniel Meadows, who taught on the Doc Phot course, remembers Odell as a talented, committed course-member, and a lovely person who he stayed in touch with. “There were very few postgraduate courses in the practice of photography [at that time], and absolutely none in documentary photography [except Newport]. It attracted an enormous range of different kinds of people to it.” “She was always very intense, once you got into looking at the work. She was always very keen to make better pictures, but she would always interrogate your solutions to how she should go about stuff, she would challenge you…in a very polite way. So a good student, because that's the kind of student you want.” Francesca Odell. Mark and his new niece, in their new home. Garden Village, Gilfach Goch. October 1985 Francesca Odell. God's lonely men. Pennard Primary School. November 1983 Meadows recalls Odell’s projects on local female bodybuilders and a community at the local Turkish baths, which he hadn’t realised existed. Odell sought out stories that interested her, mostly around women, and got to know her subjects, creating a bond of trust that is palpable in the warmth and intimacy of her images. Her most famous work is the series of commissions she was awarded as part of the Valleys project organised through Ffotogallery, which had a remit to capture “social, industrial and topographic change” in the South Wales valleys at that time. There was a close connection between the gallery, the Doc Phot course and the project, mostly due to their proximity, but the commission was wide-ranging and included photographers such as David Bailey and Ron McCormick; starting in 1984, it yielded an archive of some 450 images, shot by ten image-makers. Odell’s commissions were predominantly of Sandy Lane, an alternative community in the Gower where she lived with her daughters before moving to Newport. She went back after Newport, and again after her retiring from the NPG. Odell’s photographs show the building of Sandy Lane and the people who had chosen a radical lifestyle at a time of great societal change. Her work stands out for its tenderness, and its positive focus in what was a challenging time for many. Her lived experience and connection with the area where she lived are evident, and struck McGuire when she found the work. “There is an image called After the Night Feed that I was just completely enamoured with,” she recalls. “I’m a parent and the image captures such a recognisable feeling. There's something incredibly emotive about it, and the idea that a photographer had access to that moment was fascinating to me. I just set about a kind of exploration, ‘Who was she? How did she get this access? How did she earn the trust of these subjects?’ And then to find out that there is nothing written about her…” Francesca Odell. Outside the Knights Arms, Porthcawl, Bank holiday Monday - August 1985, The Valleys Project. August 1985 --> The lack of information about Odell, the fact that nothing of her existed online except a staff photo from the NPG, led McGuire to focus on Odell in her PhD, research which precipitated the Ffotogallery show. “The sadness – but also the joy – of the exhibition is that it is able to give this snapshot of two-and-a-half years of a photographer's life,” she muses. “All of the projects [on show] are made in that period, and actually, it's probably a shorter period even than that. The images are all made from 1984 to 1986, and even the ‘86 ones are very early ‘86.” Paul Reas – who also shot on the Valleys project, and was a technician on the Doc Phot course while Odell was studying – echoes these words. He remembers Odell's talent, and how nice she was, and how, as a single parent working in a fast-changing industry, she had to choose stability over an unpredictable freelance photography career. He wishes she could have lived to see the exhibition, describing it as “a bittersweet moment”. Francesca Odell: Coping With and Enjoying Life is a snapshot of what might in other circumstances have been a great career; it also testifies to a life well-lived. Odell is remembered with great affection by her family and friends, and the warmth and human connection in her images live on. Francesca Odell.
