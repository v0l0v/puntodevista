**Building Community Frame by Frame with Swiss Film Club**
Fotógrafos: jd, toneklee_

We recently teamed up with Vanessa, the founder of Swiss Film Club, to organize a photo walk in Geneva. Guided by Elnara (@35mmomento ), the group set out to explore various locations, each through their own lens. In this event recap, Vanessa shares about the roots of the Swiss Film Club, growing from a small online curation project into a vibrant community with over 400 members in its main chat group, and how the shift into real-life events happened. Reflecting on her own connection to photography, Vanessa prefers capturing the atmosphere and simplicity of a moment over getting lost in technical details. However, she explains how these different perspectives can bring people together through a shared love for analogue photography. From technical film enthusiasts to casual weekend shooters, Swiss Film Club aims to create an open, low-pressure space to connect and create. © Sandro (@framesbyfio) ,  Shot with LomoChrome Classicolor 35 mm ISO 200 Can you tell us the story behind Swiss Film Club? Who came up with the idea, and how has it grown or shifted since the beginning? Swiss Film Club started in 2021, and it began as just a page that I ran. I was on a train, posting my photos from my latest road trip in Switzerland and trying to get featured by bigger accounts to get a bit more traction. I kept searching for a page dedicated to Swiss film photography, typing every combination I could think of, Swiss, Switzerland, analogue, film, and I could never find one. There were loads of niche film pages out there, @mountainsonfilm was a big one I loved, but nothing for Switzerland. After hitting that wall a few times I thought, right, I will just make it myself and curate the Swiss film photography page I had been looking for. That’s how @swissfilmclub was born! © Sarah (@sarahbatt) ,  Shot with Lomography Color Negative 35 mm ISO 400 For the first couple of years, it stayed exactly that, a curated page where I reposted other people’s work. Nothing tied to events, nothing more than a curated feed. Then in 2023, I moved from Geneva to Zurich, and I realized I was missing a sense of community here and a way to meet people with the same interests. Around then, I met Sarah (@serahwe) through Instagram and Swiss Film Club, and she connected me with a local lab here in Zurich. © Jordan ([](https://www.instagram.com/jd.walker/)@jd.walker) ,  Shot with Lomography Color Negative 35 mm ISO 400 Together with that film lab, we planned the first Film Photo Walk, in January 2024. We had no WhatsApp system back then, no way to track numbers, so we just posted a bunch of Instagram stories and posts and hoped for the best. I was expecting maybe 10 people. Around 50 showed up. That is what kicked off the whole Film Photo Walk story. Since then, I have been a bit all over the place, hosting walks between all corners of Switzerland. I run the page on my own, so for each city I always bring in someone local to help guide the group and draw the route. That is where a lot of the collaboration with people from the community comes in. © Elnara (@35mmomento) ,  Shot with: LomoChrome Color ’92 Sun-kissed 35 mm ISO 400 Please introduce yourself: who are you, and what does film photography mean to you personally? What inspires you? My name is Vanessa. I live in Zurich now but I am originally from Geneva. I picked up film photography a bit randomly years ago, walking around Amsterdam, when I wandered into a photo shop and saw a film camera for sale. It was a Minolta XG1, which I still shoot with today, and I fell for it straight away. “When everyone is shooting film, there is no checking the back of the camera, no editing on the spot, no reshooting 10 times the same shot. You take the photo, keep walking, and talk to the person next to you. You are present.” I had been into digital photography since I was a kid, it was the stuff I always asked for at Christmas. Film, for me, is more about capturing a moment and the nostalgia attached to it than about a perfect photo. It is less about the shot being technically right and more about whether the vibe of the moment made it onto the roll. That is the big difference between digital and film for me. I still shoot digital, and I use digital cameras for work, so I am not closed off to it at all. You will often catch me carrying both a digital and an analogue camera, and depending on what I am doing, I will shoot with one or the other. © Lucy (@footlooselucy) ,  Shot with LomoChrome Purple Pétillant 35 mm ISO 100–400 What inspires me is a lot of the people in the Swiss Film Club community. I get to scroll through that feed every day and pull inspiration from so many talented photographers. I lean towards landscapes, and lately I have caught myself realizing I forget to capture the people I am with. **Deborah Bell Photographs : Wijnanda Deroo : Mexico 1994 : The Cibachromes**

Deborah Bell Photographs presents Mexico 1994: The Cibachromes, an exhibition of vintage color photographs made in 1994 by the Dutch photographer Wijnanda Deroo in Mexico. This is the photographer’s third exhibition with the gallery. Deroo is well known for her meticulously composed photographs of interiors, in which people are usually absent. Her earlier series from the 1980s-early 2000s, such as Amsterdam Hotels, Artists’ Studios, Atlantic City, Brooklyn Night, Curaçao, Disappearing Houses, Lower East Side, Mobile Homes, and New York Eateries, acknowledge human presence without showing a single person. Deroo has continued this approach in her more recent bodies of work, Behind the Walls of the Hermitage(2018-2019) and Out on the Street: The Dining Sheds & Empty Streets of New York, 2020-2024, an exhibition of color photographs from two series made in New York City during the COVID-19 pandemic. As Robert Mann Gallery aptly stated in their press release for an exhibition of Deroo’s work held there in 2004: Deroo is well-versed in the vernacular of the commonplace — there is an echo of human presence in the unremarkable architecture and objects she photographs. … [Her work is] also distinguished by a masterful sense of color, light and composition, allowing deeper themes to rise to the surface: the universality of human environments in the industrialized world, the melancholic beauty of the mundane or deserted, and unexpected links between disparate cultures. The art critic Ken Johnson, writing about Deroo’s work in The New York Times, described her photographs as having a “curious, searching quality…as though she were a detective.” Though her early work from 1980-1995 was primarily in black-and-white, Deroo turned to photographing in color in about 1993. Because her visit to Mexico was made on the cusp of this transition, a selection of large vintage gelatin silver prints of photographs Deroo made in black-and-white on this same trip will also be available for private viewing during the exhibition. Wijnanda Deroo was born in the Netherlands in 1955. She received a liberal arts degree from the Academy of Fine Arts in Arnhem, where she later became a professor. In 1989 Deroo was awarded an artist’s residency at MoMA P.S. 1 in Long Island City, NY. She continues to live and work in New York. Deroo’s photographs have been exhibited internationally since the early 1980s. Her work can be found in the permanent collections of the Bibliothèque Nationale, Paris; Brooklyn Museum, New York; Central Museum, Utrecht; Gemeente Museum, Amsterdam; Groninger Museum, Netherlands; Jewish Museum, New York; LaSalle Bank Photography Collection (Bank of America Collection), Chicago; Los Angeles County Museum of Art; Moderna Museet, Malmö, Sweden; Musée National d’Art Moderne, Centre Georges Pompidou, Paris; Museum Arnhem, Netherlands; Museum of the Jewish Diaspora, Tel Aviv; Museum of Modern Art, New York; Noord Brabants Museum, ‘s-Hertogenbosch, Netherlands; Printenkabinet, University of Leiden, Netherlands; Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam; San Francisco Museum of Modern Art; Stedelijk Museum, Amsterdam, among many other public institutions worldwide. **Journées photographiques du Val d’Ocre : Photo de famille, photo en famille**

The Journées photographiques du Val d’Ocre promote auteur photography, inviting 8 to 10 photographers each year, both established and lesser-known, and focusing on their ability to convey a message and emotions, while also taking into account the formal and technical quality of their work. For this 8th edition, held under the banner of photography’s bicentennial, one theme imposed itself: “Photo de famille, photo en famille”, and its ramifications into other subjects: roots, transmission, adolescence. The photographs of Albert Cibille, Charlotte Audoynaud and Pascal Baudry, along with a group exhibition of family photographs lent by members of the public, are at the heart of this theme. Charlotte Audoynaud weaves family fictions to create enigmatic and magnetic worlds. She also writes poems that travel alongside her images. Her project Bois de lune received support from the Aide à la création en art contemporain – Région Bourgogne-Franche-Comté. Philippe Cibille, a photographer, inherited his father’s photographs. An amateur photographer from the 1950s onward, his father never stopped taking pictures of his family. Today, the son looks at his father looking at his mother, his sisters, his brother… Pascal Baudry pursues a full career while developing “a more personal practice of photography since 2018, at the rhythm of his travels and encounters. Like a logbook; an almost-novel written over the long haul.” Louisa Ben, the daughter of a Franco-Moroccan couple, began making trips back and forth between France and Morocco at around the age of 20, eager to discover a territory that was supposed to be “familiar” to her. She tells us about the construction of an identity, at the crossroads of two cultures, that cannot claim allegiance to any existing flag. Léo d’Oriano immersed himself in the daily life of young people in Joigny who were, at the time of the Covid pandemic, at the age when the future adult is taking shape — a pandemic that “battered” the development of these teenagers. The project was carried out as part of the major national commission orchestrated by the Bibliothèque nationale de France (BnF), “Radioscopie de la France: regards sur un pays traversé par la crise sanitaire” (2021/2022). Natalya Saprunova introduces us to the Saami people, a formerly nomadic people of the tundra in northwestern Russia, in the Arctic, forcibly settled in the 1920s. They strive to preserve their culture while adapting to climate change. Noémie Lecampion gives us an opportunity to question “difference” with a documentary series on autistic adults. The work was awarded a Special Mention by the Bourse photojournaliste Laurent Troude in 2026. Anne Nguyen Dao presents the sequel and conclusion of the series “Quel est ton arbre?” shown in 2025, at the end of which another question arises: “Do we all have an inner tree?” In partnership with ObrigADO, a report by l’Oiseau à ressorts on the furniture-customizing activity of teenagers and children from the Montholon and Valravillon leisure centers. A meeting place between photographers and audiences, the Journées photographiques offer, as an extension of the exhibitions, illustrated talks by the photographers. These allow them to share their approaches and their ways of working, and allow the public to engage directly with the photographers. This year’s speakers are Natalya Saprunova (Saamis, nous vivions dans la Toundra) and Léo d’Oriano (Ils en avaient 16). Tina Mérandon will also speak, offering an original perspective on the works as a whole and on family photography. Tina Mérandon is a photographer and a member of the Agence VU’. She “explores the relationship to the other. Everything is linked to the body, to the skin, to gesture, whether animal or human.” The relationship between humans and animals becomes central. Tina Mérandon is also a lecturer at Sorbonne Paris 1, in the photography option. **The International Center of Photography : I’m So Happy You Are Here : Japanese Women Photographers from the 1950s to Now**

L’International Center of Photography (ICP) présente I’m So Happy You Are Here: Japanese Women Photographers from the 1950s to Now, une exposition organisée par Aperture qui met en lumière l’œuvre extraordinaire et variée de vingt-huit artistes de plusieurs générations, du Japon de l’après-guerre à nos jours. Cet ensemble d’œuvres historiques et contemporaines de femmes photographes japonaises— dont beaucoup ont été reconnues pour leur contribution, tandis que d’autres sont restées sans réelle reconnaissance publique— entrelace trois grands motifs: des observations du quotidien à la fois délicates et frontales; des regards critiques sur les rôles sociaux qu’occupent les Japonaises et qu’elles réinterprètent souvent; et des expérimentations autour de la forme photographique et de ses prolongements. Ensemble, ces œuvres jettent les bases d’une étude, d’une exposition et d’une célébration durables de la contribution des Japonaises à l’histoire de la photographie. Sous le commissariat de Lesley A. Martin, directrice exécutive de Printed Matter; Takeuchi Mariko, critique de photographie, commissaire d’exposition et professeure à l’Université des arts de Kyoto; et Pauline Vermare, Phillip and Edith Leonian Curator, Photography, au Brooklyn Museum, I’m So Happy You Are Here: Japanese Women Photographers from the 1950s to Now marque la première présentation aux États-Unis d’une exposition de référence, née du livre éponyme publié par Aperture en 2024 et de l’exposition qui l’accompagnait aux Rencontres d’Arles. Depuis, l’exposition a voyagé au Fotomuseum Den Haag, à La Haye (Pays-Bas); au Fotografie Forum Frankfurt (Allemagne); à The Photographers’ Gallery, à Londres (Royaume-Uni); et, plus récemment, au Hikarie Hall, à Tokyo (Japon), organisée par Bunkamura. Après son passage à l’ICP, l’exposition se poursuivra dans d’autres lieux à l’international. I’m So Happy You Are Here: Japanese Women Photographers from the 1950s to Now présente des œuvres de Fujioka Aya, Hara Mikiko, Hiromix, Ishikawa Mao, Ishiuchi Miyako, Katayama Mari, Kawauchi Rinko, Komatsu Hiroko, Kon Michiko, Nagashima Yurie, Narahashi Asako, Ninagawa Mika, Nishimura Tamiko, Noguchi Rika, Nomura Sakiko, Okabe Momo, Okanoue Toshiko, Onodera Yuki, Sawada Tomoko, Shiga Lieko, Sugiura Kunié, Suzuki Nozomi, Tawada Yuki, Tokiwa Toyoko, Ushioda Tokuko, Watanabe Hitomi, Yamazawa Eiko et Yanagi Miwa. «L’ICP est ravi de célébrer les contributions extraordinaires de ces vingt-huit artistes au domaine de la photographie», a déclaré Bob Jeffery, directeur général de l’ICP. «Grâce aux ressources conjuguées de l’ICP et d’Aperture, ce partenariat nous permet de présenter à New York un ensemble d’œuvres novateur, qui repousse les limites, et qui est montré pour la première fois aux États-Unis.» Depuis les années 1970, la photographie japonaise fait l’objet de nombreuses publications et expositions internationales. Si la reconnaissance s’est surtout portée sur les photographes masculins, d’innombrables femmes ont joué un rôle essentiel dans le développement du médium, même si leurs pratiques restent largement sous-représentées et sous-estimées, au Japon comme à l’étranger. Ce panorama réparateur présente un large éventail de styles et d’approches formelles employés par ces photographes pour répondre aux expériences et aux points de vue des femmes dans la société japonaise de manière plus générale, tout en mettant en valeur leur créativité à travers des images emblématiques, des livres de photographie et des magazines, ainsi que des exemples d’installation, de sculpture et de vidéo. En apportant un contrepoint nécessaire et critique à notre compréhension de l’histoire de la photographie japonaise, I’m So Happy You Are Here: Japanese Women Photographers from the 1950s to Now offre également un élargissement saisissant de l’histoire de la photographie dans son ensemble. L’ampleur historique de l’exposition montre comment la photographie a servi, au Japon, à la fois à appréhender et à interroger la transformation rapide de la vie dans tout le pays. Qu’il s’agisse de la reconstruction de l’après-Seconde Guerre mondiale et de l’occupation par l’armée des États-Unis, de l’entrée dans le débat public de questions politiques telles que le mouvement de libération des femmes— qui prônait une refonte radicale de la société patriarcale japonaise— ou de l’essor de l’industrie de la culture pop, les photographes élaboraient de nouvelles thématiques et de nouvelles stratégies formelles pour affronter le sol mouvant sous leurs pieds. Loin de simplement faire partie de l’industrie et de la culture grandissantes qui entouraient le médium, les femmes photographes ont joué un rôle crucial dans le développement et la popularité de la photographie au Japon durant cette période, en particulier en ce qui concerne la photographie couleur et la légitimation d’approches critiques de la marchandisation du corps des femmes, de l’autoportrait, de la performance, entre autres. Cette exposition s’inscrit dans l’héritage des efforts déjà déployés par l’ICP pour mettre en lumière des artistes japonais dans le contexte américain, notamment une exposition majeure organisée à peine cinq ans après sa fondation. **The International Center of Photography : Laia Abril : On Rape and Institutional Failure**

The International Center of Photography (ICP) presents On Rape and Institutional Failure, an exhibition by Laia Abril that centers on the second chapter of her ongoing project A History of Misogyny, which examines different forms of violence against women throughout history. Weaving together images, compiled testimonies, archival materials, select objects and installations with sound and video, the exhibition investigates how gender-based stereotypes, coupled with worldwide prejudices and misconceptions, have contributed to a systemic rape culture that persists today. By focusing on institutions, Abril aims to avoid narratives of victim-blaming, instead addressing the ongoing problem of social accountability that has allowed offenders and enablers alike to avoid justice. On Rape and Institutional Failure was first exhibited in 2020 in the wake of the initial, galvanizing allegations against Harvey Weinstein and other powerful men. While #MeToo has led to few sustained consequences and little systemic change, Abril offers a meaningful opportunity to address the long history of silencing victims and the transgenerational trauma that has developed as a result. While continuously emerging stories of sexual violence and institutional failures maintain the urgency and relevance of Abril’s project, it began with research into the history of systemic responses to sexual violence after a 2018 case in her native Spain: five men raped a young woman and were later released on bail. Ultimately, the men were charged with sexual harassment, which carried a lighter sentence. Video footage filmed by the perpetrators was used to claim the victim had consented. These events sparked the largest feminist protest in the country’s history, one that called for Spain’s sexual assault laws to be rewritten. Occurring at the height of the #MeToo movement and widespread, global protests against the many systems and institutions hostile to women, Abril wanted to understand why failures to deliver justice on this scale occur, and how traditional assumptions, cultural influences, myths and laws serve to shield or even encourage offenders, whether they be common citizens or those holding positions of power. Abril’s practice is always grounded in research. On Rape and Institutional Failure investigates historical and contemporary narratives from disciplines including law, history, psychology and politics. This research led to her discovery of maps, architecture, sculptures, medical devices, weapons and many other objects related to sexual violence throughout history. Presented in this exhibition as straightforward photographs Abril calls ‘conceptual portraits,’ these objects visualize laws and customs which contributed to rape culture over time. One key series of images, shown alongside first-person testimony, depicts garments which serve as stand-ins for institutions. These uniforms symbolize all of the victims and survivors past, present and future that an institution betrays. Together with the video, sound and installation components, these works switch the focus from victims to institutions, exposing the structures of justice which fail survivors of sexual violence and in the process reaffirm a culture of impunity for the perpetrators. **How I Organize My Film Negatives, Scans and Darkroom Prints**

“It sometimes seems as if curbing entropy is our quixotic purpose in the universe,” wrote science-historian James Gleick. When it comes to organizing film photos, entropy runs rife—and so does quixotism. I’ve been shooting film for 15 years, since my dad gave me his old Minolta SLR . And for the last 8 years, I’ve also been developing and printing my photos in my makeshift home darkroom. Over the years, I’ve accumulated lots of film negatives, scans and darkroom prints. I’ve also developed a system for organizing them, which I thought I would share here on 35mmc. Argenti Scale-X, reversal processed. Developed at home. I shoot both 35mm and medium-format film. The black-and-white film gets developed at home, but I send my colour film to a lab (I don’t shoot as much colour film, so I haven’t invested in C-41 chemicals). In the darkroom, I print only B&W. Some of my darkroom prints are gifted or hung on the wall, but mostly they go into my photo albums. At first I used to keep my prints in a cardboard box, but I found that I rarely looked at them again. For me, an album is much more pleasurable to leaf through, and also for showing my prints to other people. Some of my darkroom prints, shot in Kolkata, Munich and London I think of my film photos as existing in three separate forms, in three different archives: (a) negatives and transparencies, (b) scans, and (c) prints. The guiding principle behind my system is that given an item from any one of these three archives, I should be able to easily locate it in the other two . So for example, if I’m looking at a print in one of my albums, I should be able to find the corresponding negative and scan. A second principle is that the system should not take too much effort to maintain . Developing, scanning and printing are already a lot of work, and knowing myself, if the system involves too much effort and friction, it will eventually break down. Finally, a third principle is that the archives should include some basic info, such as year, camera and film stock . Scans The scans are the first thing I archive. Colour film generally gets scanned by the same lab where I develop it. But B&W film—which I shoot more of—gets developed at home, and “scanned” with a digital camera. I use pixl-latr as a film-holder and diffuser, and a diffused off-camera flash as backlight, placed inside an Ikea recycling bin. The photo below shows my home scanning setup (I’ve written about it in more detail on the pixl-latr blog ). Once I have the scans from a roll of film, I put them in a folder on my computer. I name the folder as follows: [format]-[year]-[serial] ([camera], [film], [developer or lab]) . So my folders have names like: 35mm-22-08 (Leica M3, Kodak Double-X, ID-11) or: MF-26-04 (Rolleiflex, Fuji Pro 160NS, Whampoa) In this scheme, [format] is either 35mm or MF (for medium format). [serial] is the ordinal number for that year (so in my 35mm example, it’s my 8th roll from 2022). Then I have the camera and film, and the last field is either the developer (if I developed it at home) or the name of the lab. For my film scans, I follow the 3-2-1 backup rule : keep three total copies of your data, on two different storage media types, with one copy stored off-site. My primary copy of my film scans lives on my computer hard drive. Every few months, I back them up to (a) an external SSD, and (b) to Mega cloud storage . Negatives Once I’ve finished scanning a roll of film—or collected it from the lab as the case may be—I put the negatives in a clear acetate sleeve, and file it in a binder. In each sleeve, I include a slip of paper with the same “metadata” that’s in the folder name. Some labs, like Whampoa Colour Centre in Singapore, also provide a digital contact sheet which is super helpful, and which I file along with the negatives. The only exception is slide film which I mount and store in a box, also with the same info. We have a projector at home (Zeiss Ikon Perkeo), which is why I like to mount my slides. But if you don’t plan to project your slide film photos, I guess they can go in a binder too. Prints Most of my darkroom prints go into Hama photo albums . In keeping with the analogue theme, I typewrite the captions and “metadata” in my albums. My typewriter is a Japanese-made Royal 202, which is a joy to use. I got it secondhand from an retired lady in Singapore, who said she used it to type her first job application. She was happy when I told her that I plan to use it; she had heard that “young people just use typewriters as decoration.” I generally have just one photo per page, mounted with transparent photo corners. On the right page, I have the photo, along with the caption, place and year. Right page: photo and caption I think of the right page of the album as what “normal” people are likely to be interested in. The left page is for the nerds. Accordingly, I include more info about the photo—similar to what I have in the folder names on my hard drive, but here I also note the lens (if I used an interchangeable camera). Left page: metadata Left page: mini contact sheet, metadata and test strip At bottom right is the test-strip which I always make in the process of making a darkroom print. If you’re not familiar with darkroom work, the purpose of a test-strip is to dial in the exposure and contrast for the final print. I use localised test-strips and F-stop printing, described in an earlier post . I also include a mini contact sheet (top left) with just three frames—the photo which I printed, and the two frames immediately before and after. I like this because it often shows a sequence in time. Just before I shot Kids by the Muslim Cemetery , I walked across railway lines, and just after, there’s another shot of the same kids. The contact sheet is a “proper proof”, i.e. printed at SPT (also known as minimum time to maximum black) at Grade 2 (standard contrast). If you’re not familiar with these terms and want to dig deeper, check out my article about 3 ways to make contact sheets . The proper proof is the closest we can get to an “unedited” darkroom print. Using creative printing techniques, a negative which was, say, underexposed or overdeveloped can still be made to look good in a darkroom print. But a proper proof shows us what it “really” looks like. For example, although the print of Yuwei at Mudchute Farm (above) looks good, you can see from the contact sheet (below) that the original negative was extremely overexposed. In fact, I initially thought it was unprintable, but Kodak TMax has incredible latitude. It’s good to have the proper proof as reference, because it helps me spot my own mistakes (I was shooting without a light-meter and just guessed the exposure, which was clearly off), and also if I want to print the same negative again. Tying it all together The key to the system is the indexing. I can look at a print in my album, and the label will tell me that it’s from, say, 35mm-21-09. It’s then very easy to locate the corresponding negatives (which are filed sequentially in my binders), or the scans (which have the index number in the folder name). Similarly, if I’m looking at scans, the folder name tells me how to find the corresponding negative, and vice versa. It also doesn’t take too much effort—mainly just renaming the folder after scanning, inserting an info-slip in each negative sleeve, and typing out a similar slip for my album. If anything, the most tedious part is cutting out the typewritten slips and pasting them into the album. But the typing itself is super fun, which makes up for it. For further organizing, browsing and searching my film scans, I use a couple of free DAM (digital asset management) tools. For example, I recently wanted to make an Instagram post featuring film photos of stray dogs of Kolkata , so my DAM software (Excire Foto Light) helped me search for dog photos across my various film-photo folders. I also edit the JPEG metadata, which originally just have the name of the scanning device, to reflect the actual film camera and film stock that were used. For this, I use an ExifTool GUI which I coded myself. This is probably very niche (or even borderline-obsessive) territory, but if you want to know more about my post-scan DAM workflow, let me know, and I can write a separate post about that. Obviously, I’m not saying that this is the One Right Way to archive your film photos. It may be too simple for some people, and too much effort for others. Hamish infamously bins his negatives , though I don’t know if he has since mended his ways [ Editor’s note: I haven’t ]. I’m just sharing my own process—and if you find some inspiration, or want to share your own tips or process, I’d love to hear. Share this post:

The Burkina Faso artist’s later work reveals the changing ethos and sociopolitical shifts in West African photography When asked why The Photographers’ Gallery is displaying later colour works by the Burkinabe studio photographer Sanlé Sory – and why now – TPG curator Taous Dahmani’s answer is clear and direct: “Because I’m here now.” “I had always wanted to think about the long tradition of West African studio photography as a global surface,” she adds. Fed up with dominant readings, Dahmani argues that these photographs require a relocation of function if they are to be understood in more generative, restorative ways. The job of photographer is political, artistic, often polemic and gestural; it is also, fundamentally, a job. It is labour. It is a means to get paid and a way to live one’s life, void of a punch card and with freedom to roam. Photography is a history of grafting, of making ends meet, of waking up and going to work. Dahmani looks away from the recent landscape of “revived” West African photographic exhibitions, literally blown up for the Western art powers that be, and instead sees them as they are: small and intimate. And that is enough. Perhaps ‘fine art’ – whatever that means – is a poisoned chalice, a trapdoored pedestal, a gilded cage. Instead, Sory is here a master craftsman, but one looking to get paid rather than necessarily disrupt the canon. This curse of fine art, which fixes Sory’s oeuvre and character into something discovered, demanding recontextualisation, and subsequent elevation, in turn shuts a door into the modern world. --> “[Sory’s later colour work] holds quite a significant shift in what we know about the history of West African practice” – Taous Dahmani, curator Reductive readings often position this sort of photographer, and by extent photography, as a historical event over a living being. Dahmani says this limited reading “refuses entry into contemporality”, and perpetuates Western art narratives as “acceptable forms of nostalgia about this past of liberation, a call to the time ‘when we liberated poor Africans’”. On the two far sides of the Atlantic, and deep in the white cube, when we see West African studio portraits, it is always revolutionary times. Nothing ever changes, Africa is Africa, and the West is the West. But Sory’s archive holds “thousands and thousands” of later colour negatives, many of which have never been printed beyond their original commission. Dahmani asks why and steers towards a relocation, a “re-anchoring” in her terms. Perhaps these colour works, a move on from the earlier black-and-white work of the 1960s and 70s, were deemed harder to digest for Western audiences, something less fixed than the story we know (or have been told). To Dahmani, Burkina Faso, and the wider African stage, is not a fixed event but a site to examine universal photographic systems, alongside the specificities of 20th-century African histories. “[Sory’s later colour work] holds quite a significant shift in what we know about the history of West African practice,” she says. She reads the practice as both ubiquitous and unique; closer to the functional, political and social histories caught within the portraits, and understood as wholly commissioned artefacts. Yet by curating the show in London there is an awareness that home is not always a place one can return to, that there is perhaps no true contextual providence left. Instead there is a possibility to seek out new lands, ways of being and seeing, ones which bring the photographs to audiences new and old, but allow all those looking to see for themselves. The Western art reading has a tendency to turn African nations into islands, dislocating them from neighbouring, concurrent histories. Dahmani refers to this as a “dictated, fetishised history”, one the market embraces with ease but which brings us no closer to understanding these images or people. “For me, colour became the starting point,” she says. “Can we think critically about this history through the lens of colour? Can we think of it as a form of politics? Most of the pictures we know are from the 1960s and 70s. Postcolonial moments of aspiration, of joy… at least that’s how it’s sold. Moments of ‘Finally, the freedom of the African people!’ But it stops there. No one wonders what happens after.” --> Sory’s later portraits of street vendors, personable and direct, grew out of a slowing appetite for commissioned portraits. Cameraphones had arrived and times were changing. Dahmani also stresses the desires of the later sitters he was commissioned to record, commenting on their input into the images. “Sory printed in black-and-white, but when colour became more accessible, clients asked for it. It was more expensive, and he suddenly couldn’t work alone.” For Dahmani, both sitter and printer become producer and collaborator. Dahmani traces stories of the sitters through historical relocations; every now and then you notice someone dressed fancifully for the camera, who is “wearing a suit slightly too small, their shoes maybe unvarnished”. Through these images Dahmani moves away from a fashion-first reading towards something more akin to socially informed style studies, an analysis of hand-me-downs and counterfeit designerwear. This becomes part of the re-anchoring, and perhaps a new type of non-hierarchical photographic kinship. It aids an understanding of universality, not exotic idiosyncrasy, though a story that is also deeply African. Sory had to negotiate and barter with his subjects, as ultimately the images existed off their payments. He had to follow trends, keep up with the changing times. Suddenly people did not want his famed illustrated backdrops any longer. “They wanted to be part of a new technology, and a new capacity of being seen,” Dahmani explains. Sory, as with many West African portraitists, is rarely discussed within the contexts of an emerging contemporary. Entry into the modern world, with colour, Nike, Obama T-shirts, and iPhones, has not been curatorially permitted. “I now don’t have to prove that photography is an art form,” Dahmani states. “But I do want to get back to the fact that actually, historically, there were things that we were too quick to put into one category, instead of allowing them to be a multiplicity of things. And I don’t think we often allow African artists to be a multiplicity of things.” Sanlé Sory: Colour Years is at The Photographers’ Gallery, London, from 14 October 2026 to 21 February 2027.
