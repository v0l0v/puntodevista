**“Handover” by Pig Iron Works**

Founded in 2024 by photographers Fergus Riley and Finnegan Travers, Pig Iron Works is a collaborative publishing venture that aims to use photography as a tool for connection and community, creating space for human-driven work to exist outside digital channels of distribution. Essentially, their goal is to offer an escape from the online world. As Travers shared with us: “It’s heart breaking to work in print and be apart of darkroom culture, then to see the work sat only on instagram. Days go into making images. I often think the darkroom becomes a church and its followers who like making faces in the dark join in silence and converse over colour casts. I’m sure everyone knows this feeling when they see something in the real world vs on their phone curled up in a ball with eyes burning—its just more meaningful and we really wanted to push the group publication aspect of this in hopes we can at the very least talk more about making things collectively and bringing work back into the physical world.” Their first publication with designers SM Studio, “Handover” is a visual conversation between photography communities in London and Manchester. The book brings together 27 photographers to showcase the kind of work that might otherwise not be seen. Split into three chapters—‘Even Seeing a Rainbow in the City is Rare’, ‘Lust and Laundry’, and ‘The Fable’—which contributors were asked to respond to, the result is a passing of personal perspective and interpretation between people and place.

**ESPACE MVG : Our Men In Havana : Michael von Graffenried, Emanuel Bovet, Luc Chessex**

“Reality in our century is not something to be faced.” – Graham Greene, Our Man in Havana ESPACE MVG presents Our Men In Havana. This exhibition brings together photographs taken in Cuba by Michael von Graffenried, Emanuel Bovet and Luc Chessex, at different moments in the island’s recent history. Three photographers, three temporalities, three vantage points which, set side by side, sketch less a portrait of Cuba than a fragmentary history of its representations. The title, Our Men In Havana, borrows from Graham Greene’s novel Our Man in Havana, published in 1958. The shift from singular to plural moves the figure of the agent here toward that of the photographer. Both observe, gather clues, interpret and transmit. But photography never delivers reality without mediation: it necessarily offers a point of view, situated in a time, a place and an experience. At the start of the 1990s, the collapse of the Soviet Union brutally upended Cuba’s economic balance. Deprived of a major trading partner and economic support, the island entered a profound crisis that the Cuban government came to call the Período Especial. Shortages of food and fuel, power cuts, transport difficulties, and deteriorating living conditions transformed daily life for the population. It was in this context that Michael von Graffenried travelled to Cuba in the 1990s. He was less interested in the official representations of the revolution than in their concrete consequences in everyday life. He photographed Havana, its streets, its residents, the queues, the movement of people, and places of sociability, but also the persistent presence of political symbols in public space. His images thus record a moment when Cuba’s revolutionary narrative confronted a particularly difficult economic reality. His approach belongs to a documentary practice attentive to political and social situations and to the way these manifest themselves in ordinary life. In Cuba, his eye turns to bodies, shops, infrastructure, streets, and the relationships between individuals — so many signs through which a historic crisis becomes visible. Von Graffenried also extends this work from Miami, where the large Cuban exile community offers another vantage point on the island’s history. His photographs, taken between Miami and Havana, thus set side by side two territories deeply linked by migration, exile, and the consequences of the Cuban revolution. In Our Men in Havana, this body of work is in dialogue with photographs by Emanuel Bovet and Luc Chessex, as well as images by René Burri from the MVG Collection. Bovet’s work focuses in particular on the Cuban music scene, while Chessex brings the perspective of a photographer who lived on the island from 1961 onward. Burri’s photographs — among them his images of Che Guevara taken in Havana in 1963 — situate this ensemble within a longer photographic history of Cuba. Presented today in Our Men in Havana, Michael von Graffenried’s Cuban work thus bears witness to a moment of rupture: that of an island confronted, after the disappearance of the Soviet Union, with a major economic and social crisis. Revisited more than thirty years later, these images of the Período Especial take on a particular resonance and invite us to view Cuba’s present in light of this history. Having arrived in Cuba in 1961, Luc Chessex remained there for fourteen years. His work thus accompanies the early years of the Cuban Revolution from a singular position, at once foreign and deeply immersed. Several decades later,Emanuel Bovet turns his gaze to the Cuban music scene and those who keep it alive, while Michael von Graffenried photographs the island in the 1990s, in the midst of the Período Especial, a period marked by profound economic and social transformations. Echoing these perspectives, photographs of Che Guevara taken in Havana in 1963 by René Burri, from the COLLECTION MVG, are also presented in the exhibition. Brought together today, these images make no claim to offer an exhaustive history of Cuba. Rather, they bring out continuities, gaps and persistences: bodies, gestures, political signs, urban spaces and scenes of everyday life. From one era to the next, Havana presents itself as a territory where collective history surfaces within ordinary experience. Born in 1957 in Bern, the photographer Michael von Graffenried comes from a photojournalism background and today devotes himself to long-term projects using a range of media. He has published several books, including Soudan, une guerre oubliée (1995), Nus au Paradis (1997), Algérie, Photographies d’une guerre sans images (1998), Cocainelove (2005) and Our Town (Steidl 2021). In 2006, Michael von Graffenried received the insignia of Knight of the French Order of Arts and Letters, and in 2010 the Erich Salomon Prize of the German Society for Photography. Emanuel Bovet (born in 1957 in Chamalières) is a photographer and video artist based in Paris. Since 1981, he has been developing a body of work attentive to human situations and the realities of everyday life. A traveller, he builds his projects through encounters and territories, bringing to the world a gaze at once documentary, intimate and poetic. Through photography and film, his work explores the paradoxes and experiences that shape individual and collective lives. Luc Chessex (born in 1936 in Lausanne) is a Swiss photographer. After studying at the École de photographie de Vevey, he left for Cuba in 1961, where he remained for fourteen years. He worked in particular for the Cuban Ministry of Culture, served as photo editor of the magazine Cuba Internacional and became a correspondent for the Prensa Latina agency. His time in Cuba constitutes a major chapter of his photographic work. Returning to Switzerland in 1975, he continued a documentary practice attentive to political and social realities. His publications include Quand il n’y a plus d’Eldorado (1982), devoted to Latin America, Swiss Life (1987) and Around the World (1999). **Archives : Brad Walls : Detached, in Harmony**

Archives – September 23, 2021 Award-winning aerial photographer Brad Walls has released a new series titled ‘Detached, in Harmony’, drawing inspiration from the pandemic, as well as from a renowned 40s fashion photographer. Audiences are invited to experience the first-of-its-kind conceptual art series from above. Walls, widely known for his ‘Pools From Above’ series, first set his sights on creating conceptual photography series when he noticed the distinct lack of aerial-based photography in the genre. As he explains, “Conceptual photography is mostly shot on handheld cameras, but I wanted to showcase the value of an alternate viewpoint to convey a meaningful story.” In creating ‘Detached, in Harmony’, Walls wanted to harness nuance and meticulous attention to detail to mirror the state of the world during the pandemic. Like many, the artist had experienced how distant and repetitive life had become, and placed a keen focus on this element in his work. Through his take on the topic, Walls presents these concepts using repetition, symmetry of figures, and negative space throughout the series – trademark compositing techniques that can be observed throughout Walls’s greater body of work. Each frame within the series is carefully arranged, placing the model at even lengths and highlighting the shadow cast to highlight our solitude and the lurking darkness of the era. Walls’s choice to have the models posed mid-movement emphasises the uniformity and surrealism of the series. As he explains, “The figures are purposefully static, to symbolise how we have been frozen in time over the past 18 months.” Walls also draws inspiration from 40s fashion photographer Clifford Coffin’s ‘Models Sitting on Sand Dunes’. Walls’ himself is deeply inspired by Coffins, who likewise like himself pushes the boundaries of traditional photography. The artist displays a melancholic sense of loneliness in his series, and balances these emotions with warmer hues, similarly found in Coffins’ work as Walls states “We all belong to something that separates us. It’s a sort of melancholic irony”. ‘Detached, in Harmony’ placed second in the 2021 Fine Art Photography Awards ‘Conceptual’ category, and placed silver in both the prestigious PX3 awards and Moscow Fine Art Awards of 2021. The connection between Los Angeles and Cavaillon lends the exhibition a particular resonance. **A Tour of Franco Summa’s Studio – One (plus Two) Shot Story**

Franco Summa (1938-2020) was an Italian architect and artist born in Pescara, Italy, where he realised most of his artistic vision. Summa’s works have been showcased in two Biennale di Venezia (1976 and 1978) and many other locations, in Italy and abroad, and are now preserved by a foundation in his name , based in his studio —now officially acknowledged by the Ministry of culture as a Casa-Museo (Home-Museum)— where I shot these photos. He aimed to explore the relationship between humans and urban space, a concept that was realised in a series of temporary installations such as the Porta del mare ” (The Sea Door) and Monumenti urbani (Urban Monuments), of which this photo captures a peculiar reproduction, that has become a work in itself. One of Summa’s most iconic works is Le fanciulle d’Abruzzo (Abruzzo’s Young Ladies), a series of twelve monumental statues which depict an idealised image of young women from the Abruzzo region wearing traditional clothing. They have also been reproduced in a smaller size, and are on display at the Foundation’s premises. Although it may looks deliberate, the work captured by this next photo is not made by Summa as it actually doesn’t exist. In fact, the photo frames the reflection of two paintings located in front of a small mirror and the result can only be seen looking into the mirror from a specific angle. Thus, the obvious challenge was finding a point of view that would allow the reflection of the paintings to be framed without the photographer appearing in the image. The composition is not perfect because I would have preferred to frame the paintings dead centre of the oval mirror, however, no matter how hard I tried, this was the only angle from which I could shoot without appearing in the photo. Since the museum is not far from the Pescara Airport, if you have a few hours to spare while waiting for a connecting flight Summa’s studio is definitely worth a visit. — I shot with a Panasonic FZ100 and the photo of Le Fanciulle was post-produced with AI to fix a focus issue. **What We Saw: Lake Como Design Festival**

The festival takes on a tangible form when the programme intertwines with the places, objects and people that bring it to life. Over the nine days spent between Como and Lecco, the theme ‘Confine’ found expression above all through tangible elements, such as materials, buildings and the way in which objects occupy spaces. The 2026 edition extended the event to Lecco and local educational institutions for the first time, integrating the event’s geography into the overall project. Among the most notable aspects was the Contemporary Design Selection, hosted this year in the neoclassical rooms of Villa Saporiti. The contrast between container and content was clearly evident. Pastina’s Soft Monuments transformed the visual weight of monumental sculpture into a padded, tactile volume, whilst Yelyzaveta Rudenok’s Soft Structure Chair softened the rigidity of the wood through kerf cutting. Elsewhere, the boundary took on a cultural dimension. In her work To Read A Pattern, Marie Vilay integrates Laotian weaving motifs into contemporary production processes, whilst Threads of Origin by Vittorio Di Girolamo and Xenia Kwiatkowska draws on the Amazigh textile traditions of Morocco to explore themes of transmission and belonging. The Long Take by The Happy Few, on the other hand, translates the experience of walking through Shanghai into a carpet, bringing together fragments of the street and urban perceptions. Among the most significant works are Sedia 2 by Mateusz Wappa, a pine structure almost overrun by rough branches of birch, beech and hazel; Constellation by Marijke De Cock, in which beads and ornamental techniques become structures in space; and Porous Boundaries by Barbara Munsel, hand-woven tapestries that visitors do not merely observe but walk through. Olive Tree Console &amp; The Black Chair by Gerard Kuijpers and Schneidersitz by VON DENEN were also based on contrasts: organic material and steel in the former, traditional construction and a minimalist language in the latter. At Villa del Grumello, Future in the Past approached the same idea from another direction, treating the archive not as something static, but as material that can still be activated through design. Historical references were placed back into circulation: Lupo Borgonovo’s Disegno Nomade enlarged the forms of ancient Eurasian buckles into drawings, etchings and a woven jute rug, while ETEL’s presentation of Lina Bo Bardi brought furniture into dialogue with images of Casa de Vidro and SESC Pompéia. AG Fronzoni’s archive, shown alongside his Collezione 64 for Cappellini, made this relationship even more direct: here, the past was not reconstructed, but allowed to remain useful. Outside these spaces, the festival demonstrated how objects can transform their surroundings. In the former church of Santa Caterina, the Fine Comune project by Lobby Nomade and Ultrapresent Studio softened the verticality of the religious setting, steering it towards a more horizontal and communal dimension. Acerbis’s Free System and Roberto Sironi’s Hypercode rugs for cc-tapis transformed the nave into a temporary space intended for collective use. In Lecco, Eterno Femminino at the Polytechnic explored the boundary between archetype and stereotype. At Grumello, Nella Natura: storie di chi ha scelto un’altra vita, curated by Paola Manfredi with photographs by Selene Magnolia Gatti, presented six stories of people who have opted for a life between the city and the wilderness. The most effective projects did not seek to represent the theme of ‘Confine’, but made it tangible: an element to sit on, to cross, to touch or to observe from a different perspective.

**Inside Studio Portaluppi: Architecture, culture and making**

Some architectural works, once they have gone down in history, may seem untouchable. Studio Portaluppi could well have fallen into this category. After two years behind closed doors, Piero Portaluppi’s historic Milanese studio is reopening in a new form. The premises at 5 Via Morozzo della Rocca return with a different approach to conservation: it is not a question of freezing the past, but rather of allowing it to generate new possibilities. The historic studio, designed by Piero Portaluppi and completed in 1939, has become the new headquarters of Filippo Taidelli Architecture and, at the same time, of Studio Portaluppi, a transdisciplinary laboratory curated by Julia Koropoulos. Taidelli, Portaluppi’s great-grandson, has intervened whilst avoiding both a philological reconstruction and a contemporary gesture aimed at creating contrast. The result appears more nuanced. In the images, the old and the new seem almost to overlap: the walnut panelling meets metal structures, white surfaces and a new grid of light; the openings are transformed into frames; the greenery of the garden visually extends into the interiors. It is a form of architecture composed of subtle shifts, in which distinguishing what dates from 1939 from what belongs to 2026 is not always straightforward. And this is probably the central aspect. In the meeting room, the polychrome marble floor comprises over five hundred pieces sourced from quarries across the globe, some of which are now impossible to find. Portaluppi had also conceived this surface as a sample display for clients. At its centre stands the walnut table, reconstructed according to the original drawings. Viewed from above, the floor evokes the forms of a modernist collage, where the finish becomes an atlas of colours, origins and periods. In other parts of the project, more playful elements emerge. Portholes, passageways, openings and hidden mechanisms echo the ironic and almost mechanical quality already present in Portaluppi’s imagination. Even the air-raid shelter, once forgotten beneath the garden and rediscovered thanks to historical drawings, has been reintegrated into the life of the studio and made accessible via a metal drawbridge integrated into the flooring. The reopening of the space stands out for its value beyond mere restoration. Following two years of renovation, the former private office of Piero Portaluppi now houses Filippo Taidelli Architecture, while the entrance hall, meeting room, courtyard and rediscovered underground bunker form Studio Portaluppi, opening the building to a new public and cultural life. Its programme will begin in autumn 2026 under the title C’est l’heure de bien faire — “It’s time to do things well” — with exhibitions, workshops, talks, concerts and site-specific interventions bringing architecture into dialogue with materials, craftsmanship and research. Rather than becoming a monument dedicated to its architect, the studio returns as an active cultural infrastructure. Memory, after all, can also be preserved through continued use. Perhaps the most contemporary way of inhabiting Portaluppi’s space is precisely to avoid treating it as a relic.

Over the past decade or so, I’ve seen dozens of headlines for “fully 3D printed cameras,” and the one question that always got me to click on them was “how did someone 3D print a shutter?” What I found was that nearly all of these, as beautiful as they were, were either pinhole cameras or were adapting old medium or large format lenses with integrated shutters. So, with next to no design or engineering experience to my name, I thought I’d take a crack at it. While researching for this project, I had found only three cameras using homemade shutters: Léo Marius’ OpenReflex , Amos Dudley’s SLO , and the homemade cameras of Czechoslovakian photographer Miroslav Tichy . The first two designs were fascinating, but required finger pressure through the full range of the shutter’s motion, which seemed like a recipe for shutter shake, and Tichy’s were made from garbage, so there seemed to be plenty of room for improvement. After struggling to design for springs or rubber bands, what I wound up with is a magnetic two-way rotary blade shutter — a bit like older box cameras — with a speed of 1/100s. The action feels a lot like a light switch. As I knew a functional lens would be too difficult to make by hand, I limited myself to a 65mm single-element meniscus from an optical surplus reseller. **Jordan Rossi: “Joy Is Political in Its Own Way”**

News - September 22, 2026 by Guénola Pellen Jordan Rossi has always walked a fine line. British with Italian and Irish roots, he shoots the London queer scene and luxury brand campaigns with equal conviction, never appearing to betray either. His gaze turns fashion photography — vivid, often irreverent — into a tool for intimate and social portraiture. With a single obsession: catching the moment his subjects drop the mask. The title of your first exhibition, “Homecoming”, speaks of both return and belonging. What does that word mean to you? Jordan Rossi : “Homecoming” felt like the only possible title. I grew up between two cultures, Italian and Irish, but I was born and raised in London — so I’ve always had a slightly complicated relationship with the idea of “home”. I don’t think I’ve ever felt entirely one thing or another. Bringing this body of work to Italy feels like closing a circle. These photographs cover nearly a decade of my life, made in London, Los Angeles and elsewhere. Bringing them here, to a country that is part of my identity and my family, feels very personal. But with time, “home” has become less geographical for me. It’s the people you surround yourself with, the communities you find, and eventually the space you create for yourself. In that sense, “Homecoming” is as much about where I am now as it is about where I came from. Lukita Maxwell © Jordan Rossi, courtesy of the artist and Galleria Rubin You have shot the queer community of East London just as much as campaigns for Rolls-Royce. Are these two worlds not in conflict, and how do you move between them? Jordan Rossi : I’ve never really seen them as being in conflict. The scale, budgets and expectations might be completely different, but ultimately I’m still interested in the same things: people, character, and creating a world around them. I also don’t think queer artists should have to exist solely within queer spaces. I can photograph someone in a club in East London and then direct a campaign for a global brand — both can still contain my point of view. If anything, moving between those worlds matters to me. The experiences and communities that shaped me inevitably find their way into the commercial work, and the resources of the commercial world feed back into my personal work. I don’t want either side of my career to exist in isolation from the other. Your photography is generally described as queer, committed, rooted in fashion and portraiture. What would you add about your work and where it has taken you? Jordan Rossi : I’d probably add that there’s a sense of performance running through a lot of it. I’m interested in the version of ourselves that we construct for the camera: fashion, sexuality, masculinity, femininity, celebrity, glamour. Portraiture can document somebody, but it can also give them permission to become something else for a moment. Over the past decade that has taken me into very different rooms — from emerging performers and people within the queer community to actors, musicians, fashion houses and global brands. I’ve photographed people I admired long before I ever picked up a camera professionally, which still feels slightly surreal. But when I look across the work now, what interests me isn’t necessarily who is famous and who isn’t. It’s that the same curiosity runs through all of it. I’m drawn to people who have a strong sense of who they are — or who are in the process of discovering it. Rickey Thompson © Jordan Rossi, courtesy of the artist and Galleria Rubin Yasmin Finney © Jordan Rossi, courtesy of the artist and Galleria Rubin Lamar Johnson © Jordan Rossi, courtesy of the artist and Galleria Rubin Munroe Bergdorf © Jordan Rossi, courtesy of the artist and Galleria Rubin The Vivienne © Jordan Rossi, courtesy of the artist and Galleria Rubin Rickey Thompson © Jordan Rossi, courtesy of the artist and Galleria Rubin You are a photographer, a filmmaker and a creative director all at once. Concretely, how do you hold those three roles together? Jordan Rossi : What I’ve realised over time is that every time I’ve added another discipline, it has changed the way I approach everything else. There are instincts and skills that move between them. Photography made me much more aware of the relationship you build with the person in front of your camera. It can be incredibly intimate — just two people trying to find something together. Learning how to create that trust changed the way I worked with performers when I returned to directing. In the same way, years of filmmaking and creative direction have made me think differently about still images. I’m rarely just thinking about whether something looks beautiful; I’m thinking about the world around it, the character, the emotion, and what the image is trying to say. That’s ultimately how I hold all three together: they’ve become part of the same visual language. I’m constantly taking things I’ve learned in one area into another, often without consciously realizing it. I’m less interested now in whether something makes me a photographer, director or creative director — and more interested in finding the best way to tell the story in front of me. In your work, you have chosen to show the joy of the LGBTQIA+ community rather than its trauma. Do you feel a particular responsibility towards that community — and if so, what does it look like? Jordan Rossi : Absolutely — although I try not to let that responsibility turn into the idea that there is one correct way to represent a community as enormous and varied as ours. For a long time, so many LGBTQIA+ stories were framed through tragedy, rejection or suffering. Those stories are real and important, but they aren’t the entirety of queer life. I became much more interested in photographing the other side of it: friendship, sex, humor, beauty, ridiculousness, confidence, intimacy and joy. Not as a denial of the difficult things, but because those things are also political in their own way. There is power in seeing queer people simply having a brilliant time, taking up space, being desired rather than apologising for being there. My responsibility is really to the people in front of my camera. I want them to feel they have agency in how they’re represented, and I want the work to come from being part of a community rather than looking at it from the outside. I don’t think my job is to speak for LGBTQIA+ people. It’s to contribute my perspective to a much bigger, messier and more interesting story.
