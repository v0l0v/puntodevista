Our Learning with Lomography series explores student experiences with the Lomographic way of life all over the world, and teaches us a lesson or two on how to stay creative, curious and, of course, analogue. Dr. Huw Halstead of the University of Edinburgh and Professor Carl Smith of the University of Arkansas recently brought Lomography to Leith, a district of Edinburgh which has seen significant change over the past decades. Swimming with the steady current of student life and reckoning with its industrial legacy, the area has been undergoing a tide of transformation and gentrification. The academic project, Lomography in Leith, invited ten student participants to explore this evolving city space through the lens of Lomography’s 10 Golden Rules, and reflect on their experience of place through photography, gestural drawing, and autoethnographic writing. Bringing together the past and place in a series of visual explorations, including film photography, Lomography in Leith aimed to explore everyday meanings. > My background is in the history of everyday life and the study of collective memory. One of the things I’m really interested in is how historical landscapes become meaningful to people in their everyday lives. In addition to traditional archival sources, I use a method that I call “ethnokafenology”, a lovely portmanteau of “ethnography” and “kafeníon” (café in Greek) coined by the archaeologist Bill Alexander to describe chatting with the elderly patrons of cafés as a way of learning something about past and present society. > Extending this concept to other everyday settings, I’ve variously conducted “interviews”, for instance, outside a nightclub, in a barbershop, and trailing a shepherd with his flock around the outskirts of a village. What I really like about these more informal encounters is that they often throw up memories and perspectives that wouldn’t necessarily come up in more formal one-to-one oral history interviews. **Peishen Liang**

Misread (2024–2025) Misread examines the distance between being seen and being understood through a series of constructed underwater portraits. A woman occupies a transparent tank in which water makes her intensely visible while refraction alters her face, gestures and perceived identity. The same element that creates luminosity and weightlessness also restricts movement, breath and voice. The project begins with the Chinese stereotype of the “flower vase”, in which a beautiful woman is reduced to an ornament, and connects it to wider forms of beauty bias and sexualised visibility. Here, the tank becomes an enlarged vessel for display: she is not the vase, but the container reveals the system of looking that treats her as one. Water functions as both material and metaphor, showing how a person can be carefully observed yet still be fundamentally misread. Peishen Liang is a London-based photographer and lens-based artist who has worked across photography and visual art since 2018. His practice spans documentary, portraiture and conceptual image-making. **Mark Edward Harris**

The Way of the Japanese Bath While all the primeval geological and geothermal rumblings under Japan have set off devastating volcanic eruptions, tsunamis, and earthquakes (ten percent of the world’s volcanic activity occurs in Japan), they have also provided the 125 million inhabitants of the island nation on the Ring of Fire with a very unique way of life. An estimated 100 million people check in each year at onsen (hot spring) ryokans (Japanese style inns) or hotels, often to escape the extreme stress-inducing population density. The 20,000 thermal hot springs that flow from the ground have become meccas, a place to bathe not only the body but the soul as well. In fact, bathing for cleaning is not practiced in Japan. Soap is never brought into a bath either at a communal bath or at home. Washing is done before going into the bath for a soak and then often followed by a thorough rinsing and shampooing after the bath. Physical maladies are often treated at baths with specific mineral properties in the water. The Ainu, the indigenous people of the Japanese islands, used hot springs for healing purposes. An old Kusatsu (a town on the main island of Honshu) folksong declares, “A hot spring bath can cure anything but love.” Scientific research continues to be conducted on the medicinal benefits of the hot spring water. For instance, radioactive water is reported to help those suffering from gout, diabetes, chronic digestive problems, gallstones, and fatigue. Sulfur content in the water is considered a remedy for metallic poisoning and is said to clear up a bad complexion. Noboribetsu in Hokkaido is one of the most popular onsen towns because of its variety of waters with medicinal benefits yet most water is classified as simple thermal and most onsen visitors come for simple relaxation. People often shed their city clothes for the duration of their visits and stroll the streets of these onsen towns dressed in yukata – bath (yu) garment (kata). Humans are not the only creatures to take advantage of the onsens. Snow monkeys (Japanese macaques) are regular patrons at Jigokudani in Joshinetsu Plateau National Park in northeastern Nagano Prefecture. Back at home in even the smallest apartments, a deep bathtub can be found for this same meditative soaking. After sitting on a small stool and rinsing off, you immerse yourself in the tub. After your bath, a plastic roll up top is placed back on the top of the tub to keep the heat in for the next member of the household. Only after every one has taken a bath – including overnight house guests – will the water be drained. My first Japanese hot spring experience in Beppu, a town often shrouded in water vapor on the southern island of Kyushu, converted me into a furo-aholic (bath-aholic) in the early 1990s. Three decades and four editions of my book “The Way of the Japanese Bath” later I still find the magical waters an endless source of both visual and visceral pleasure. **Tristan Olphe**

Public Interiors I arrived in Vietnam without a photographic project. I was simply there on holiday. Then these nocturnal façades imposed themselves, naturally, without my looking for them. For three weeks I walked through Hanoi, Huế, Hội An, Tuy Hòa and Hồ Chí Minh City after dark, a Ricoh GR3x in my pocket, no plan. What drew me back night after night were small scenes around the shops: a vegetable seller doing her hair inside her stall, men seated at a table on the sidewalk, a child playing with a stool while his mother arranges bouquets in her flower shop, someone doing the dishes outside, neighbours sharing a meal, a shopkeeper trimming her nails at her counter. Daytime commerce and private life share the same space. Night doesn’t close these spaces, it reveals them differently, with a kind of serenity, almost a form of poetry: people living where inside and outside blend, a public interior, with quiet, matter-of-fact ease. I photographed head on, keeping the frame flat and frontal, so each façade reads like a small stage, although nothing was staged. No flash, no direction, no return visits to the same spot. Just walking, and stopping when something held. These are places where commerce, intimacy and the public realm coexist without separation, visible from the street to anyone willing to slow down and look. Three weeks is a beginning, not an answer. For now, what these images hold is closer to a question than a conclusion: what happens, at night, in these threshold spaces. **Morgan Roué**

On the banks of the Styx Since time immemorial, death has been part of human daily life. Long managed within the family home, it has gradually shifted to healthcare facilities with the rise of modern medicine. In the space of a few generations, rituals that were once passed down naturally have become little known, or even invisible. Yet men and women continue to care for the deceased on a daily basis. These carers, working behind the scenes, ensure a continuum of care through essential tasks: mortuary washing, the removal of medical devices, preservation, dressing, applying make-up and placing the body in the coffin, before it is presented to the bereaved families. Often overlooked, the work of mortuary staff remains discreet despite its importance. Whilst our society now addresses subjects that were once taboo more openly, the world of death paradoxically remains largely out of sight. Through this photographic series, I sought to shed light on this little-known aspect of care in France. This analogue photographic series was produced whilst working alongside staff in the mortuary at the Bichat-Claude Bernard AP-HP Hospital in Paris. Some of the photographs may be distressing to some viewers. The full photographic series is available here: https://morgan-roué.com/styx

**Thierry Bazin**

Mea Silva Mea Silva explores the forest as a space for experience, reflection and encounter with living beings. The installation combines photography, video and sound design to create a journey in which the gaze moves forward, hesitates, delves deeper and loses its way – just like a walker who leaves the marked path to enter the depths of the world. The forest as both a trial and a revelation, as a place of emancipation, as a clearing where being is unveiled, as an interconnected ecosystem where humans and non-humans respond to one another. Here, the forest is neither a backdrop nor a metaphor: it is a subject, a presence, living matter. The photographic series play on the gradual penetration of the gaze: a single scene is layered, densified and made more complex, as if each step added a layer of fusion with reality. Elsewhere, the image becomes a sensory inventory: isolated objects, fragments of life, stones, humble elements brought to light and torn from their anonymity. They become the silent witnesses to a memory of the living. The motif of the holobiont body extends this reflection. The human being no longer appears as a separate individual but as a network of biological alliances, a mobile ecosystem that exists solely through its connections. In the same way, the forest is conceived as a complex organism, criss-crossed by flows, circulations, and visible and invisible relationships. In Mea Silva, body and forest engage in dialogue, respond to one another, and at times merge. The videos and soundscapes—field recordings, voices, electronic textures—extend this sensory immersion. They do not describe the forest: they offer an experience of it, a breath, a temporality of its own. Mea Silva is thus a journey, an attempt to approach the impenetrable part of the world. An inner and outer walk where, step by step, one experiences the vitality, complexity and fragility of living things.

**Sandrine Amadoux**

Seuil SEUIL is a black-and-white series spanning 13 years. For this project, I have removed the locations, dates and captions in order to keep the images timeless and detached from their immediate context, without evoking any specific reference or personal memory, and to leave each stage in a deliberately suspended state of time. The series unfolds like a silent film, in two parts: what things convey, and then what people feel. **Just a sec**

I often see older men (and they are always men – “camera ojisan” in Japan) sporting the latest digital equipment, following in the steps of Shinzo Maeda , the camera standing on a tripod, photographing a sunlit scene that has been photographed 1000 times before possibly to “prove” they are just as good as that pioneering photographer. Maybe they are pixel shifting to get an image that needs printing 2m by 3m to appreciate all the detail – but I think not. The ‘rule’ for hand held photography is photograph at a speed of 1/focallength or faster. What does this mean in reality? Different people are different – since childhood I have had a tremor in my right hand which can present difficulties. Some people cannot reliably get sharp images following the rule, some people can do a stop or more better. But in all cases sharpness is not guaranteed, there is a higher probability of getting a sharp shot at faster speeds, and as the shutter speed drops the percentage of fails increases. Modern digital cameras offer image stabilisation with 4.5 stops (Sony A7Rii), 5.5 stops (A7Riii) or 8 stops (A7Rv – I want to get my hands on one of those!) improvement over the hand holding rule quoted above. The rule then becomes 2^S / focallength (see the note below) where S = 4.5 or 5.5 in the case of the first two cameras mentioned. Image blur in a photograph which contains a sharp element – for example a long exposure photograph of a waterfall – is something that I always found attractive. Not being keen on lugging a tripod around I was keen to put the rule to a test. The featured image taken in a museum was using a 55mm lens at f8 and iso100 on an A7Riii with a shutter speed of 1.6 sec. The rule suggests 0.8 sec. Out of six images three were sharp. The following images were taken on an A7Rii with a 20mm lens at iso100 in an underground pedestrian street in Sapporo. The rule suggest 1 sec. 2 sec exposure at f22. I was leaning against a pillar. The gentleman (and wife and child) were passing my left shoulder as the exposure terminated. Crop of the above. Focus was on the sleeping lady’s left sleeve. Diffraction at f22 softens the image. 1 sec at f11. There is slight blurring of the text on the sign. There are at least 15 people rendered as a barely visible blur (for example centre bottom) while others have disappeared entirely. The girl (right) was walking directly away from the camera. Don’t be afraid of hand holding a digital camera at what might seem like stupidly slow shutter speeds. A little practice is required – the first time you take a one sec exposure the blank screen on pressing the shutter comes as a surprise causing a physical reaction. You need to try this a few times to get used to it. Also if possible make use of some support, either something to lean against or rest on. But once mastered – and don’t expect 100% success – it opens up a new world of possibilities. NOTE: 2^S means 2 to the power of S. For example if S=3, 2^S = 2x2x2. If you don’t have a scientific calculator and need 4.5 stops calculate 4 and 5 stops (16 and 32) and add one half of the difference – giving 24 (the technically correct answer is 22.6). Shooting a 50mm lens this give 0.48 seconds (i.e. half a second) vs the technically correct 0.45 seconds (also half a second in practice). Share this post:

### C41 Magazine

**Lost in Tokyo: Woven into the City**

For decades, Tokyo has been portrayed as the quintessential symbol of modern-day speed. Yet what happens when the world’s fastest-paced metropolis becomes a setting for waiting? ‘Lost in Tokyo’ turns this perspective on its head, replacing movement with stillness and transforming the urban space into an emotional landscape. In this vision, the city is not a spectacular backdrop, but a discreet presence that invites us to notice what usually escapes our attention: moments of pause, detours, those instants when the body slows down whilst the urban fabric carries on with its course. Shot predominantly on film and using only natural light, Elie Bianco’s photo series creates an atmosphere that belongs more to memory than to the present. The grain of the images softens the frenetic pace and presents a Tokyo far removed from the hyper-technological aesthetic often associated with its Western image. The reference is not to the neon lights of Blade Runner, but rather to the suspended melancholy of Sofia Coppola’s Lost in Translation or the contemplative pace of Wim Wenders’ Perfect Days, where everyday life takes on an almost ritualistic quality. In this context, fashion engages in a dialogue with architecture rather than overshadowing it. Openwork knits, crochet, floral embroidery and open weaves create a language defined as much by absences as by presences. It is a slow, artisanal craft that seems to contrast with the geometric rigidity of concrete, tiles and infrastructure. However, the contrast does not create tension: the garments ultimately absorb the city, reflecting its hidden rhythm. The floral motifs echo the street lamps dotting the alleyways, the open weaves harmonise with the electric cables criss-crossing the sky, and the form-fitting silhouettes express a vulnerability that the environment amplifies. The protagonist, Enya, does not wander through Tokyo like a modern flâneuse, but experiences it with an almost alienating calm. Standing at the corner of a traditional, lantern-lit street, motionless in the middle of the road or suspended on a footbridge, she seems to defy the logic of having a destination. Her gaze is not searching for anything: she observes, waits, and allows the landscape to wash over her. Consequently, the city ceases to be a mere backdrop and becomes a measure of inner distance. It is precisely this choice that makes ‘Lost in Tokyo’ more profound than a simple tale of ‘lost’ youth. Being lost is not a dramatic condition, but an act of silent resistance. In an age that measures time in terms of productivity and speed, allowing oneself a pause becomes a meaningful gesture. Elie Bianco’s images suggest that, perhaps, the only way to rediscover a place is to stop, at least for a moment, looking for it.
